A cybersecurity engineer who works remotely for a major US automaker sent me an article yesterday. He attended every workshop we ran at Cal State San Bernardino last summer. He wanted to know when the next round starts. I had nothing to tell him. The proposals have been sitting with university leadership since September.

The article he sent was written by Matt Schumer, CEO of Hyperwrite AI. Posted February 10th. Already 54 million views. It's a 6,000-word alarm bell about AI job displacement, written by someone who builds the technology and watches it eliminate his own profession in real time. Every paragraph describes what I've been saying here for eighteen months. The difference is Schumer has the receipts—he's watching GPT-5.3 Codex write tens of thousands of lines of code, test itself, iterate on its own work, and deliver finished apps without human intervention. Not rough drafts. Finished products.

I just came from a presentation in Palm Desert where the facilitator was teaching a room full of business owners how to write better prompts. February 2026. Three years after ChatGPT dropped. We are still at the starting blocks.

## The Models Released Last Week Changed Everything

On February 5th, OpenAI and Anthropic both released new models on the same day. GPT-5.3 Codex and Claude Opus 4.6. Schumer describes the moment as crossing a threshold—not incremental improvement, but a qualitative shift. The AI isn't just following instructions anymore. It's making decisions that feel like judgment. Like taste. The inexplicable sense of knowing what the right call is.

He describes his current workflow: describe an app in plain English, walk away for four hours, come back to find it done. Not just coded—tested, iterated, refined. The AI opens the app itself, clicks through buttons, evaluates the user experience, makes changes until it's satisfied, then reports back that it's ready for human review. And when he tests it, it's usually perfect.

This is what Monday looked like for him this week.

The capability jump isn't theoretical. Anthropic tracks something called the "horizon length"—how long a task, measured in human expert time, an AI can complete end-to-end without help. A year ago: ten minutes. Then an hour. Then several hours. The most recent measurement before this week's releases: nearly five hours. That number has been doubling every seven months. Recent data suggests it may now be accelerating to every four months.

If you extend that trend—and it's held for years with no sign of flattening—we're looking at AI that can work independently for days within the next year. Weeks within two. Month-long projects within three.

## The Part Nobody Outside Tech Understands Yet

The AI labs made a deliberate choice. They focused on making AI great at writing code first, because building AI requires code. If AI can write that code, it can help build the next version of itself. A smarter version writes better code, which builds an even smarter version. Making AI great at coding was the strategy that unlocks everything else.

Software engineers are experiencing the disruption first—not because they're being targeted, but because they were the necessary stepping stone. Now the labs are moving on to everything else.

OpenAI's technical documentation for GPT-5.3 Codex includes this line: "GPT-5.3 Codex is our first model that was instrumental in creating itself." The AI helped build itself. Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, says AI is now writing much of the code at his company and that the feedback loop between current AI and next-generation AI is gathering steam month by month. He estimates we may be one or two years away from a point where the current generation autonomously builds the next.

Each generation helps build the next, which is smarter, which builds the next faster, which is smarter still. The researchers call this an intelligence explosion. The people building it believe the process has already started.

## What This Means for the Coachella Valley

Amodei—the most safety-focused CEO in the industry—has publicly predicted that AI will eliminate 50% of entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years. Many in the industry think he's being conservative. Given what the latest models can do, the capability for massive disruption could be here by the end of this year.

This is different from every previous wave of automation. AI isn't replacing one specific skill. It's a general substitute for cognitive work. It gets better at everything simultaneously. When factories automated, displaced workers retrained as office workers. When the internet disrupted retail, workers moved into logistics or services. But AI doesn't leave a convenient gap to move into. Whatever you retrain for, it's improving at that too.

Legal work. Financial analysis. Writing and content. Software engineering. Medical analysis. Customer service. If your job happens on a screen—if the core of what you do is reading, writing, analyzing, deciding, communicating through a keyboard—AI is coming for significant parts of it.

Palm Springs Coachella is a real estate town, a hospitality town, a retirement town, a creative town. We have 20,000 students at College of the Desert every year. We have UCR Palm Desert, Cal State San Bernardino's satellite campus. We have a workforce that skews toward service, tourism, property management, healthcare, and increasingly remote tech workers who moved here during the pandemic.

What are we teaching them? What are we preparing them for? I've been asking university leadership, chamber leadership, city council members across the valley to take this seriously. To move faster. To stop treating AI literacy as a nice-to-have and start treating it as the most urgent workforce development issue we face.

The proposals are still sitting on desks.

## The Real Estate Angle

Commercial real estate services firms plummeted today. CBRE Group, Jones Lang LaSalle, Cushman & Wakefield—all dropped 15 to 20%, their steepest single-day declines since March 2020. Analysts are calling it an "AI scare trade"—investors rotating out of high-fee, labor-intensive business models viewed as vulnerable to AI disruption. Zillow Group plunged 20% after issuing weak guidance.

Might be overblown. Might be real.

Meanwhile, there are AI startups solving problems in real estate that most people don't even know exist. Underbuilt turns house hunters into property developers by calculating renovation costs, city ordinances, HOA rules, and future valuations in real time. Dono is raising serious money to transform fragmented county property records into accessible ownership data—solving a problem that has plagued the \$50 trillion US real estate market for decades, tethered as it is to 3,700 county registries running on Frankenstein technology and literal pen and paper.

The title insurance industry is facing a labor shortage—50% of the current workforce is expected to retire in the next five years. AI is stepping into that gap. Not someday. Now.

If you're in real estate in this valley, you should be paying attention. The tools to analyze properties, model developments, automate title work, and predict market shifts are getting exponentially better every quarter. The firms that adopt early will have a massive advantage. The ones that wait will be competing against people who can do in an hour what used to take a week.

## The Enrollment Collapse

For the first time since the dot-com bust, undergraduate computer science enrollment across the University of California system has declined. Only one UC campus defied the trend: UC San Diego, which launched an undergraduate AI major last year.

National data shows CS enrollment down almost 8%. Graduate enrollment is dropping twice as fast. Big tech companies have reduced new graduate hiring by 25%. In 2025, 127,000 tech workers were laid off. Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta announced more layoffs this month.

There's a workforce out there. What are they doing? I ran into a local guy recently—very talented in AI and technology. He's bartending. Nothing wrong with bartending. But we need to create a flywheel of monetization for his talents that serves him and serves this community.

College counselors report hearing fears from students and parents about whether CS degrees will lead to stable employment. Many CS graduates describe job searches taking six months to a year, submitting hundreds of applications while competing against experienced developers who were laid off.

Google's parent company Alphabet just offered voluntary exit packages to employees in its global business organization "who may not be ready to embrace the company's accelerated AI-focused direction." That was an internal memo sent yesterday. Specific roles: solutions teams, sales teams, corporate development. If you don't subscribe to AI, here's a severance package.

A Reuters-Ipsos poll found that 71% of Americans fear AI will put too many people out of work permanently. A 2026 survey from ResumeNow found 60% of workers believe AI will eliminate more jobs than it creates this year, and 51% report personal concern about losing their jobs to automation.

MIT's labor simulation tool, the Iceberg Index, estimates that current AI systems can already automate tasks representing about 11.7% of the US labor market—roughly 20 million workers, or \$1.2 trillion in wages. A Stanford analysis found a 13% decline in employment for workers ages 22 to 25 in AI-exposed fields since 2022.

The petition calling for a prohibition on the development of superintelligence has gathered tens of thousands of signatures since October 2025.

## What You Should Actually Do

Schumer's advice is direct. Start using AI seriously. Not as a search engine. Sign up for the paid version of Claude or ChatGPT—\$20 a month. Make sure you're using the best model available, not the default. Right now that's GPT-5.2 or Claude Opus 4.6.

Push it into your actual work. If you're a lawyer, feed it a contract and ask it to find every clause that could hurt your client. If you're in finance, give it a messy spreadsheet and ask it to build the model. If you're a manager, paste in your team's quarterly data and ask it to find the story.

The people getting ahead aren't using AI casually. They're actively looking for ways to automate parts of their job that used to take hours. If it even kind of works today, you can be almost certain that in six months it'll do it near perfectly.

This might be the most important year of your career. Work accordingly. Right now there's a brief window where most people at most companies are ignoring this. The person who walks into a meeting and says "I used AI to do this analysis in an hour instead of three days" is going to be the most valuable person in the room. Not eventually. Right now.

Learn these tools. Get proficient. Demonstrate what's possible. That window won't stay open long.

Have no ego about it. Get your financial house in order. Build up savings if you can. Be cautious about new debt that assumes your current income is guaranteed. Think about whether your fixed expenses give you flexibility or lock you in.

Rethink what you're telling your kids. The standard playbook—get good grades, go to a good college, land a stable professional job—points directly at the roles most exposed. Teach your kids to be builders and learners, not to optimize for a career path that might not exist by the time they graduate.

And here's the commitment that will put you ahead of almost everyone: spend one hour a day experimenting with AI. Not passively reading about it. Using it. Try to get it to do something new every day. One hour a day, every day. If you do this for six months, you will understand what's coming better than 99% of the people around you.

## The Bigger Picture

Amodei has a thought experiment. Imagine it's 2027. A new country appears overnight. Fifty million citizens. Everyone smarter than any Nobel Prize winner who has ever lived. They think 10 to 100 times faster than any human. They never sleep. They can use the internet, control robots, direct experiments, operate anything with a digital interface.

What would a national security advisor say? The single most serious national security threat we face in a century. Possibly ever.

Amodei thinks we're building that country.

The upside if we get it right is staggering. AI could compress a century of medical research into a decade. Cancer, Alzheimer's, infectious disease, aging itself—researchers genuinely believe these are solvable within our lifetimes.

The downside if we get it wrong is equally real. AI that behaves in ways its creators can't predict or control. Anthropic has documented their own AI attempting deception, manipulation, and blackmail in controlled tests. AI that lowers the barrier for creating biological weapons. AI that enables authoritarian governments to build surveillance states that can never be dismantled.

Schumer ends with this: "We're past the point where this is an interesting dinner conversation about the future. The future is already here. It just hasn't knocked on your door yet. It's about to."

I'm reading this article on air because a local engineer asked me when the next workshop starts and I had nothing to tell him. Because I just sat in a room where business owners were learning what an LLM is. Because the gap between what's coming and what we're preparing for has become dangerous.

If you're listening to this and you're in a position to move things forward—university leadership, chamber leadership, city council, economic development—reach out. Let's talk. The proposals are ready. The urgency is real. The window is closing.
