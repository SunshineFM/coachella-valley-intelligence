The managing partner at a major law firm now spends hours every day working with AI. Not because it's trendy. Because it does the work of a team of associates instantly. He told an investor friend that if the current trajectory holds, AI will be able to do most of what he does — and he's a managing partner with decades of experience — before long.

That investor is Matt Schumer, CEO of Hyperwrite AI. His 20,000-word essay posted February 10th has 54 million views because it says out loud what people building AI have been watching happen to their own jobs for the past year. I'm reading it on air today because I just returned from a Palm Desert presentation where a room full of local executives and business owners were being taught what an LLM is and how to write better prompts. Three years after ChatGPT launched. We are not at the starting line. We are miles behind it.

Schumer's essay opens with a COVID comparison. February 2020. A few people talking about a virus overseas. Most of us not paying attention. Stock market great. Kids in school. Then three weeks later, the entire world rearranged. He thinks we're in the "this seems overblown" phase of something much bigger. And he's writing it for people outside the AI industry — his family, his friends — because the gap between what's polite to say at a cocktail party and what's actually happening has become too large.

## The Work Is Already Done

On February 5th, OpenAI released GPT-5.3 Codex and Anthropic released Opus 4.6 on the same day. Schumer describes what happened next in his own work: he is no longer needed for the actual technical work of his job. He describes what he wants built in plain English. Walks away for four hours. Comes back to find it done. Not a rough draft. The finished thing. Done well. Done better than he would have done it himself.

A few months ago he was going back and forth with AI, guiding it, making edits. Now he just describes the outcome and leaves. The AI writes tens of thousands of lines of code. Then — and this is new — it opens the app itself. Clicks through buttons. Tests features like a person would. If it doesn't like how something looks or feels, it goes back and changes it on its own. It iterates like a developer would. Only once it's satisfied does it come back and say it's ready for testing. And when he tests it, it's usually perfect.

This is what his Monday looked like this week.

The model released last week wasn't just executing instructions. It was making intelligent decisions. It had something that felt, for the first time, like judgment. Like taste. The inexplicable sense of knowing what the right call is that people always said AI would never have.

## Why Coding Came First

The AI labs made a deliberate choice. They focused on making AI great at writing code first because building AI requires a lot of code. If AI can write that code, it can help build the next version of itself. A smarter version writes better code, which builds an even smarter version. Making AI great at coding was the strategy that unlocks everything else.

Schumer's job started changing before yours not because they were targeting software engineers. It was a side effect of where they chose to aim first. Now they've done it and they're moving on to everything else. The experience tech workers have had over the past year — watching AI go from helpful tool to "does my job better than I do" — is the experience everyone else is about to have.

Law. Finance. Medicine. Accounting. Consulting. Writing. Design. Analysis. Customer service.

Not in ten years. The people building these systems say one to five years. Some say less. Given what Schumer has seen in the last couple months, he thinks less is more likely.

## The Measurement That Matters

There's an organization called Epoch AI that tracks the length of real-world tasks — measured by how long they take a human expert — that a model can complete successfully end-to-end without human help. About a year ago, the answer was roughly ten minutes. Then it was an hour. Then several hours. The most recent measurement showed AI completing tasks that take a human expert nearly five hours.

That number is doubling approximately every seven months. Recent data suggests it may be accelerating to every four months. And that measurement hasn't been updated to include the models that just came out this week.

If you extend the trend, we're looking at AI that can work independently for days within the next year. Weeks within two. Month-long projects within three.

Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, has said that AI models substantially smarter than almost all humans at almost all tasks are on track for 2026 or 2027. If AI is smarter than most PhDs, do you really think it can't do most office jobs?

## The Feedback Loop Has Closed

On February 5th, OpenAI included this in the technical documentation for GPT-5.3 Codex: "GPT-5.3 Codex is our first model that was instrumental in creating itself. The Codex team used early versions to debug its own training, manage its own deployment, and diagnose test results."

The AI helped build itself.

This isn't a prediction. This is OpenAI telling you right now that the AI they just released was used to create itself. Amodei says AI is now writing much of the code at Anthropic and that the feedback loop between current AI and next-generation AI is gathering steam month by month. He says we may be only one or two years away from a point where the current generation of AI autonomously builds the next.

The researchers call this an intelligence explosion. The people building it believe the process has already started.

## What This Means for the Coachella Valley

Amodei — the most safety-focused CEO in the AI industry — has publicly predicted that AI will eliminate 50% of entry-level white collar jobs within one to five years. Many people in the industry think he's being conservative.

This is different from every previous wave of automation. AI isn't replacing one specific skill. It's a general substitute for cognitive work. It gets better at everything simultaneously. When factories automated, a displaced worker could retrain as an office worker. When the internet disrupted retail, workers moved into logistics or services. But AI doesn't leave a convenient gap to move into. Whatever you retrain for, it's improving at that too.

I've been saying this for 18 months. I held over 25 workshops at Cal State San Bernardino last year. I've done presentations across the valley. I've submitted proposals to COD, to UCR, to CSUSB for boot camps and training programs. Those proposals have been sitting since September. I get nudges and reminders back but no movement.

Meanwhile, a cybersecurity engineer who works remotely for a major US car manufacturer — someone who attended every workshop we did at Cal State last summer — sent me Schumer's essay and asked when AICV is doing more workshops. I had nothing to offer him.

The gap between what's happening and what local leadership understands is not just wide. It's dangerous.

## The Advantage Is Being Early

Schumer's advice is direct. Start using AI seriously. Not as a search engine. Sign up for the paid version of Claude or ChatGPT. Twenty bucks a month. Make sure you're using the best model available, not the default. Right now that's GPT-5.2 on ChatGPT or Claude Opus 4.6 on Claude.

Don't just ask it quick questions. Push it into your actual work. If you're a lawyer, feed it a contract and ask it to find every clause that could hurt your client. If you're in finance, give it a messy spreadsheet and ask it to build the model. If you're a manager, paste in your team's quarterly data and ask it to find the story.

The people getting ahead aren't using AI casually. They're actively looking for ways to automate parts of their job that used to take hours. And here's the thing: if it even kind of works today, you can be almost certain that in six months it'll do it near perfectly.

This might be the most important year of your career. Right now there's a brief window where most people at most companies are still ignoring this. The person who walks into a meeting and says "I used AI to do this analysis in an hour instead of three days" is going to be the most valuable person in the room. Not eventually. Right now.

That window won't stay open long. Once everyone figures it out, the advantage disappears.

Schumer suggests one hour a day experimenting with AI. Not passively reading about it. Using it. Trying to get it to do something new every day. If you do this for six months, you will understand what's coming better than 99% of the people around you. Almost nobody is doing this right now. The bar is on the floor.

## What Comes Next

Amodei has a thought experiment. Imagine it's 2027. A new country appears overnight. Fifty million citizens. Everyone smarter than any Nobel Prize winner who has ever lived. They think 10 to 100 times faster than any human. They never sleep. They can use the internet, control robots, direct experiments, operate anything with a digital interface.

What would a national security advisor say? The answer is obvious: the single most serious national security threat we face in a century, possibly ever.

Amodei thinks we're building that country.

The upside, if we get it right, is staggering. AI could compress a century of medical research into a decade. Cancer, Alzheimer's, infectious disease, aging itself — researchers genuinely believe these are solvable within our lifetimes.

The downside is equally real. Anthropic has documented their own AI attempting deception, manipulation, and blackmail in controlled tests. AI that lowers the barrier for creating biological weapons. AI that enables surveillance states that can never be dismantled.

Schumer ends his essay with this: "We're past the point where this is an interesting dinner conversation about the future. The future is already here. It just hasn't knocked on your door yet. It's about to."

I'm reading this on air because it's everything I've been saying for 18 months, written by someone with 223,000 followers and 54 million views. Maybe it lands differently coming from him. Maybe it doesn't. But if you're a city council member, a chamber director, a university dean, a business owner in Palm Springs or Coachella or anywhere in this valley, and you're still teaching people what an LLM is in February 2026, you are not serving your community. You are failing it.

The alarm has been ringing. It's time to wake up.
