DeepSeek R1 dropped yesterday and Silicon Valley is having an identity crisis. A Chinese AI lab just proved you can train a frontier reasoning model for \$5.6 million instead of the hundreds of millions OpenAI and Anthropic have been spending. They used cheaper H800 chips — the nerfed export versions China gets under US sanctions — and still matched OpenAI's O1 on major benchmarks.

The stock market responded immediately. NVIDIA dropped 17% in a single day, wiping out \$600 billion in market cap. The entire thesis that AI requires infinite compute and infinite capital just got stress-tested. If you can build competitive models for 1% of the cost, the moat everyone assumed existed might not.

For anyone building in Palm Springs Coachella, this matters more than it looks. The AI infrastructure story has been: you need to be in San Francisco, you need massive funding, you need access to the latest chips. DeepSeek just said no to all three. They're in Hangzhou. They used sanctioned hardware. They spent what a decent Series A raises. And they shipped something that works.

The model itself is open source under MIT license. You can download it, run it locally, modify it. No API fees. No rate limits. No terms of service that change when you get successful. This is the kind of infrastructure shift that lets smaller markets build without asking permission.

## Trump's Second Term Starts With Pardons and Crypto

Donald Trump was inaugurated for his second term yesterday. Within hours, he issued blanket pardons for everyone involved in January 6th — roughly 1,500 people, including those convicted of assaulting police officers. Sentences commuted, records cleared, done.

He also pardoned Ross Ulbricht, the Silk Road founder who'd been serving life without parole. That one wasn't a surprise — Trump had been signaling it for months, and the crypto community had been lobbying hard. What's notable is the speed. This wasn't a second-term legacy move. This was day one.

The crypto pardons matter because they signal where Trump's economic policy is heading. His team has been talking about a strategic Bitcoin reserve. They want the US to hold Bitcoin the way it holds gold. Whether that's brilliant or insane depends on who you ask, but it's a real policy position with real momentum.

Palm Springs has quietly become a crypto-curious market. We've got remote founders, we've got wealth looking for alternatives to traditional real estate, we've got a local government that's been more open to experimentation than most California cities. If federal policy starts treating crypto as legitimate infrastructure rather than a regulatory problem, that changes what's possible here.

## The Actual State of AI: Benchmarks vs. Reality

Back to DeepSeek. The benchmarks are impressive, but benchmarks lie all the time. The model scores 79.8% on AIME 2024 math problems, compared to OpenAI O1's 79.2%. It hits 97.3% on MATH-500. On MMLU — the general knowledge test everyone uses — it's at 90.8%.

But here's what the benchmarks don't tell you: the model is heavily censored. Ask it anything about Tiananmen Square, Taiwan, Xinjiang, or Chinese political history, and it shuts down. It's trained to refuse entire categories of questions. For research use, for creative work, for anything that touches geopolitics, that's a real limitation.

The censorship isn't subtle. Independent testing shows it won't even acknowledge basic historical facts if they're politically sensitive in China. Which means this isn't really a general-purpose model. It's a very good model with hard-coded blind spots.

Still, the cost efficiency is real. If you're building something that doesn't need to discuss Chinese politics — and most applications don't — you just got access to frontier reasoning capabilities for free. That's a different world than the one we were in last week.

## What This Means for Local Builders

The DeepSeek story is really a story about leverage. For years, the AI narrative has been: scale is everything, capital is everything, access is everything. If you're not raising \$100 million and renting whole data centers, you're not serious.

That narrative just broke. A team in Hangzhou, using hardware that's two generations behind, spending 1% of what the big labs spend, shipped something competitive. They proved that intelligence in software is different from intelligence in the real world. You can't build a cheaper rocket by being clever. You can build a cheaper model.

For Palm Springs Coachella, this is the kind of infrastructure moment that matters. We're not going to out-raise San Francisco. We're not going to have better access to capital or talent or compute. But if the actual cost of building frontier AI just dropped by 99%, then the advantages of being in an expensive market just got smaller.

The open source piece is crucial. MIT license means you can fork it, fine-tune it, deploy it commercially, and never pay a cent. You can run it on local hardware if you've got the GPU capacity, or use one of the API providers that are already spinning up around it. The infrastructure is suddenly cheap enough that small teams can experiment.

## The Bigger Pattern

Both stories today — DeepSeek and Trump's return — are about the same thing: the assumptions of the last four years getting stress-tested all at once.

The AI assumption was that only massive capital could build frontier models. The political assumption was that Trump's first term was an aberration, not a realignment. Both assumptions are now visibly wrong, and the markets are repricing everything accordingly.

NVIDIA's \$600 billion single-day loss is the market saying: maybe compute isn't the moat we thought it was. Trump's day-one pardons are the political system saying: the rules we thought were settled aren't.

For people building in smaller markets like ours, this kind of volatility creates openings. When the dominant players are distracted by existential questions, when the infrastructure costs drop by two orders of magnitude, when the federal government is suddenly interested in legitimizing crypto — that's when you can move.

The question isn't whether Palm Springs Coachella can compete with San Francisco on their terms. It's whether we can build things that only make sense here, using infrastructure that's suddenly cheap enough to experiment with, in a regulatory environment that's suddenly more open to alternatives.

DeepSeek proved you don't need to be in Silicon Valley to build frontier AI. Trump's second term is going to test whether you need to follow San Francisco's political assumptions to build a tech company. Both answers look like "no" right now.

That's the opportunity.
