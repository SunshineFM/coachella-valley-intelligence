Trump isn't president yet, but his tariff threats are already restructuring the global economy. Walmart told suppliers last week to brace for price increases. Apple is moving production to India. Companies are frontrunning policy that doesn't technically exist yet, and that tells you everything about how seriously the market is taking this.

The numbers are specific and large. Trump has floated a 10% universal tariff on all imports, 60% on Chinese goods, and as high as 100% on countries that move away from the dollar. These aren't negotiating positions buried in policy papers — he's saying them out loud, repeatedly. The Peterson Institute ran the model: if all of this happens, the median American household loses \$2,600 in purchasing power annually. Not through direct taxes. Through higher prices on everything that crosses a border.

For Palm Springs Coachella, this matters in ways that aren't immediately obvious. We're not a manufacturing hub. But we are a hospitality and construction economy, and both of those sectors run on goods that move through global supply chains. Building materials, kitchen equipment, linens, electronics, vehicles — all of it gets more expensive if tariffs land as threatened. The desert's development boom, already dealing with higher interest rates, now faces input cost inflation that can't be financed away.

## Walmart's Warning and What It Means for Retail

Walmart doesn't typically lead with bad news. But their CFO told suppliers to prepare for price hikes if tariffs hit. This is the largest retailer in America signaling that it will pass costs through rather than absorb them. That's a policy choice, but it's also a realistic one — Walmart's margins are thin, and a 10% universal tariff doesn't leave room for corporate charity.

The mechanism is straightforward. Tariffs are paid by the importing company, not the exporting country. Walmart buys a container of goods from China, the tariff is assessed at the port, Walmart pays it, and then decides whether to eat the cost or raise prices. Given that Walmart's entire brand is low prices, raising them is a last resort. If they're preemptively telling suppliers this is coming, they've already done the math and decided there's no other path.

For local retailers in the valley, this creates a compression problem. If Walmart raises prices, smaller stores lose one of their few competitive advantages — being able to undercut the big box on certain items. If Walmart holds prices and absorbs losses, they can sustain that longer than independents can. Either way, the margin for error shrinks.

## Apple's India Move and the Decoupling Acceleration

Apple is shifting iPhone production to India, and they're not doing it for labor cost savings. This is a hedge against tariff risk and a broader move to diversify away from China. By 2026, Apple wants a significant percentage of its global production outside of Chinese factories. India, Vietnam, and other Southeast Asian countries are absorbing that capacity.

This is the physical manifestation of decoupling — the slow, expensive process of rebuilding supply chains that were optimized for cost over the past thirty years. It doesn't happen instantly. Factories take years to build and certify. Worker training, quality control, logistics networks — all of that has to be replicated. And it costs more. Apple's gross margins will compress, and that either comes out of shareholder returns or consumer prices.

The broader point is that even without tariffs being implemented, the threat alone is enough to move billions in capital investment. Companies are making irreversible decisions based on policy that might not happen. That's a tax in itself — the cost of uncertainty.

## The Dollar Weaponization Play

Trump's threat to impose 100% tariffs on countries that move away from the dollar is the wildcard in all of this. It's not about trade balance. It's about maintaining dollar dominance in global finance. The dollar's reserve currency status gives the U.S. enormous leverage — it lets us run deficits that other countries can't, and it makes our financial sanctions meaningful.

But threatening 100% tariffs to enforce that dominance is a blunt instrument. It assumes other countries won't call the bluff or find workarounds. China and Russia are already building alternative payment systems. The BRICS nations are discussing a common currency. None of that is imminent, but the more the U.S. uses the dollar as a weapon, the faster those alternatives develop.

For a place like Palm Springs, this feels distant until you consider that our economy is deeply tied to international visitors and investors. Canadians own property here. Europeans vacation here. Wealthy families from Asia and the Middle East buy second homes here. If global finance fragments and currency volatility spikes, that capital flow gets choppier.

## The Selective Tariff Strategy

Not all tariffs are created equal. The Trump administration is reportedly considering targeted tariffs on specific sectors — semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, critical minerals. This is industrial policy dressed up as trade enforcement. The goal is to reshore production of goods deemed strategically important, even if it costs more.

Semiconductors are the obvious example. The U.S. is heavily dependent on Taiwan for advanced chips, and Taiwan is one geopolitical crisis away from being inaccessible. Tariffs on foreign chips, combined with subsidies for domestic production, are meant to rebuild that capacity here. The CHIPS Act already allocated \$52 billion for this. Tariffs would be the stick to match that carrot.

The problem is that building a semiconductor fab takes five years and tens of billions of dollars. In the meantime, prices go up and availability tightens. Every industry that uses chips — which is every industry — deals with that friction.

## What This Means for the Desert Economy

Palm Springs Coachella is not insulated from any of this. Construction costs are already elevated. If tariffs hit steel, lumber, and electrical components, the math on new projects gets harder. Hospitality businesses that rely on imported goods — furniture, fixtures, equipment — face the same squeeze.

The other risk is demand destruction. If the median household loses \$2,600 in purchasing power, discretionary spending takes a hit. Travel, dining, entertainment — those are the first things to get cut. The desert economy runs on people having enough disposable income to visit, buy property, and spend money while they're here.

There's also a labor dimension. If tariffs spark inflation and the Fed has to keep rates higher for longer, the housing market stays frozen. That makes it harder to attract workers, which is already a constraint in the valley.

The optimistic case is that tariffs are a negotiating tactic and get scaled back before they do real damage. The pessimistic case is that they're implemented broadly, inflation spikes, and we end up in a stagflationary environment where growth slows but prices keep rising. That's the scenario where a lot of marginal businesses don't make it.

## What to Watch

The key date is January 20, when Trump takes office. If tariffs are announced in the first week, markets will react fast. If they're phased in or limited to specific sectors, the impact is more gradual. Either way, businesses with exposure to imports should be running scenarios now — what happens to costs, what happens to pricing power, what happens to demand.

The other thing to watch is retaliation. If the U.S. imposes sweeping tariffs, other countries will respond. China has already signaled they'll target U.S. agriculture. Europe will go after different sectors. The tit-for-tat escalation is where things get unpredictable.

For anyone building or operating in Palm Springs Coachella, the message is simple: the global economy is being rewired in real time, and the costs of that rewiring are going to show up locally. Plan accordingly.
