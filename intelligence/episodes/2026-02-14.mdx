The people who built the AI models we're all using now are saying something specific, and it's not what most coverage suggests. They're not warning that the technology might get out of control. They're saying they don't know what they created in the first place.

That distinction matters. A lot.

This comes from founders and senior people who've left Anthropic and OpenAI — not junior engineers with an axe to grind, but the architects who were in the room when these systems were designed. And their message isn't "we need better safety protocols" or "we should slow down deployment." It's "society needs to get ready because we built something we don't fully understand, and governments need to be involved now."

The acceleration itself is apparently beyond anyone's ability to manage. Not because the labs are reckless, but because the models are evolving in ways that surprise their own creators. That's a fundamentally different problem than the one most people think we're dealing with.

## What This Means for Regional Economies

Palm Springs Coachella sits at an interesting intersection here. We have a business community that's already adopting AI tools faster than most mid-sized metros — marketing agencies using generative content, hospitality groups deploying chatbots, real estate firms running predictive models on property values. But we don't have the infrastructure or policy framework that larger tech hubs are building.

When the people who created these systems say governments need to get involved, they're not talking about federal agencies in Washington. They mean local jurisdictions, regional planning bodies, the people who actually govern how technology gets deployed in communities. Because the effects show up locally first — in hiring decisions, in which businesses survive automation, in how services get delivered.

The Coachella Valley has about 350,000 permanent residents and an economy heavily weighted toward hospitality, healthcare, and small business services. All three sectors are in the direct path of AI transformation. Hotels are already experimenting with AI concierge services. Medical practices are adopting diagnostic assistants. Small businesses are using AI for everything from bookkeeping to customer service.

If the builders themselves don't know what they built, then local leaders definitely don't know what they're adopting. That's not a criticism — it's just the reality. And it means we're making decisions about technology integration without the information we'd normally require for any other major infrastructure change.

## The Employment Question Nobody's Answering

The standard response to AI displacement concerns is that new jobs will emerge, just like they did with previous technological shifts. That's probably true in aggregate. But "aggregate" doesn't help the 32-year-old hospitality worker in Cathedral City whose job disappears in 2027 and whose new opportunity supposedly appears in 2031.

The gap is what matters. And when the technology's own creators say they don't understand what they built, it becomes impossible to predict what that gap looks like or how long it lasts.

Palm Springs Coachella doesn't have the economic cushion that San Francisco or Seattle have. Our median household income is lower. Our social services are thinner. Our job market is less diverse. When a hotel chain decides to cut its front desk staff by 60% and replace them with AI systems, those workers don't have a dozen other industries to move into. They have hospitality, retail, healthcare, and not much else.

This is why the "we don't know what we created" admission is so significant. It means we can't plan. We can't train people for jobs that might not exist. We can't build safety nets sized appropriately for the displacement that's coming. We're flying blind, and the people with the best visibility are telling us they can't see either.

## Why People Are Staying Despite the Uncertainty

The observation about why people remain at OpenAI and Anthropic despite these concerns is revealing: where else would they go to get a better job or a better gig? That's not loyalty or belief in the mission. That's just market reality. If you're working on frontier AI, there are maybe five places in the world where you can do that work, and they're all facing the same fundamental uncertainty about what they're building.

This creates a strange dynamic. The people with the most expertise and the deepest understanding of these systems are economically locked into continuing to build them, even when they're publicly saying they don't understand what they're creating. It's not malice. It's not even negligence. It's just how the incentives line up.

For everyone outside those five companies — which is essentially everyone in Palm Springs Coachella — this means the technology will keep advancing regardless of whether we're ready for it, regardless of whether we understand it, regardless of whether we've built the social and economic structures to handle it.

## What Preparation Actually Looks Like

When founders say "society has to get ready," what does that mean in practical terms for a regional economy like ours?

It probably means rethinking workforce development completely. Not training people for specific jobs, but building adaptability and resilience into how people think about work. It means social services that can scale quickly when displacement happens. It means economic development strategies that aren't built on assumptions about employment stability.

It definitely means local government getting involved in conversations about AI deployment in ways they haven't yet. Not to ban or restrict — that ship has sailed — but to understand what's being adopted, who's affected, and what the community-level impacts are.

And it means business owners and community leaders acknowledging that the people who built these tools don't have the answers either. Nobody's coming to save us with a perfect implementation plan. We're all figuring this out in real time.

The difference is that some communities will figure it out proactively, with intention and planning, and others will just react to whatever happens. Palm Springs Coachella has enough economic momentum and enough smart people that we could be in the first category. But only if we start treating this like the infrastructure challenge it actually is.

The AI models are here. They're accelerating. And the people who built them are saying out loud that they don't know what they created. That's the situation. What we do with that information is up to us.
