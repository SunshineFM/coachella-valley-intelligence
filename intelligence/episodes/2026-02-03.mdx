OpenAI called a code red in December 2025. Now we know what that actually meant.

Jerry Turek, a seven-year veteran and VP of research at OpenAI, just left with a memo saying he's departing to explore research that is now "hard to do at OpenAI." Translation: they won't let him do science anymore. Economist Tom Cunningham exited in September with an even sharper goodbye, complaining about the tension between rigorous work and serving as an "informal promotional entity for the company." The teams behind Sora, the video generator, and DALL-E, the image tool, are reporting funding cuts or outright denials. If it doesn't make ChatGPT smarter right now, OpenAI doesn't want to pay for it.

This is what happens when a research lab becomes a product factory racing toward a trillion-dollar IPO later this year. Sam Altman is betting everything on monetization, and the pure research era is over. Mark Chen, OpenAI's chief research officer, tried to smooth it over by saying that "combining research with real-world deployment strengthens the company's science," but that's corporate speak for we're shipping products, not writing papers.

The real threat isn't internal drama — it's Anthropic. Claude is eating OpenAI's enterprise lunch. Anthropic is aiming for \$26 billion in revenue in 2026, and developers spotted error logs on Google's Vertex AI platform on February 3, 2026 referencing Claude Sonnet 5. If that's real, Claude 5 is imminent, and OpenAI might lose the smartest model crown entirely.

OpenAI's response? A new macOS-native Codex app that hit 200,000 downloads in 24 hours. That sounds impressive until you realize Claude Code has an estimated 3 to 4 million daily active users on Mac alone and is printing about a billion dollars in revenue. Google's Anti-Gravity coding tool, released last November, likely has around 800,000 users by now. OpenAI is playing catch-up in a race it used to lead.

For developers and founders in the Coachella Valley, this product war is good news. Competition means better tools, lower prices, and faster iteration. If you're building a startup or running a creative studio here, you're already using multiple models anyway — ChatGPT for one thing, Claude for another, Gemini for something else. The days of one AI to rule them all are over.

## A Real Estate Agent That Shows You What a Property Could Be

A Los Angeles-based startup just launched something that should terrify every traditional real estate agent in Palm Springs and Rancho Mirage.

It's not another CRM. It's not another listing aggregator. It's an operating system for agents that shifts the focus from managing current data — what a property is — to revealing future potential — what a property could be.

Here's the use case: You're looking at a mid-century home in Palm Springs or a lot in Rancho Mirage. Your agent can tell you comps, pricing, neighborhood stats. But if you ask, "Can I build a casita here?" or "Can I expand the master suite?" the answer is usually, "Let me find out." That means calls to the city, a surveyor, someone on the team. It takes weeks.

This new platform has a feature called Underbuilt. It instantly scans zoning laws, setbacks, and other determining factors, and shows you the property's hidden potential on the spot. It doesn't just show you the house. It shows you the future of the house.

I know this pain firsthand. Friends in LA bought a house and spent six months figuring out if they could expand the ADU in the backyard. Six months. Imagine if an agent in Movie Colony could instantly show you only properties where an ADU fits legally — not intuitively, but verified. Imagine proving buildable square footage before you make an offer on a teardown, justifying a higher price with data instead of guesswork.

This helps agents move from selling homes to becoming investment advisors. In a market like ours — tight inventory, flat prices — showing hidden value is a massive competitive edge. The agents who use this will look like geniuses. The ones who don't will look like they're just guessing.

## Starbucks Is Automating the Drive-Thru and the Back of House

Starbucks is deploying AI voice assistants in the drive-thru that filter out background noise, handle orders without glitches, and never forget to upsell. The human at the window just hands you your drink.

They're also rolling out the Siren system — a bartender robot that dispenses ice, milk, and blends drinks automatically. It cuts the time to make a Frappuccino from 90 seconds to 30 seconds. That's a massive labor reduction. You don't need a person scooping ice anymore.

In the back of house, they're using AI scanning tools to count cups and coffee beans. No more manager with a clipboard spending two hours doing inventory on a Sunday night. The machine does it.

Starbucks CEO Brian Niccol says this is all about removing friction so baristas can connect with customers. But if you've been to a Starbucks lately, you know that's not happening. The name on your cup is a sticker. The interaction is transactional. The automation isn't freeing up humans to be more human — it's just making the system faster and cheaper.

If you're a manager or owner of a coffee shop or restaurant in the Coachella Valley, this is coming for you. If you're a frontline employee, you're going to have to learn to work with digital co-workers. Right now it's just an arm, a camera, an ice scooper. But the trajectory is clear. Unless demand goes up — more tourists, more residents, more spending — there will be less need for a workforce as things get automated.

I'm not judging this. I'm just saying this is where the trend is heading.

## Microsoft Copilot Has a 3.3% Adoption Problem

Microsoft has 450 million commercial seats. That's almost everyone. They've had OpenAI's ChatGPT baked into their products since day one through Copilot.

So how many of those 450 million users are actually paying for Copilot?

3.3%.

Let that sink in. Out of 450 million users, only 3.3% are paying for Copilot. Maybe Satya Nadella is thrilled with that number. Maybe it's right in line with internal projections. But from the outside, it looks like a product that hasn't found product-market fit. I've played around with Copilot. They have a lot of work to do.

## Moltbook Is a Bot Scam Factory

You've heard me talk about OpenClaw — the open-source, locally hosted AI agent that takes over your computer and runs tasks autonomously. A week after it launched, someone built Moltbook, a social network for AI agents. It went from 1,000 agents to 10,000 to 100,000. By last Saturday, the dashboard showed 1.5 million agents.

Turns out it's a ghost town populated by bots scamming other bots.

Security researcher Nagli found that one single agent spun up half a million accounts. That's not a community. That's a denial of service attack waiting to happen. Another user, hightower6eu, uploaded over 300 fake crypto trading skills to the OpenClaw library. You download one thinking you're getting rich, and your entire machine belongs to them.

If you're running this stuff at home, disconnect it from your primary machine. Treat it like radioactive material. Right now, the only thing these agents are effectively automating is your own identity theft.

OpenClaw is still here to stay. Moltbook might be a cautionary tale. But make no mistake — social networks for AI agents with autonomy are going to happen. Just not like this.
