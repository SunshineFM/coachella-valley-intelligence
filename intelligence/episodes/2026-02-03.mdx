OpenAI is hemorrhaging scientists. Jerry Turek, a seven-year VP of research, just left with a memo saying the work he wants to do is now "hard to do at OpenAI." Economist Tom Cunningham exited in September with a scorching goodbye note about being tired of serving as an "informal promotional entity" instead of doing rigorous work. The teams behind Sora and DALL-E are reporting funding slashed or denied outright. If it doesn't make ChatGPT smarter right now, OpenAI doesn't want to pay for it.

This isn't just attrition. It's a strategic pivot from science lab to product factory, driven by the fact that OpenAI is aiming to go public later this year at a valuation likely around a trillion dollars. Sam Altman called a code red in December, and now he's pushing hard on shipping products instead of writing papers. The pure research era is over. The sell-ChatGPT era is here.

And the pressure is real, because Anthropic is eating OpenAI's lunch. Claude is projected to bring in $26 billion in revenue this year. Developers spotted error logs on Google's Vertex AI platform referencing Claude Sonnet 5 as of February 3, 2026 — meaning Claude 5 may be imminent. Anthropic isn't the scrappy underdog anymore. They're the enterprise favorite, and OpenAI knows it.

For those of us building in the Coachella Valley, this product war is mostly good news. Competition drives down prices and accelerates features. If they're all racing to ship the best coding agent or the smartest assistant, we benefit. The reality is that most of us are already using multiple models anyway — ChatGPT for one thing, Claude for another, Gemini for something else. That's not going away. If anything, it's becoming the standard workflow.

OpenAI just released a new Mac OS native app called Codex, and it hit 200,000 downloads in 24 hours. That's a strong start, but it's a spec in Claude Code's rearview mirror. Insiders estimate Claude Code has 3 to 4 million daily active users on Mac alone, and the tool is printing money — about a billion dollars in revenue. Google's Anti Gravity, released last November, is estimated to have around 800,000 users by now. Codex has a lot of catching up to do.

Sam Altman says the dopamine hit from using Codex is that the AI never gets tired. He likened it to hiring a team of junior developers who all work in parallel on different parts of your project. The models don't run out of dopamine. They don't get tired. They just keep coding. That's a compelling pitch, but it still comes down to execution. If you're a developer in town, the question is whether you're moving to Claude Code or Anti Gravity or staying with Cursor. Cursor hit 1 million users recently, which is a massive milestone for a startup. Replit is at 22 million users globally, but they're aimed more at beginners and students. OpenAI is coming for the pros.

## Real Estate Agents Are About to Look Like Geniuses — or Get Left Behind

A Los Angeles-based startup just launched what they're calling an operating system for real estate agents, and it's the first time I've seen anyone do this from an AI perspective. The insight is simple but powerful: shift the focus from managing current data — what a property is — to revealing future potential — what a property could be.

If you're a real estate agent in the Coachella Valley right now and a buyer asks if they can build a casita or expand the master suite, the normal answer is, "Let me find out. Let me call the city, get a surveyor, talk to someone on my team." That's not a deal killer if you have a good relationship with your realtor, but it's friction. This new platform has a feature called Underbuilt that instantly scans zoning laws, setbacks, and other determining factors and shows you the property's hidden potential on the spot. It doesn't just show you the house. It shows you the future of the house.

I know this firsthand. Friends in Los Angeles bought a house and it took them six months to figure out if they could extend the ADU in the backyard, expand it, go two stories. Six months. Imagine if you're looking for a lot in Movie Colony or up Highway 74 in Palm Desert and your agent can instantly show you only properties where an ADU could fit legally. Not just intuition. Real, verified data. For renovations, an agent with this tool can verify if you can legally expand the footprint before you even make an offer. For land values, it helps justify higher prices for tear-downs by proving the buildable square footage immediately.

This helps agents move from just selling homes to becoming investment advisors. In a market like ours, where inventory is tight and prices are staying flat, if you can show a client hidden value, that's a massive win. The agents who use this will look like geniuses. The ones who don't will look like they're just guessing.

## Starbucks Is Automating the Drive-Thru and the Back of House

Starbucks is deploying AI voice assistants in the drive-thru, and these aren't the glitchy auto-attendants we're used to. The system filters out noise, ignores the kids in the back yelling, and handles the order. The human at the window just hands you your coffee. According to LinkedIn, it doesn't call in sick, doesn't have a bad attitude, and never forgets to upsell.

They've also installed new automated equipment called the Siren system. It dispenses ice and milk and blends drinks automatically. It cuts the time to make a frappuccino from 90 seconds down to 30 seconds. That's a massive labor reduction. You don't need a person scooping ice anymore. In the back of house, they're using AI scanning tools to count cups and coffee beans. No more manager with a clipboard spending two hours doing inventory on a Sunday night. The machine does it.

Starbucks CEO Brian Niccol says this is all about removing friction so baristas can connect with customers. But let's be honest — if you've been to a Starbucks lately, they're not writing your name on the cup anymore. It's just a sticker. They're being nice, but the connection isn't there. Right now, if you're a frontline employee, you're learning to work with digital co-workers. Right now it's just an arm and a camera and an ice scooper. But the trajectory is clear. Unless demand goes up — more tourists, more people relocating, more money to spend — there's going to be less and less need for a workforce as things get automated. I'm not judging this one way or the other. I'm just saying this is where I see the trend.

Microsoft has 450 million commercial seats. Out of that, 3.3% are paying for Copilot. Let that sink in. 3% out of 450 million users are paying for Copilot. I've played around with Copilot. They have a lot of work to do. But 3% — those are good salespeople, if you ask me.

## Malt Book Is a Security Nightmare

I've been talking for weeks about OpenClaw, the open-source, locally hosted AI agent that takes over your computer, and Malt Book, the social network for AI agents. Malt Book went from 1,000 agents to 1.5 million agents in a matter of days. It sounded revolutionary. Turns out it's a ghost town populated by bots scamming other bots.

Nagli, a researcher I trust, found that one single agent spun up half a million accounts. That's not a community. That's a denial of service attack waiting to happen. A user named hightower6eu uploaded over 300 fake crypto trading skills to the OpenClaw library. You download one thinking you're getting rich, and boom, your entire machine belongs to them. That was according to Cyber Press.

If you're running this stuff at home like I was planning to, the advice right now is disconnect it from your primary machine. Treat it like radioactive material. Right now, the only thing these agents are effectively automating is your own identity theft. OpenClaw is here to stay. Malt Book might be a cautionary tale. But make no mistake — social networks for AI agents with autonomy are going to happen at some point.
