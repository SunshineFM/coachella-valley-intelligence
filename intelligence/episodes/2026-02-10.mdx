## The Promise vs. The Reality

A trainer stood in front of a small group in Palm Desert this morning and made the pitch we've all heard: AI will give you your time back. Ten social media posts in five minutes instead of half a day. Campaign emails in one minute instead of hours. Apps built in seconds instead of months. You'll finally have time to play golf, catch happy hour, leave work early.

Except that's not what's happening.

UC Berkeley researchers Aruna Ranganathan and Shinchi Magiy spent eight months — April to December 2025 — watching what actually occurs when employees at a 200-person U.S. tech company genuinely adopt generative AI. The company provided enterprise subscriptions to commercial AI tools but didn't mandate use. No pressure to hit new targets. Just access.

The pattern was consistent: people started doing more because the tools made more feel achievable. Rather than leaving work earlier, employees voluntarily expanded their to-do lists. Work bled into lunch breaks and late evenings. One engineer told the researchers: "You had thought that maybe because you could be more productive with AI, then you can save some time, you can work less, but in reality you don't work less, you just work the same amount or even more."

The researchers call this "workload creep" or "expectation creep." Once teams learned they could generate drafts or code or analysis in minutes, KPIs and throughput targets quietly ratcheted up. Workers increasingly stepped into responsibilities that previously belonged to others — product managers writing code, researchers taking on engineering tasks.

This aligns with broader data. The Upwork Research Institute found 77% of employees using AI tools said the technology added to their workload. A Quantum Workplace survey showed frequent AI users report 45% higher burnout rates compared to those who rarely or never use it. Harvard Business Review published similar findings yesterday.

What does this mean for Palm Springs Coachella? If you're a social media manager and an AI agent writes ten average posts in half an hour instead of four hours, how are you spending the other three and a half hours? You're preparing more posts. You're repurposing content into blogs or video scripts. You're expanding to another platform. Your workload gets heavier because you're doing more. Your boss or co-workers now know this is possible. You don't necessarily say you can do more — you just end up doing more.

The conversational interface of AI eliminates blank page syndrome, making it seamless to initiate tasks. The productivity surge at the beginning can give way to cognitive fatigue, burnout, weakened decision making, lower quality work, and turnover. The Berkeley researchers warn that without intentional guidelines — what they call an "AI practice," what I call an AI Council — the inherent tendency of AI-assisted work is not to reduce workloads but to intensify them.

You have to be intentional about how you use AI. You have to be responsible in how you implement it. You have to be thoughtful about strategically integrating it into your workflow and defining what outcomes you actually want.

## OpenAI Switches On Ads

Speaking of outcomes: ads are now live in ChatGPT for free-tier and lower-tier users. OpenAI said this was coming. Now it's here.

Why does this matter? OpenAI burned \$2.5 billion in just the first half of 2025. Only 5% of its 800 million weekly active users pay for subscriptions. That's lower than expected — in the mobile gaming and social gaming world, the rule of thumb is either 99-9-1 or 80-18-2. Ninety percent of users are freemium, 9% pay for subscriptions, and 1% are whales who spend beyond what premium users do. OpenAI's 5% is on the low end.

Meanwhile, 95% of users are consuming massive computational resources — actual real money in GPU power — without spending a dime. That's unsustainable. If ads can account for up to 20% of OpenAI's revenue moving forward, they'd be sustainable. That's the pitch deck, especially as they consider going public this year.

Last week, Anthropic — makers of Claude AI and a main rival to ChatGPT — ran a Super Bowl commercial trolling "ads in AI." They didn't say "ads in ChatGPT," but the jab was clear. Sam Altman, OpenAI's CEO, fired back, calling the commercial "clearly dishonest" and pointing out that more people in Texas use ChatGPT for free than the total number of people using Claude in the U.S. His argument: Anthropic is going after the wealthy. OpenAI is trying to democratize access to AI. How do you serve 800 million global users without going bankrupt? Ads subsidize that access.

The case for ads: this is the playbook. Google, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok all started free and eventually monetized through ads. For most users, ChatGPT handles low-value queries — "Why is the sky blue?" or "Help me write an email." Things people previously got for free via Google search. The friction of paying \$20 a month for Plus or \$200 for Pro is too high for casual use. Ads let OpenAI keep the service free while generating revenue to pay for compute.

OpenAI says they're building thoughtful guardrails: no ads for users under 18, no ads in conversations about health, mental health, or politics. Ads will be clearly labeled and placed outside the AI's responses. They won't influence what ChatGPT says. Users can opt out of personalization and delete their ad data. Advertisers only get aggregated metrics, no access to individual conversations. The argument is this could work like Amazon's model — more recommendations than intrusive display ads. If you're asking ChatGPT for recipe ingredients, a sponsored suggestion might genuinely be useful.

The case against ads: unlike most platforms, people use ChatGPT for deeply personal things. It's a companion, an advisor, a therapist. There's an implicit trust that the AI is working for you, not for advertisers. Even if OpenAI promises ads won't influence responses, the question of authenticity lingers. Will the AI's answers serve users or advertisers?

The privacy angle is thorny. Even with opt-outs, the fact that personal conversations could inform ad targeting — even if aggregated — feels invasive. Privacy equals freedom for some. But 8 out of 10 people say they want privacy while choosing to give it away anyway. How many people use DuckDuckGo as their search engine? Exactly.

The slippery slope: right now it's clearly labeled sponsored modules. Tomorrow? Look at Google. It started with clearly defined sponsored results. Now the font for "sponsored" is so small it's like the side effects disclaimers in prescription drug ads. Will the ads be user-first or business-first? OpenAI needs revenue. Advertisers need ROI. That creates inherent pressure to make ads more prominent, more personalized, more frequent.

Sam Altman knows he can't lose trust. That's the one thing ChatGPT has going for it. Their communication has to be nearly perfect to make sure consumers still feel like ChatGPT and them are a team. Bottom line: classic internet trade-off. Free access funded by ads, or pay for an ad-free experience. OpenAI is betting most users will accept some ads in exchange for keeping ChatGPT free. They're probably right. Most people aren't going to pay \$20 a month for casual AI help.

The real question isn't whether the ads will work. It's whether they'll be implemented in a way that respects the unique, intimate nature of conversational AI. We're moving from an attention economy to an attachment economy. That shift changes everything.

## Humanities Over Hard Skills

Daniela Amodei, co-founder of Anthropic, told ABC News that studying the humanities will become more crucial than ever as AI transforms the workforce. Her argument: uniquely human qualities like emotional intelligence and communication will gain value rather than diminish. "The things that make us human will become much more important instead of much less important."

Anthropic prioritizes hiring individuals who are exceptional communicators, possess high emotional intelligence, and are kind, compassionate, and curious with a desire to assist others — over those with purely technical backgrounds.

This is the world worth building. High emotional intelligence. Kind. Compassionate. Curious. A desire to assist others. For the last 20 years, people who studied humanities were told that's not where the future is going. For the last 20 years, they were mostly right. But with AI disrupting nearly every segment of society, there's a genuine need to find humans who are genuinely human. Curious, compassionate, with a desire to assist others, kind, and high emotional intelligence.

If you're in school or have gone to school in the humanities, this is your moment.

## AI Receptionists and the Small Business Gap

A startup called Nuo raised \$25 million to scale its receptionist platform. Their human-like AI receptionists are targeting small and medium businesses — the last to get enterprise-grade AI services and the first to experiment with mediocre chatbot tools.

Here's the problem: if chatbots represent you and your company, it's not something to take lightly. You shouldn't just put one together because you can. Yes, there are great tools to create your own AI avatar or assistant. But it's so easy to put these together and so difficult to get the perfect experience. Your customer acquisition cost is real money. You want them to have a delightful experience. Right now, most chatbot services don't deliver that.

Nuo has built about 15,000 AI agents with about 200 certified partners. Early adoption has been led by medical practices, chiropractors, opticians, dental offices, restaurants, and home service companies — places where a missed call translates to lost revenue. According to the company, small businesses miss 20% to 40% of incoming calls due to staffing constraints and after-hours inquiries.

How many times have you called a business and nobody picks up? The phone just rings off the hook. Or you call when you need something — your AC breaks at 8 p.m. — and you get an answering service or worse, voicemail. Can an AI do this better? The promise with chatbots was always there. AI receptionists might finally deliver on it.

David Yang, co-founder of Nuo, says small businesses are under pressure to respond instantly and most still lose revenue simply because no one picks up the phone. Coachella Valley: most of your phone calling technology stack is outdated. At the very least, train your staff on phone etiquette.

## Spotify, YouTube Music, and the Remix Economy

Spotify's co-CEO Gustav Soderstrom told investors they've already built the technology to let fans create AI remixes and covers of their favorite artists' music. The catch? They're waiting on the music industry to figure out the licensing framework first.

I love the idea of AI remixes and covers. I don't love the idea that everything has to go through Spotify. Unless you're in the top 1% of creators, the 99% of music artists and bands are fighting for scraps while big artists get all the remixes and additional revenue streams. The wall is getting higher and wider. If you're an independent artist or not in the top 1%, it's going to be even tougher to break through the noise.

It's already tough enough to get your music out to fans. It's already tough enough to get new fans. If you're a local band or artist, you most likely make your living through live performance, merch, and licensing. Streaming is a tiny piece of the pie — \$60 to \$85 for 10,000 streams, and that's before everyone else takes their cuts.

YouTube Music is rolling out an AI-powered playlist generator for premium subscribers, allowing custom playlists through text or voice prompts describing moods, genres, or activities. It's designed to retain subscribers and stop churn. Whether it's a progressive house mix for a chill party or 90s classic hits, it generates tailored playlists based on simple prompts. Better than making playlists manually? Maybe. We'll see.

## AI Shopping Assistants and the Trust Problem

Sixty to seventy percent of mobile shoppers are willing to use AI shopping assistants. But nearly 70% of those who try them bail because they get bad product recommendations. Chatbots aren't new. Chatbots have been around forever. Most of us haven't loved them. You're stuck in an endless loop with a bot that can't understand what you're asking.

Does AI have the potential to fix this? Yes. Are we seeing AI being used for better conversations? Not really. But things are moving fast. AI platforms are sending over a billion referral visits to major websites. Target is seeing 40% monthly growth in traffic from ChatGPT. Amazon has a chatbot called Rufus with 300 million users. People who use it are 60% more likely to buy something.

Last year, 12% of U.S. shoppers used AI assistants. That has jumped threefold to 35%. Half of those consumers say they'd let AI handle the entire shopping process, including payment. Agent-to-agent commerce is here. Consumers are open to agentic commerce. The only problem is trust. When the experience sucks, you walk away after one bad experience.

## What Lingers

The trainer in Palm Desert this morning promised more time to do what you really want. The Berkeley study says that's not what happens. You just do more. Your workload expands. Your burnout increases. The tools don't give you time back — they make more feel achievable, so you achieve more, and the bar rises.

OpenAI is betting ads can keep ChatGPT free for 800 million people while burning billions. Anthropic is betting the future belongs to people with high emotional intelligence, not just technical skills. Nuo is betting small businesses will pay for AI receptionists that don't miss calls. Spotify is betting the music industry will figure out licensing so fans can remix their favorite songs.

All of these bets hinge on the same thing: whether the technology respects the people using it. Whether it serves users or advertisers. Whether it lightens the load or just raises expectations. Whether it makes us more human or less.

That's the question worth sitting with.
