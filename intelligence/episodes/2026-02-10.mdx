A Harvard Business Review study published yesterday confirms what many of us suspected but few wanted to say out loud: workers who adopt AI tools are burning out at higher rates as workloads expand rather than contract.

This directly contradicts the pitch I heard this morning at an AI training session in Palm Desert, where the presenter promised attendees they'd get social media posts done in five minutes instead of half a day, finish email campaigns in one minute instead of hours, and have so much more time to hit happy hour or play golf. The promise sounded familiar because it's the same one the tech industry has been selling for two years.

But an eight-month study conducted by UC Berkeley researchers Aruna Ranganathan and Shinchi Magiy tells a different story. They observed a 200-person U.S. technology company that provided enterprise AI subscriptions without mandating use. The pattern was consistent: nobody was pressured to hit new targets, yet people simply started doing more because the tools made more feel achievable. Rather than leaving work earlier, employees voluntarily expanded their to-do lists, allowing work to bleed into lunch breaks and late evenings.

One engineer told the researchers: "You had thought that maybe because you could be more productive with AI, then you can save some time, you can work less, but in reality you don't work less, you just work the same amount or even more."

The researchers documented what they call "workload creep" or "expectation creep." Once teams learned they could generate drafts or code or analysis in minutes, their KPIs and throughput targets quietly ratcheted up. Workers increasingly stepped into responsibilities that previously belonged to others — product managers writing code, researchers taking on engineering tasks.

For the Coachella Valley, this isn't just a tech startup problem. If your AI agent writes ten social media posts in half an hour versus the four hours you used to spend, how are you spending the other three and a half hours? You're preparing more posts for tomorrow. Or repurposing content into blogs. Or scripting videos for another platform. Your workload gets heavier because you are doing more. And now your bosses or co-workers know this is possible.

The Berkeley research aligns with broader findings. The Upwork Research Institute found 77% of employees using AI tools said the technology had added to their workload. A Quantum Workplace survey showed frequent AI users report 45% higher burnout rates compared to those who rarely or never use the technology.

The conversational interface of AI makes initiating tasks seamless, eliminating blank page syndrome. But without intentional guidelines — what the Berkeley researchers call an "AI practice" and what I call an AI Council in the workshops we run — the inherent tendency of AI-assisted work is not to reduce workloads but to intensify them. That workload creep leads to cognitive fatigue, burnout, and weakened decision making. The productivity surge enjoyed at the beginning gives way to lower quality work and turnover.

You have to be intentional in how you use AI. You have to be responsible in how you implement it. And you have to be thoughtful about strategically integrating it into your daily workflow and defining what outcomes you actually want.

## Anthropic Bets on Humanities Over Hard Skills

Daniela Amodei, co-founder of Anthropic, told ABC News that studying the humanities will become more crucial than ever as artificial intelligence transforms the workforce. Her argument: uniquely human qualities like emotional intelligence and communication will gain value rather than diminishing.

"The things that make us human will become much more important instead of much less important," she said. Anthropic prioritizes hiring individuals who are exceptional communicators, possess high emotional intelligence, and are kind, compassionate, and curious with a desire to assist others — over those with purely technical backgrounds.

This is the world I hope we inherit. For the last 20 years, anyone studying the humanities has been told that's not where the future is going. And for the last 20 years, generally they were right. But with AI disrupting almost every segment of society, there will be a genuine need to find humans who are genuinely human. Curious, compassionate, kind, emotionally intelligent, with a desire to assist others.

If you've been to school for the humanities, your moment is coming.

## OpenAI Switches On Ads in ChatGPT

OpenAI has activated ads in ChatGPT for free and lower-tier users, exactly as promised. If you're on a Pro or premium plan, you won't see them. If you're on the free tier or the new sub-$10 Go tier, this is happening now.

OpenAI is burning cash at an astonishing rate — $2.5 billion in just the first half of last year. Only about 5% of their 800 million weekly active users actually pay for subscriptions. That's lower than expected. In the gamification world, the rule of thumb is either 99-1 or 80-18-2: 90% of users are free, 9% pay for subscriptions, and 1% are whales who spend beyond what premium users do. OpenAI's 5% conversion rate means 95% of users are consuming massive computational resources — actual real money and GPU power — without spending a dime. That's unsustainable.

OpenAI's pitch is that if ads can account for up to 20% of their revenue moving forward, they would be sustainable. Last week, Anthropic ran a Super Bowl commercial trolling "ads in AI" — a clear jab at ChatGPT that got under Sam Altman's skin. He fired back, calling the commercials "clearly dishonest" and said more people in Texas use ChatGPT for free than the total number of people using Claude in the U.S. His point: Anthropic is going after the wealthy. OpenAI is trying to democratize access to AI. And how do you serve 800 million global users without going bankrupt? You need ads to subsidize that access.

The case for ads: Google, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok all started free and eventually monetized through ads. It's the playbook that's been around for 20 to 25 years. For most users, ChatGPT is handling low-value queries like "why is the sky blue?" or "help me write an email" — things people previously got for free via Google search. The friction of paying $20 a month for Plus or $200 for Pro is too high for casual use. Ads let OpenAI keep the service free while still generating revenue.

OpenAI says they're building thoughtful guardrails: no ads for users under 18, no ads in conversations about health, mental health, or politics. Ads will be clearly labeled and placed outside the AI's responses. They won't influence what ChatGPT actually says. Users can opt out of personalization and delete their ad data. Advertisers only get aggregated metrics, no access to individual conversations. The argument is this could work like Amazon's model — more like recommendations than intrusive display ads.

The case against ads: unlike most platforms, people are using ChatGPT for deeply personal things. It's a companion, an advisor, a therapist. There's an implicit trust that the AI is working for you, not for advertisers. Even if OpenAI promises ads won't influence responses, the question of authenticity lingers. Will the AI's answers serve users or advertisers?

The privacy angle is thorny. Even with opt-outs, the fact that your personal conversations could inform ad targeting — even if aggregated — feels invasive. And there's the slippery slope: right now it may be clearly labeled sponsored modules, but tomorrow? Google started with clearly defined sponsored results. Now the font for "sponsored" is so small it's like the disclaimers on prescription drug ads.

ChatGPT cannot lose trust. That's the one thing it has going for it. Sam Altman knows this. Their communication has to be almost perfect to make sure we as consumers still feel like ChatGPT and me, we're a team.

Bottom line: classic internet trade-off. You get free access funded by ads or you pay for an ad-free experience. OpenAI is betting most users will accept some ads in exchange for keeping ChatGPT free. And honestly, I believe they're right. Most people aren't going to pay $20 a month for casual AI help. Even last summer in our workshops, most attendees weren't paying for ChatGPT. I was shocked. To me, $20 a month is affordable if you can afford Starbucks two or three times a week. Consider trading one entertainment subscription — Netflix, Prime Video, Paramount, Peacock — for access to the world's intelligence.

The question isn't whether ads will work. It's whether they'll be implemented in a way that respects the unique, intimate nature of conversational AI. We're moving from an attention economy to an attachment economy.

## Spotify Has the Tech for AI Remixes — Just Waiting on Licensing

Spotify co-CEO Gustav Soderstrom told investors they've already built the technology to let fans create AI remixes and covers of their favorite artists' music. The catch: they're just waiting on the music industry to figure out the licensing framework first.

I love the idea of AI remixes and covers. I do not like the idea that everything has to go through Spotify. Most music already goes through Spotify, and if historical precedent can be counted on, unless you're the top 1% of creators, the 99% of music creators and artists and bands are going to be fighting for scraps while the big artists get all their remixes and additional revenue streams. The wall is getting higher and wider. If you're an independent artist or not one of the top 1%, it's just going to be tougher and tougher to break through all that noise.

It's already tough enough to get your music out to your fans. It's already tough enough to get new fans. If you're a local band or artist, you most likely make your living through live performance, merch, and licensing. Streaming is a very tiny piece of the pie — we're talking about $60 to $85 for 10,000 streams, and that's before everybody else takes their cuts.

Meanwhile, YouTube Music is rolling out an AI-powered playlist generator for premium subscribers, allowing you to create custom playlists through text or voice prompts describing moods, genres, or activities. It's basically trying to give you more for your subscription to stop the churn. Whether it's a progressive house mix for a chill party or 90s classic hits, it generates tailored playlists using simple words. I think it's better than making a playlist manually. We all got tired of making our own playlists. If you haven't yet, just give it time.

## AI Shopping Assistants Are Failing — But the Traffic Is Real

About 60 to 70% of mobile shoppers are willing to give AI shopping assistants a shot. But nearly 70% of those who use them bail because they get bad product recommendations. Chatbots aren't new. Chatbots have been around forever and most of us haven't exactly loved them. You know the drill: you're stuck in some endless loop with a bot that can't understand what you're asking.

Does AI have the potential to actually fix this problem? Yes. Absolutely. Are we seeing AI being used for better conversation? Not really.

But things are moving fast. AI platforms are sending over a billion referral visits to major websites. If you were in our AI workshops last summer at Cal State University San Bernardino on Cook Street at the ERC Center, you know we trained you on how to be cited and sourced by AI chatbots. ChatGPT is number one with 800 million weekly active users. Google Gemini is catching up fast. Then you've got Claude, Perplexity, Copilot, and a bunch of other search engines using AI in their search results.

Target is seeing 40% monthly growth in traffic from ChatGPT. Amazon has a chatbot called Rufus. Did you even know that? I'm telling you, every time I use it, it's not very good. But apparently it has 300 million users, and people who use it are 60% more likely to actually buy something. That's wild.

Last year, about 12% of U.S. shoppers used AI assistants. That has jumped threefold. Now we're at about 35%. And here's something even wilder: half of those consumers say they'd let AI handle the entire shopping process, including payment. Agent-to-agent commerce is here. We told you it was coming in January 2026. Surveys and data say consumers are getting ready and are open to agentic commerce.

The only problem is trust. Trust is fragile. When the experience sucks, you walk away and you don't do much after one bad experience.

## Nuo Raises $25 Million to Scale AI Receptionists

A startup called Nuo raised $25 million to scale its AI receptionist platform. I'm bringing this up because we just talked about chatbots and how they've been not very good. Consumers want a better chatbot experience. They're just not getting it.

While we ran workshops last year showing you how to create your own AI assistant, and I was at an event this morning in Palm Desert where the facilitator was talking about how he could easily create chatbots for you, the challenge is this: if the chatbots are a representative of you and your company, it's not something you should take lightly. And in my opinion, you certainly shouldn't just put one together just because you can.

Yes, there are a bunch of great tools out there. Create your own AI avatar, create your own AI assistant who may know your data or your menu or your pricing. It is so easy to put these together, but so difficult to get that perfect experience where the person calling or texting you — your customer, whose acquisition cost is real money — has a delightful experience. Right now, most chatbot services don't have that.

That's why I'm bringing up Nuo. I know a few of the folks working with this, and their human-like AI receptionists are fantastic. They're going after small and medium businesses, not the enterprise side. I'm hoping — I think we've got converging forces here. Enterprises are providing pretty good AI services. Small and medium businesses are usually last to get these, so they're the first to jump on AI models. When they experiment, which I love — please experiment — they think these are the chatbots or AI receptionists they're going to use for their business. And two things happen. One, they're not very good because, let's be frank, you're not developers or strategists to figure out what outcome you're looking for and how to build this in the best way possible. Number two, generally you give up after you try to deploy it. You don't have all the security in place and the experience is not very good from the consumer's point of view. So you as a business owner either give up on it or just leave a mediocre product up.

I'm really glad Nuo is going after small and medium businesses. I've reached out to them to see if we can bring their services to the Valley. If so, I'd be happy to do a workshop or two sometime this year and we can go through how to set one of these up and personalize it to your business.

To date, they've built about 15,000 AI agents with about 200 certified partners. We're on the list to be one of them. We haven't been approved yet, but we'll get there. Early adoption has been led by medical practices, chiropractors, opticians, dental offices, restaurants, home service companies — places where a missed call translates to lost revenue.

According to the company data, small businesses miss about 20 to 40% of incoming calls due to staffing constraints and after-hours inquiries. How many times have you as a consumer called a business and nobody picks up the phone? It just rings off the hook. Or you call when you need something — your AC breaks and you're calling somebody at 7 or 8 or 9 o'clock at night — and they've got an answering service or worse, voicemail.

David Yang, co-founder of Nuo, says: "Small businesses are under pressure to respond instantly and most still lose revenue simply because no one picks up the phone." I know this firsthand. It frustrates me. Coachella Valley, most of your phone calling technology stack is outdated. Please get with it. At the very least, train some of your staff on phone etiquette.
