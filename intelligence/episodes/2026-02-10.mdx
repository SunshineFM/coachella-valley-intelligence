The promise was simple: AI would give you your time back. You'd finish your social media posts in five minutes instead of half a day. You'd write entire campaigns in one minute. You'd build apps in seconds. Then you'd take the afternoon off, hit happy hour, play golf.

That's not what's happening.

UC Berkeley researchers spent eight months inside a 200-person tech company watching what actually occurs when employees adopt AI tools. The finding: workers who use AI experience burnout at significantly higher rates because workloads expand rather than contract. Nobody mandated higher targets. People just started doing more because the tools made more feel achievable. Work bled into lunch breaks and late evenings. One engineer told researchers: "You thought maybe because you could be more productive with AI, then you can save some time, you can work less, but in reality you don't work less, you just work the same amount or even more."

The researchers call it workload creep. Once teams learned they could generate drafts or code or analysis in minutes, KPIs and throughput targets quietly ratcheted up. Product managers started writing code. Researchers took on engineering tasks. The conversational interface of AI eliminates blank page syndrome, making it seamless to initiate more tasks. What looks like a productivity win becomes cognitive fatigue, weakened decision making, and eventual burnout.

This aligns with broader data. Upwork Research Institute found 77% of employees using AI tools said the technology added to their workload. A Quantum Workplace survey showed frequent AI users report 45% higher burnout rates compared to those who rarely or never use the technology.

For Palm Springs Coachella, this matters immediately. Social media managers, hospitality staff, retail operations — if your AI agent writes ten social media posts in thirty minutes instead of four hours, how are you spending the other three and a half hours? You're not leaving early. You're preparing more posts for tomorrow. You're repurposing content into blogs or video scripts. You're expanding into another platform. Your workload gets heavier because you're doing more. Your boss knows you can do more. You don't necessarily say you can do more — you just end up doing more.

The Berkeley researchers warn that without intentional guidelines around AI usage — what they call an AI practice, what I call an AI Council — the inherent tendency of AI-assisted work is not to reduce workloads but to intensify them. The productivity surge at the beginning gives way to lower quality work, turnover, and other problems.

You have to be intentional. You have to be responsible. You have to be thoughtful about strategically implementing AI into your daily workflow and defining what outcomes you actually want.

## OpenAI Switches On Ads as It Burns Through Billions

Ads are now live in ChatGPT for free and lower-tier users. OpenAI spent \$2.5 billion in just the first half of last year. Only 5% of its 800 million weekly active users pay for subscriptions. That's unsustainable. The company is betting ads can account for up to 20% of revenue moving forward, enough to keep the service free for the 95% who consume massive computational resources without spending a dime.

Sam Altman fired back at Anthropic's Super Bowl commercial trolling ads in AI, saying OpenAI has more people in Texas using ChatGPT for free than the total number of people using Claude in the US. His argument: how do you serve billions of people globally who can't afford subscriptions without going bankrupt? You need ads to subsidize that access.

OpenAI promises thoughtful guardrails. No ads for users under 18. No ads in conversations about health, mental health, or politics. Ads will be clearly labeled, placed outside the AI's responses, and won't influence what ChatGPT actually says. Users can opt out of personalization and delete their ad data. Advertisers only get aggregated metrics, no access to individual conversations.

The case for ads: this is the playbook. Google, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok all started free and eventually monetized through ads. For most users, ChatGPT handles low-value queries like "why is the sky blue" or "help me write an email" — things people previously got for free via Google search. The friction of paying \$20 a month for Plus or \$200 for Pro is too high for casual use. Done tastefully, ads could be value-added rather than disruptive.

The case against: people use ChatGPT for deeply personal things. It's a companion, an advisor, a therapist. There's an implicit trust that the AI is working for you, not for advertisers. Even if OpenAI promises ads won't influence responses, the question of authenticity lingers. And the privacy angle is thorny. Even with opt-outs, the fact that your personal conversations could inform ad targeting — even if aggregated — feels invasive.

The slippery slope is real. Google started with clearly defined sponsored results. Now that font for sponsored results and advertiser info is so small it's like the disclaimers on prescription drug ads. ChatGPT can't lose trust. That's the one thing it has. Their communication has to be nearly perfect to make sure consumers still feel like ChatGPT and me, we're a team.

## The Humanities Will Matter More Than Ever

Daniela Amodei, co-founder of Anthropic, told ABC News that studying the humanities will become more crucial than ever as AI transforms the workforce. Her argument: uniquely human qualities like emotional intelligence and communication will gain value rather than diminish. "The things that make us human will become much more important instead of much less important."

Anthropic prioritizes hiring individuals who are exceptional communicators, possess high emotional intelligence, and are kind, compassionate, and curious with a desire to assist others — over those with purely technical backgrounds.

For the last twenty years, people who studied humanities were told that's not where the future is going. With AI and the ongoing disruption these technologies will have on almost every segment of society, there will be a genuine need to find humans who are genuinely human. Curious, compassionate, desire to assist others, kind, high emotional intelligence. That's the world I hope we're building.

## AI Receptionists Are Getting Real

A startup called Nuo raised \$25 million to scale its receptionist platform. They've built about 15,000 AI agents with 200 certified partners. Early adoption has been led by medical practices, chiropractors, dental offices, restaurants, home service companies — places where a missed call translates to lost revenue.

According to company data, small businesses miss 20 to 40% of incoming calls due to staffing constraints and after-hours inquiries. How many times have you called a business and nobody picks up? The phone just rings off the hook. Or you call when your AC breaks at 8pm and they've got an answering service or worse, voicemail.

David Yang, co-founder of Nuo, says small businesses are under pressure to respond instantly and most still lose revenue simply because no one picks up the phone. Coachella Valley, most of your phone calling technology stack is outdated. At the very least, train your staff on phone etiquette.

The promise with chatbots was always there. AI receptionists are getting to the point where they can be like a digital employee on payroll. But this is not something you should take lightly. Yes, there are great tools out there to create your own AI avatar or assistant. It's so easy to put these together, but so difficult to get that perfect experience where the person calling you — your customer, whose acquisition cost you've already spent — has a delightful experience. Most chatbot services don't have that yet.

## Spotify Waits on the Music Industry to Figure Out Licensing

Spotify's co-CEO Gustav Soderstrom told investors they've already built the technology to let fans create AI remixes and covers of their favorite artists' music. The catch: they're waiting on the music industry to figure out the licensing framework first.

I love the idea of AI remixes and covers. I do not like the idea that everything is going to have to go through Spotify. If existing precedent can be counted on, unless you're the top 1% of creators, the 99% of music creators and artists and bands are going to be fighting for scraps. The wall is getting higher, getting wider. If you're an independent artist or not one of the top 1% artists, it's just going to be tougher and tougher to break through all that noise.

YouTube Music is rolling out an AI-powered playlist generator for premium subscribers, allowing you to create custom playlists through text or voice prompts describing moods, genres, or activities. It's basically trying to give you more for your subscription to stop the churn. Whether it's a progressive house mix for a chill party or 90s classic hits, it generates tailored playlists using simple words.

I think it's better than making a playlist. We all got tired of making our own playlists. Telling your AI DJ to make you a playlist based on your mood could work.
