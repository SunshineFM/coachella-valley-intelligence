OpenAI just made a move that should change how we think about the intelligence economy taking shape around us. Sam Altman and the founding team pledged to give away 80% of their future wealth to AI safety and societal benefit. Not 2%. Not 10%. Eighty percent. This is the kind of number that either signals genuine conviction or sets up the most elaborate reputation-washing operation in tech history. Either way, it matters for what comes next.

The timing is worth noting. This announcement arrives as OpenAI transitions further from its original non-profit structure into something that looks more like a standard Silicon Valley rocket ship. The pledge functions as a counterweight — a public commitment that wealth accumulation from AI won't follow the usual script where founders cash out, buy sports teams, and call it impact. Instead, Altman and company are saying the money flows back into the hardest problems: making sure AI doesn't become a runaway system we can't control, and making sure the benefits don't pool exclusively in San Francisco and Seattle.

For those of us watching from Palm Springs Coachella, this isn't abstract philosophy. It's a blueprint. The intelligence layer being built right now — the agents, the models, the orchestration tools that will run everything from hospitality operations to healthcare scheduling — that layer will either concentrate power and capital in a few coastal cities, or it will distribute capability more widely. Philanthropy focused on AI safety and societal benefit is one of the few mechanisms that could push toward the latter.

Which brings us to CV Giving Day. March 3rd. First Tuesday of March. This is the Coachella Valley's largest coordinated philanthropic effort, and it's happening in six weeks. Early giving starts February 3rd. There's a kickoff event this Thursday at Acrisure Arena. If you run a nonprofit or a business that thinks about community impact, you should be in that room.

Here's the idea I've been sitting with: what if Palm Springs Coachella positioned itself as a testbed for AI-driven social impact? What if we approached OpenAI, Anthropic, or one of the other major labs and said — make us an impact partner. Not a donation. A partnership. Use our region to pilot AI tools for nonprofits. Build agent systems that help small community organizations do intake, coordinate volunteers, measure outcomes. Use our giving day as a live experiment in what intelligence infrastructure can do when it's pointed at local resilience instead of ad optimization.

The valley has the ingredients. We have a nonprofit sector that punches above its weight. We have a donor base that shows up when the ask is clear. We have a tech-curious business community that's tired of being treated like a satellite market. And we have a region that's small enough to move fast but real enough that the results would matter beyond a pilot program.

The 80% pledge from OpenAI creates an opening. It's not just about writing checks to established institutions. It's about figuring out how AI wealth can be redirected toward the places and problems that won't naturally attract venture capital. A regional giving day in a mid-sized desert market is exactly the kind of thing that fits that mandate.

This also connects to something broader happening in the AI world right now. There's a growing recognition that the people building these systems need to be in more rooms, in more kinds of communities, seeing more kinds of problems. The monoculture of San Francisco produces a monoculture of solutions. If you're only optimizing for knowledge workers in expensive cities, you're going to build tools that serve knowledge workers in expensive cities. The rest of the country — including places like ours — gets the runoff.

But if you're serious about societal benefit, you have to go where society actually is. That means smaller cities. It means nonprofits operating on tight budgets. It means local businesses trying to figure out how to stay competitive without a team of engineers. It means the kind of infrastructure work that doesn't generate TechCrunch headlines but does generate actual resilience.

The pledge also raises a harder question: what does AI safety mean in practice? It's easy to talk about existential risk and alignment research. It's harder to talk about the immediate, local risks — the small business that adopts an AI tool that hallucinates financial data, the nonprofit that automates donor outreach and accidentally alienates its base, the healthcare provider that uses a transcription model that misses critical patient information. Safety isn't just about preventing a rogue superintelligence. It's about making sure the systems being deployed today don't quietly erode trust and capability in the communities using them.

That's where local testbeds matter. If you're running experiments in a place where you can actually see the downstream effects, where the feedback loop is tight and the stakes are real, you learn faster. You build better. You catch the problems before they scale.

So here's the ask: if you're involved in CV Giving Day, start thinking about what it would mean to have a major AI partner at the table. If you're running a nonprofit, start asking what intelligence tools you actually need — not what some vendor is trying to sell you, but what would genuinely make your work more effective. If you're a donor, start asking how your dollars could help build the infrastructure that makes AI useful here, not just in Palo Alto.

And if you're at OpenAI or Anthropic or any of the other labs reading this — consider it an invitation. We're building something here. It's not a tech hub in the traditional sense. It's a community that's figuring out how to thrive in a region that doesn't fit the usual economic models. That makes it exactly the kind of place where your 80% pledge could do something real.

The intelligence economy is coming whether we shape it or not. The question is whether it arrives as something that makes us more capable, more connected, more resilient — or whether it's just another layer of infrastructure that extracts value and sends it somewhere else. Philanthropy focused on AI safety and societal benefit could be the difference. But only if it shows up in places like this.
