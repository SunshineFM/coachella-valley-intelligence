A Chinese AI lab just spent $6 million and shattered the entire economic logic of Silicon Valley's AI race.

DeepSeek released a model that matches — and in some benchmarks beats — OpenAI's latest work. The company that raised $6.6 billion in October now faces a competitor that spent roughly one-thousandth of that capital to reach the same capability. The stock market understood immediately: Nvidia dropped 17% in a single day, erasing $600 billion in market value. Broadcom fell 17%. The entire semiconductor complex that bet on infinite AI spending got repriced in hours.

This matters acutely for Palm Springs Coachella. We have founders here building on constrained budgets, watching the Bay Area and Seattle pour billions into models and infrastructure. The implicit message has been: you need venture scale capital to compete in AI. DeepSeek just proved that's wrong. They used distillation — teaching a smaller model to mimic a larger one — and reinforcement learning to achieve frontier performance without frontier budgets. The code is open source. The techniques are now public.

The implications run deeper than one company's stock price. If you can build competitive AI for $6 million instead of $6 billion, the entire narrative about moats and defensibility collapses. OpenAI's valuation rests on the assumption that training cutting-edge models requires resources only a handful of companies can marshal. DeepSeek didn't just build a good model — they published a roadmap for anyone else to do the same. That's not competition. That's commoditization.

Nvidia's CEO tried to reframe the news as positive, arguing that DeepSeek's efficiency will drive more AI adoption and therefore more chip demand. The market didn't buy it. Investors understand that if you need 1,000x less compute to reach the same result, you're not going to sell more chips — you're going to sell radically fewer. The AI infrastructure boom was predicated on exponentially growing compute requirements. DeepSeek suggests the opposite: that we're entering a phase where intelligence gets cheaper, not more expensive.

## The Attention Economy Keeps Grinding

While AI economics were imploding, Meta reminded everyone what actually prints money at scale: advertising tied to human attention. The company reported $164 billion in revenue for 2024, up from $134 billion the prior year. Net income hit $62 billion. These aren't projections or valuations — this is cash that cleared.

Meta now has 3.35 billion daily active users across its family of apps. Not monthly. Daily. That's nearly half the humans with internet access, showing up every single day. The company spent $71 billion on expenses, which sounds enormous until you realize they're generating $62 billion in profit after all of that. The operating margin is absurd.

Mark Zuckerberg also announced Meta is building a 2-gigawatt data center. For context, that's roughly the output of two nuclear reactors. The facility will be the size of a small city and will cost tens of billions to construct. Meta is betting that even if models get cheaper to train, the inference costs — running those models billions of times per day for billions of users — will still require staggering amounts of infrastructure. That might be the right bet. Or DeepSeek-style efficiency gains might apply to inference too, making this the most expensive stranded asset in tech history.

For Palm Springs founders, the lesson is simpler: attention remains the most valuable resource in the digital economy, and the companies that own it at scale can afford to make $70 billion bets. If you're building something that requires reaching people, you're either paying Meta or you're building on Meta's platforms. There's no third option at scale.

## What Efficiency Gains Actually Mean Locally

The DeepSeek story isn't just about AI labs and chip makers. It's about what becomes possible when the cost of capability drops by three orders of magnitude.

If you're running a hospitality business in Palm Springs and you've been told that implementing AI customer service or dynamic pricing requires enterprise software contracts, that calculus just changed. If you're a real estate developer trying to model climate risk or traffic patterns, the tools to do that are about to get much cheaper. If you're a local government trying to optimize water usage or energy consumption, the models that were previously accessible only to utilities with massive budgets are now in reach.

The flip side: if your business model was selling AI services at prices justified by the old cost structure, you're about to get compressed. Consulting firms that charge $50,000 to implement chatbots or automate workflows are going to face clients who know the underlying models cost almost nothing to run. The margin in AI is moving from the infrastructure layer to the application layer — and specifically to whoever owns the customer relationship and the data.

This is the pattern we've seen in every technology cycle. When compute got cheap, the value moved to software. When software got cheap, the value moved to data and distribution. When AI gets cheap, the value moves to taste, curation, and trust. The question for anyone building here is: what do you own that doesn't commoditize?

## The Bigger Uncertainty

Zuckerberg mentioned something in passing that's worth sitting with: Meta is now spending at a scale that requires them to think in terms of energy infrastructure, not just data centers. A 2-gigawatt facility isn't something you plug into the grid. It's something you build the grid around.

If the AI boom continues, we're going to see technology companies become energy companies by necessity. If the boom fizzles because efficiency gains outpace demand, we're going to see a lot of very expensive infrastructure sitting idle. Both scenarios have implications for the desert. We already have utility-scale solar. We have land. We have transmission capacity constraints that make new large loads complicated.

The question isn't whether AI matters. It clearly does. The question is whether the economic model that drove the last two years of investment survives contact with reality. DeepSeek suggests it might not. Meta's results suggest the companies with real revenue and real users will be fine either way. Everyone in between is about to find out which category they're in.
