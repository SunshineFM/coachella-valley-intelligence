President Trump announced 25% tariffs on imports from Canada and Mexico, effective February 4th. Not proposed. Announced. Done. And the markets responded exactly how you'd expect when the world's largest economy suddenly makes trading with its two biggest partners 25% more expensive overnight.

The Dow dropped 800 points. The S&P fell 1.6%. The Nasdaq shed 2%. This wasn't a gradual policy rollout with hearings and comment periods. This was an executive order weaponizing trade policy as immigration enforcement, bypassing Congress entirely under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. The justification? Fentanyl and immigration. The mechanism? Making every Canadian and Mexican import a quarter more expensive starting Tuesday.

For Palm Springs Coachella, this isn't abstract trade policy. This is construction materials for the hotel projects going up in Palm Desert. This is produce flowing through the Coachella Valley. This is the cost structure for every restaurant, every retail operation, every hospitality business that relies on cross-border supply chains. And those supply chains are about to get significantly more expensive.

## What Actually Happens When Tariffs Hit

The mechanics matter here. A 25% tariff doesn't mean prices go up 25% at the consumer level. It means importers pay 25% more to bring goods across the border, then decide how much of that cost to absorb versus pass through. In competitive markets with thin margins — which describes most of hospitality and retail — companies eat some of it and pass the rest to customers. In markets with pricing power, it all flows downstream.

Canada and Mexico aren't minor trading partners. They're the top two. Canada sends \$418 billion in goods to the US annually. Mexico sends \$466 billion. We're talking about nearly \$900 billion in annual trade suddenly subject to a 25% tariff. The scale is staggering.

And here's the part that matters locally: Canada immediately announced retaliatory tariffs on \$155 billion in US goods. Mexico is preparing its response. This isn't a negotiation. This is the opening move of a trade war, and trade wars don't have winners — they have participants who lose at different rates.

## The Market's Verdict

Markets don't panic over theoretical risks. They panic over realized ones. The 800-point Dow drop wasn't speculation. It was repricing based on new information: the US government is willing to blow up established trade relationships without warning, using emergency powers designed for actual emergencies.

The Nasdaq fell harder than the Dow because tech supply chains run through Mexico. The automotive sector, which depends on parts crossing borders multiple times during assembly, is looking at cost structures that no longer work. Every manufacturer with cross-border operations is now running scenarios on how to restructure or whether to eat the cost.

For Palm Springs Coachella, the question is what this does to the broader California economy. If manufacturing slows, if construction costs spike, if consumer spending pulls back because prices are rising — that flows through to tourism, to hospitality, to the service economy that drives the Coachella Valley.

## The Constitutional Question Nobody's Asking

Here's what should bother everyone: Trump used the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose these tariffs. That law was designed for actual emergencies — freezing assets of hostile nations, responding to immediate threats. It was not designed as a workaround for Congress on trade policy.

Congress has constitutional authority over tariffs. Article I, Section 8. The executive branch has been delegated some of that authority through various trade laws, but using emergency powers to impose 25% tariffs on your two largest trading partners because you don't like their immigration enforcement is a novel interpretation of "emergency."

This matters because if this stands, there's no limiting principle. Any president can declare any policy priority an emergency and use IEEPA to bypass Congress. That's not how the system is supposed to work, and the fact that markets are reacting to the policy rather than the process tells you how normalized executive overreach has become.

## What Palm Springs Coachella Should Watch

Construction costs. If you're building anything right now, your materials just got more expensive. Steel from Canada, components from Mexico — the tariffs hit immediately, and suppliers will adjust pricing fast.

Hospitality supply chains. Restaurants source produce, linens, equipment from across borders. Those costs are about to rise, and in an industry already operating on tight margins post-pandemic, there's not much room to absorb.

Consumer spending. If prices rise broadly — and they will — discretionary spending takes a hit. Tourism is discretionary spending. People don't cancel vacations immediately, but they do when they're feeling economic pressure for months.

The broader California economy. If this escalates into a full trade war, California's export economy gets hit hard. Agriculture, tech, manufacturing — all of it depends on open trade. Retaliatory tariffs from Canada and Mexico will target politically sensitive sectors, and California has a lot of those.

## The Timing

February 4th. That's six days from the announcement to implementation. Companies have less than a week to restructure supply chains, renegotiate contracts, or decide how to handle the cost increase. That's not policy implementation. That's shock doctrine.

And the timing matters for another reason: this is happening as the Fed is trying to manage inflation without triggering a recession. Tariffs are inflationary by definition. They make imported goods more expensive, which either raises consumer prices or squeezes margins. The Fed was already in a difficult position. This makes it harder.

For a region like Palm Springs Coachella, which depends on people having disposable income and choosing to spend it here, inflation plus economic uncertainty is a bad combination. It doesn't kill tourism overnight, but it changes the calculus for every potential visitor deciding whether to book that weekend getaway.

The markets dropped 800 points because they're pricing in what comes next: retaliation, disruption, uncertainty. Those aren't conditions where businesses invest, expand, or hire aggressively. They're conditions where everyone pulls back and waits to see how bad it gets.

We'll know soon enough. February 4th is Tuesday.
