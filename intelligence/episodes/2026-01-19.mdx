Trump announced the tariffs yesterday. Twenty-five percent on steel, aluminum, copper, and pharmaceuticals. Another 25% on autos. Effective February 1st. That's twelve days from now.

If you're building anything in the Coachella Valley right now — a house, a hotel, a solar array — your material costs just became a moving target. Steel framing, aluminum fixtures, copper wiring: all about to cost a quarter more if they cross a border. And in a region where construction timelines already stretch because of heat and labor shortages, locking in pricing before February 1st might be the difference between a project that pencils and one that doesn't.

The auto tariff hits different but just as hard. Twenty-five percent on vehicles means a \$40,000 car becomes \$50,000 at the port. Dealers in the valley are already thin on inventory after years of supply chain chaos. Now they're looking at sticker shock that could freeze sales for months while buyers recalibrate what they can afford. And this is a car-dependent region. Public transit exists in theory. In practice, everyone drives.

Trump's framing this as leverage — a way to force manufacturing back onshore and close the trade deficit. The problem is that onshore capacity doesn't exist yet for most of these materials. You can't will a steel mill into existence in six months. So the near-term effect is just higher costs with no domestic alternative ready to absorb demand. Builders and buyers eat the difference.

## Treasury Yields Are Doing Something Weird

Ten-year Treasury yields dropped 15 basis points in a single session last week. That's a massive one-day move for the bond market, which usually inches along. Yields fall when investors pile into bonds, and they're piling in because they're spooked about growth.

Here's why that matters locally. When Treasury yields drop, mortgage rates usually follow — but with a lag and not one-for-one. If this trend holds, we could see some relief on financing costs for real estate projects in the valley. That would be welcome after two years of rates sitting above 6%, which has frozen a lot of would-be buyers and made new development pencil only for the best-located projects.

But the reason yields are falling is the uncomfortable part. The market is pricing in a slowdown. Maybe a recession. The tariffs are part of that calculation. So are the federal workforce cuts, which we'll get to. The bond market doesn't panic easily. When it moves this fast, it's seeing something.

## Musk Wants to Cut Two-Thirds of Federal Employees

Elon Musk, now running the so-called Department of Government Efficiency, has been explicit: he wants to reduce the federal workforce by 66%. That's roughly 1.8 million people if you're counting civilian employees. The plan is to offer buyouts first, then move to firings if the targets aren't met.

The federal government is already seeing an exodus. Applications for federal jobs have dropped sharply. People who were considering government work are looking elsewhere. The ones already in are weighing whether to take a buyout before the axe falls.

For Palm Springs Coachella, this has two immediate implications. First, a lot of infrastructure and environmental work in the desert runs through federal agencies. Bureau of Land Management. Department of Energy. EPA. If those agencies lose two-thirds of their staff, projects slow down or stop. Permitting timelines, which are already long, get longer. Solar and wind projects that depend on federal land leases face new uncertainty.

Second implication: if 1.8 million people leave federal employment, that's 1.8 million households with less income stability. Many of them are in the DC area, but federal employees are everywhere. Some are here. And even the ones who aren't affect the national economy, which affects demand for everything from tourism to second homes.

Musk's argument is that the federal government is bloated and inefficient, and he's probably right about parts of it. But cutting two-thirds of a workforce in a matter of months doesn't produce efficiency. It produces chaos. Institutional knowledge walks out the door. Systems break. The people left behind spend their time firefighting instead of doing their jobs.

## The Confidence Problem

There's a pattern emerging across all of this. Tariffs create cost uncertainty. Bond markets signal growth fears. Federal agencies hollow out. Each of these alone is manageable. Together, they create a confidence problem.

Businesses delay investments when they can't predict costs. Buyers delay purchases when they expect prices to drop or fear losing their jobs. Developers delay projects when permitting and financing both look shaky. And once that cycle starts, it feeds on itself.

The Coachella Valley has been on a run. Population growth. Real estate appreciation. New hotels and restaurants. A lot of that momentum came from people and capital fleeing higher-cost markets and betting on the desert as a place to build. But momentum is fragile. It depends on the belief that things will keep moving forward.

If material costs spike, mortgage rates stay elevated despite falling Treasury yields, and federal projects stall, that belief gets tested. Not catastrophically. But enough to slow things down. Enough to make the marginal project not happen.

## What February 1st Actually Means

The tariffs take effect in twelve days. That's not a lot of time for businesses to adjust. Some will front-run the deadline, ordering materials now and eating storage costs to avoid the tariff. Others will wait, hoping for exemptions or delays that may not come.

For anyone in the valley working on a project with a material-heavy component, the next two weeks are decision time. Lock in pricing now, or roll the dice that costs won't actually rise as much as the tariff suggests. Neither option is great.

And for the broader economy — local and national — February 1st is when theory becomes reality. We'll see how much of the tariff cost gets absorbed by suppliers, how much gets passed to buyers, and how much demand just evaporates because people can't or won't pay the new prices.

The bond market is already voting. It thinks this ends badly. Whether that's right depends on how long the tariffs stay in place, whether domestic production can actually scale up, and whether the federal workforce cuts create the efficiency gains Musk promises or just break things.

We're about to find out.
