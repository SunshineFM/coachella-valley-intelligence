ICE detains 41,000 people every single day. That number alone makes it the largest detention system in the United States — bigger than any state prison network, bigger than the federal Bureau of Prisons. And it costs \$3.4 billion a year to run.

This isn't theoretical infrastructure being debated in Congress. It's operational. Right now. The machinery of mass deportation already exists, humming along with private contractors, county jails converting wings into ICE facilities, and a legal framework that lets the government hold people indefinitely without the protections that apply to criminal defendants.

The business model is straightforward. ICE pays private companies and county governments a per-bed, per-day rate to house detainees. GEO Group and CoreCivic — the two dominant private prison operators in this space — have built empires on this arrangement. GEO Group's stock jumped 56% the day after the 2024 election. CoreCivic saw similar gains. Investors understood immediately what a second Trump administration would mean for detention capacity.

Here's what makes this different from criminal incarceration: immigration detention is civil, not criminal. That means none of the constitutional protections around speedy trials, bail hearings, or maximum sentences apply. People can be held for months or years while their cases wind through immigration courts. The average stay is 55 days, but that's an average — plenty of people are held far longer.

The system runs on a quota. Congress has funded ICE to maintain 34,000 detention beds as a minimum. Not a maximum — a minimum. That's written into appropriations language. ICE must fill at least 34,000 beds every night, which creates a perverse incentive structure. If detention numbers drop, Congress asks why they're funding unused capacity. So ICE keeps the beds full.

County jails have figured out they can lease empty space to ICE and generate revenue. A county in rural Texas or Louisiana builds a new jail with extra capacity, then signs a contract with ICE to fill those beds at \$120 to \$150 per person per day. The county gets a guaranteed income stream. ICE gets detention space without building new facilities. Private companies often manage these contracts, taking a cut of the per-diem rate.

## The Deportation Flight Network

ICE operates its own air fleet. Not commercial flights — dedicated charter planes that move detainees from detention centers to deportation destinations. The agency calls it ICE Air. It's one of the largest charter flight operations in the country, running hundreds of flights a month to Central America, South America, the Caribbean, and increasingly to African and Asian countries.

These flights are contracted out to private carriers. Swift Air and iAero Airways are two of the main operators. They get paid per flight, per passenger, per mile. It's a logistics operation at scale — coordinating detention center pickups, routing through hub facilities, managing international landing rights, and ensuring receiving countries will accept the passengers.

The cost per deportation flight runs between \$8,000 and \$12,000 per person when you factor in detention, processing, transportation, and the actual flight. Multiply that by the 1.1 million deportations Trump has said he wants to execute, and you're looking at \$88 billion just for the removal process. That doesn't include the enforcement surge to find and arrest people, the additional immigration judges needed to process cases, or the expanded detention capacity required to hold everyone while their cases move through the system.

## What This Means for Palm Springs Coachella

The Coachella Valley has one of the highest concentrations of undocumented residents in California. Agriculture, hospitality, construction — the industries that define the local economy — all depend heavily on immigrant labor. A sustained deportation campaign here wouldn't just affect the people removed. It would collapse labor markets overnight.

Restaurants can't staff kitchens. Hotels can't turn over rooms. Date farms can't harvest crops. Construction projects stall. The ripple effects hit every business that depends on consumer spending, because when a significant portion of your population is either detained, deported, or too afraid to leave their homes, economic activity freezes.

Local law enforcement becomes a pressure point. ICE can't execute mass deportations alone — they need cooperation from county sheriffs and city police. Riverside County has historically been more cooperative with ICE than other California counties. That matters. If local agencies honor ICE detainer requests and allow ICE access to county jails, the deportation pipeline runs smoothly. If they don't, ICE has to do street enforcement, which is slower, more visible, and generates more political backlash.

The legal infrastructure around this is already being tested. Several cities and states have passed sanctuary policies that limit local cooperation with ICE. The Trump administration has threatened to withhold federal funding from sanctuary jurisdictions. That fight will play out in courts, but in the meantime, ICE will focus enforcement in places where they have local cooperation.

## The Detention Expansion Plan

Trump's team has floated the idea of building large-scale detention camps to handle the surge in arrests. These wouldn't be traditional jails — they'd be temporary facilities, possibly on federal land, designed to hold thousands of people while their cases are processed. The model is the family detention centers that operated during the first Trump administration, but scaled up significantly.

Private contractors would build and operate these facilities. GEO Group and CoreCivic are already positioning for these contracts. The per-diem rates for large-scale detention camps are lower than traditional jails — around \$80 to \$100 per person per day — but the volume makes up for it. A facility holding 5,000 people generates \$400,000 to \$500,000 in daily revenue.

The legal challenges to this approach are significant. Courts have ruled that prolonged detention without bond hearings violates due process. But those cases take years to resolve, and in the meantime, the facilities operate. The Trump administration's strategy seems to be: build the infrastructure, fill the beds, and fight the legal battles while the deportations continue.

## The Economic Calculation

Mass deportation is expensive, disruptive, and economically destructive. It removes workers from industries that can't easily replace them. It creates fear that suppresses economic activity even among people who aren't directly targeted. It requires massive government spending on enforcement infrastructure that produces no economic return.

But it's politically popular with a specific base, and it generates profits for private companies that have spent years building the capacity to execute it. That's the calculation. The economic damage is diffuse and long-term. The political benefits and corporate profits are immediate and concentrated.

For anyone building a business in Palm Springs Coachella, this is the environment. The deportation infrastructure is real, funded, and operational. The question isn't whether it exists — it's how aggressively it will be used and what that does to the labor markets and consumer base that local businesses depend on.
