description: "Mass deportation plans could remove 8.3 million workers from the U.S. economy, triggering labor shortages across construction, agriculture, and hospitality. For Palm Springs Coachella, where these sectors anchor the local economy, the stakes are existential."
---

The American Immigration Council ran the numbers on mass deportation, and the results aren't theoretical. Removing 8.3 million undocumented workers from the U.S. economy would cost $968 billion over ten years. That's not an advocacy figure — it's what happens when you pull millions of people out of industries that can't function without them.

For Palm Springs Coachella, this isn't a distant policy debate. This is about the people who build our hotels, harvest our dates, staff our restaurants, and maintain the infrastructure that makes this region work. The construction crews framing new developments in Indio. The agricultural workers in the eastern valley. The hospitality staff at every resort from Palm Springs to La Quinta. These aren't abstract labor categories. They're the economic foundation of the desert.

The Council's report lays out what mass deportation actually means in practice. You'd need to arrest 20,000 people per day to hit the numbers being discussed. That requires a deportation force larger than any current federal law enforcement agency. The logistics alone — detention facilities, processing, transportation — would require infrastructure that doesn't exist. Building it would cost more than the entire annual budget of several cabinet departments.

But the real damage isn't in the deportation costs. It's in what disappears when those workers are gone.

## The Labor Shortage Nobody's Ready For

Construction would lose 1.5 million workers overnight. In a region where housing costs are already pushing locals out and where every major development depends on crews that show up and know how to frame, pour, and finish, that's not a labor adjustment. That's a full stop.

Agriculture would lose 1.2 million workers. The Coachella Valley's date industry, its citrus groves, its specialty crops — all of it depends on workers who know the land and the seasons. You can't replace that workforce with a Craigslist ad. These are skilled jobs that require knowledge, endurance, and timing. Lose those workers and you lose the harvest.

Hospitality would lose nearly a million workers. Palm Springs runs on tourism. The hotels, the restaurants, the events that bring people here year-round — they all depend on staff who keep the machine running. When Modernism Week hits or Coachella draws 100,000 people, there's no backup workforce waiting in reserve. The people who do this work are the people who do this work.

The report projects U.S. GDP would shrink by 4.2 to 6.8 percent. For context, the 2008 financial crisis shrank GDP by 4.3 percent. This would be worse, and it would be self-inflicted.

## What Fear Does to an Economy

Even before deportations begin, the threat changes behavior. Workers disappear from job sites. Families pull kids from school. People stop going to hospitals. The chilling effect is immediate, and it doesn't require a single arrest.

In communities where undocumented workers are concentrated, businesses that depend on those workers start planning for the worst. Construction projects slow because crews are incomplete. Restaurants cut hours because staff don't show up. Agricultural operations delay planting because they can't count on harvest labor.

This isn't speculation. It's what happened in Alabama in 2011 when the state passed HB 56, one of the harshest immigration laws in the country. Workers left. Crops rotted in fields. Construction projects stalled. The state's GDP took a measurable hit. Alabama eventually backed off, but the damage was done.

The difference now is scale. Alabama was one state. A federal mass deportation program would hit every state, every sector, every community where undocumented workers are part of the economic fabric. And in the Coachella Valley, that's everywhere.

## The Tax Revenue Nobody Talks About

Undocumented workers pay taxes. Sales taxes on everything they buy. Property taxes embedded in rent. Payroll taxes withheld from paychecks, even when they're using someone else's Social Security number. The American Immigration Council estimates undocumented immigrants paid $96.7 billion in federal, state, and local taxes in 2022.

They pay into Social Security and Medicare — systems they can't access. They pay into unemployment insurance they can't claim. They fund schools their kids attend, roads they drive on, emergency services they rarely use because they're afraid of deportation.

Remove 8.3 million workers and you lose that tax base. State and local governments lose revenue they've already budgeted. Schools lose funding. Infrastructure projects get delayed. The fiscal hole doesn't get filled by the workers who replace them, because in most cases, those workers don't exist.

## What This Means for the Desert

Palm Springs Coachella is not insulated from this. The valley's economy is built on industries that depend on immigrant labor. The new hotel going up in Palm Desert. The date farm in Thermal. The restaurant in Cathedral City. All of it depends on workers who may not be here if mass deportation becomes policy.

Local businesses are already operating in an environment where labor is tight and costs are rising. A mass deportation program would turn a tight labor market into a crisis. Projects would stop. Businesses would close. The economic momentum the valley has built over the past decade would reverse.

And the workers who leave don't come back. Once someone is deported, they're banned from re-entry for years, often permanently. The institutional knowledge, the relationships, the skills — all of it disappears. You can't rebuild that quickly, and in many cases, you can't rebuild it at all.

The American Immigration Council's report is a warning. The cost of mass deportation isn't just financial. It's the cost of breaking an economy that depends on people who are here, working, contributing, and building the communities we live in.

For Palm Springs Coachella, the question is simple: Are we willing to lose the workers who make this place function? Because that's the choice on the table.
