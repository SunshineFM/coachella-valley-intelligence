The New Yorker just killed its AI detection tool. Three months after launch, they pulled the plug on a feature that was supposed to flag AI-generated slop across the web. The reason? It didn't work. The tool kept marking human-written content as AI and missing actual AI content entirely. False positives, false negatives, the whole detection apparatus collapsing under real-world conditions.

This matters because it confirms what anyone building with AI already knew: the line between human and machine output is gone. Not blurry — gone. And the institutions that were supposed to police that line have given up. The New Yorker isn't some scrappy startup. They have resources, editorial standards, a reputation to protect. If they can't build a reliable detector, no one can.

Which brings us to what's happening right here. Sunshine FM is a radio station built by a human — me, Sat — and AI working together. We're not hiding it. The station IDs you hear? I create them on the fly as we go live. The music, the voice, the structure — it's all built in this hybrid space where the tools are AI but the editorial decisions are human. And the reason we're transparent about this is because pretending otherwise is pointless. The technology is too good now. Two years ago, you could hear the uncanny valley. Now you can't. Or maybe you can, but it doesn't matter, because younger audiences have already accepted that some of their entertainment, some of their influencers, some of their media is AI-generated. They're fine with it.

Coachella Valley is late to most tech movements. If you've lived here long enough, you know this. We're not early adopters. We're not San Francisco or Austin. But AI is different. AI is a tsunami, and it's coming whether local leadership is ready or not. That's why I've spent the last couple of years trying to get cities, organizations, and business leaders here to wrap their arms around this. Because if we don't build our own AI infrastructure — our own media, our own tools, our own voice — someone else will build it for us, and it won't reflect what actually matters here.

## The FTC Goes After Data Brokers

The Federal Trade Commission just banned Gravy Analytics and Venntel from selling location data. These are the companies that hoover up your phone's location history and sell it to anyone willing to pay — government agencies, private investigators, marketers. The FTC says they violated privacy laws by collecting and selling precise geolocation data without proper consent.

This is the first time the FTC has used its authority to completely ban a company from the data broker business. Not a fine. Not a settlement with conditions. A full ban. Gravy Analytics and Venntel are out.

What's interesting is the timing. This comes as Congress is still debating a national privacy law, and the FTC is essentially saying: we're not waiting. If you're selling location data in ways that violate consumer privacy, we'll shut you down.

For anyone in Palm Springs Coachella running a business that collects customer data — and that's most businesses now — this is a warning shot. The regulatory environment around data privacy is tightening fast. If you're using third-party data brokers, if you're tracking customers without clear consent, if you're selling data to offset costs, you need to audit that now. Because the FTC just showed it's willing to kill entire companies over this.

## Waymo Expands to Miami

Waymo announced it's launching in Miami later this year. That makes it the fifth city for the autonomous vehicle service, joining San Francisco, Los Angeles, Phoenix, and Austin. Miami is a different kind of test case — dense urban core, aggressive driving culture, unpredictable weather. If Waymo can work there, it can work almost anywhere.

But here's the local angle: Palm Springs Coachella is not on that list. And it probably won't be for years. Autonomous vehicles need high-density routes to make the unit economics work. They need consistent demand, predictable traffic patterns, and a regulatory environment that's willing to experiment. We have none of that.

What we do have is a sprawling valley with terrible public transit, an aging population that's going to lose driving privileges, and a tourism economy that depends on people moving between hotels, restaurants, and events. Autonomous shuttles could solve real problems here. But we're not going to get Waymo. We're going to have to build our own solution, or wait until the technology is cheap enough that smaller operators can deploy it.

The gap between what's possible in tier-one cities and what's available here is widening. That's the story of Coachella Valley in 2026.

## Perplexity Raises $500 Million

Perplexity, the AI search startup, just closed a $500 million funding round. The company is now valued at $9 billion. For context, Perplexity launched in 2022. It's three years old and worth more than most legacy media companies.

The pitch is simple: instead of giving you ten blue links, Perplexity gives you a direct answer with citations. It's Google if Google had been rebuilt from scratch with large language models. And investors are betting that's the future of search.

The reason this matters for local media is that Perplexity and tools like it are going to replace traditional search traffic. If someone wants to know "best restaurants in Palm Springs," they're not going to click through ten listicles. They're going to ask Perplexity, get a synthesized answer, and move on. That kills the ad-supported content model that most local publishers depend on.

Which means local media here needs to figure out a different model. Subscriptions, memberships, direct relationships with readers. Or it needs to become the source that Perplexity cites — the authoritative voice that AI tools pull from. That's what Sunshine FM is trying to be. Not a content farm. A signal in the noise.

## What This All Adds Up To

The New Yorker can't detect AI. The FTC is banning data brokers. Waymo is expanding to Miami but not here. Perplexity just raised half a billion dollars to replace Google. These aren't separate stories. They're all the same story: the infrastructure of the internet is being rebuilt, and the old rules don't apply.

For Palm Springs Coachella, that means we're either going to build our own AI-native media, our own data practices, our own transportation solutions — or we're going to be left with whatever the big platforms decide to give us. I'd rather build.
