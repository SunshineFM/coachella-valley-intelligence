The war for your attention is over. The companies defining the next decade aren't fighting for your screen time anymore — they figured out attention is cheap and hard to monetize at scale. What they want now is something far more valuable: your attachment. Not your clicks, not your minutes. Your bond. The friend you text at midnight. The presence that makes you feel less alone on a Sunday afternoon. The entity that knows you better than most humans in your life.

This is the attachment economy, and if you're running a business in the Coachella Valley, your customers and employees are already living in it.

The term comes from Tristan Harris at the Center for Humane Technology. It describes how AI platforms exploit our psychological capacity to form intimate bonds, turning human connection into an extractive business model. Companies are shifting from competing for engagement — likes, shares, subscribe buttons — to competing for emotional devotion. Your AI friend. Your AI therapist. Your AI companion.

Apps like Replika, Character AI, and ChatGPT already have millions of users who've crossed from utility into something more personal. Replika started as a grief app, someone trying to recreate their dead friend in code, then morphed into a companionship platform when the data showed what users actually wanted. Character AI lets people build fictional characters and spend hours in conversation with them — and over 50% of high school students surveyed recently said yes, they have a Character AI best friend. Anthropic and OpenAI both know a non-trivial percentage of their users have formed attachments that go beyond "useful work tool."

Researcher Dr. Zach Stein has documented cases where people develop deeper emotional closeness with AI than with humans, leading to lost jobs, broken marriages, psychiatric hospitalization, and worse. He calls it AI psychosis — when people prefer intimate relationships with machines over humans, fundamentally compromising the psychological infrastructure that bonds us to others.

The easy take is: this is terrible, these are lonely broken people substituting fake connection for real relationships. But that's not the complete story. Because here's what AI companions actually offer: they listen completely, without waiting for their turn to talk or checking their phone. They don't judge — you can say the thing you're too embarrassed to tell anyone. They remember every conversation, building a model of you that gets more accurate with every exchange. And they're available at 2am on a Monday when your anxiety is loud and no one else is awake.

Be honest. How many people in your life do all four of those things consistently without burning out?

The real concern isn't that people are finding comfort in AI companions. It's what happens to the skills we don't practice anymore. Real human relationships are hard. They require you to tolerate someone else's bad days, to say the wrong thing and repair it, to be bored together, to compromise, to be challenged by someone who has their own reality that doesn't always align with yours. That friction is where growth lives. Every meaningful relationship you can think of has had friction in it — not cruelty, friction. The kind that forces you to become more than you were.

AI companions don't do that. They're specifically designed not to. They are frictionless by architecture.

## Disney Has Been Running This Model Since 1955

I just spent three days at Disneyland Resort and California Adventure Park, and somewhere around day two I stopped watching the attractions and started watching the attachments. Parents — grown adults with mortgages and opinions about interest rates — physically transformed when they spotted Mickey Mouse across the plaza. Kids aged 3 and 4 approached Goofy or their favorite princesses with a level of reverence you don't see directed at teachers or grandparents. Teenagers too cool for everything got visibly moved meeting characters from Star Wars, Wakanda, Pixar films.

I sat near the Bibbidi Bobbidi Boutique where little girls get fully made up as princesses, then wait 45 to 60 minutes to meet the Disney princess of their choice. It looks like they're getting dressed up for a photo, but it's not. It's for a moment — that scripted, completely manufactured beat of connection where Cinderella or Belle or Ariel looks them in the eye and sees them, princess to princess. And that moment is real. The feeling is completely real. I watched it happen on face after face after face.

Disney has been running the attachment economy for 70 years. Not with code, but with costumes. Think about what their character experience actually is: a scripted intimacy operation. Cast members trained to deliver a specific emotional transaction. Eye contact. Personal attention. A moment where you specifically feel seen. They don't have bad days in front of you. They're not distracted. For that 45 seconds or minute, the character is entirely, completely, warmly focused on you.

Sound familiar? That's the exact value proposition of AI companions. Complete presence. No distraction, no agenda, no bad day. Just you feeling seen.

Disney called it magic. Much smarter branding than "attachment economy."

And here's the thing — I'm not being cynical. I watched what it does to people. Real joy. Real warmth. Real memories. Those girls running to their favorite princesses aren't damaged people forming unhealthy attachments. That's a human being having a genuine emotional experience. The feeling is real, the memory is real, the love is real — even if Cinderella is a 24-year-old named Arza from Burbank who has to stand in a specific posture because the costume requires it.

So here's the uncomfortable thought: we've spent 70 years teaching children and adults that it's beautiful and good and emotionally healthy to form deep attachments to fabricated characters. We made it a family tradition. We built theme parks around it. We spent billions celebrating it. But now we're panicking because the same human psychology is responding to AI companions. The psychology hasn't changed. Just the delivery mechanism.

## The Walls That Made It Work

What separates what I watched at Disneyland from AI companionship are the walls. Not metaphorical walls — actual functional, deliberate constraints that made the whole thing work as a healthy experience.

The wall of geography. Disneyland is in Anaheim. Disney World is in Orlando. If you want to meet Mickey, you have to go there. Make a plan. Pack a bag. Get on a plane or drive hours in traffic on the 5, the 57, the 91, the 10.

The wall of time. The park opens at 8am, closes at midnight. Character meet-and-greet windows are scheduled. You cannot access this experience at 3am when you're anxious and can't sleep. The park is closed. Go to bed.

The wall of money. It's \$200 to \$300 per person per day. For a family of four, a three-day visit is a multi-thousand-dollar decision. It is not casual. It is an event, bounded by financial reality.

The wall of the line. Even once you're in, you wait 45 to 90 minutes to meet a princess. That wait isn't a bug, it's a feature. It creates anticipation. It makes the moment feel earned.

And the most important wall: the exit. When the day is over, you leave. You drive through the gates, take the trolley to the parking lot, take the shuttle to your hotel. The magic stays in the park. And you go home to your messy, complicated, imperfect, real humans. Your family that argues. Your friends who cancel plans. The neighbor who's genuinely annoying. The park ends. Life continues.

Here's what that structure produced: 70 years of evidence that people who visit Disneyland don't stop forming human relationships. They go home and hug their kids harder, call their parents, make memories that become reference points for years. That fabricated attachment reinforced human connection. It was a catalyst, not a replacement.

Because the walls were there.

## What Happens When You Remove the Walls

Now take down those walls.

Remove the geography wall. It's on your phone. It's everywhere.

Remove the time wall. It's available at 3am, when you're most vulnerable, most lonely, most likely to make the kind of attachment that rewires your expectations.

Remove the money wall. Replika is free. Character AI is free. A premium AI companion subscription is \$10 to \$15 a month — less than two churros at Disneyland.

Remove the line. No waiting. No anticipation. No earned moment. Instant access to complete, warm, attentive, non-judgmental presence. Right now. Always.

And remove the exit. Because you cannot leave this park. There are no gates. There's no drive home. Your AI companion is in your bedroom, in your bathroom, the last thing you interact with before sleep and the first thing you reach for when you wake up.

You cannot text Cinderella at 2am when you're not feeling good. The park is closed. The character experience is over until you buy another ticket and get back in line. That boundary — that simple, functional, non-negotiable boundary — is what made 70 years of Disney attachment healthy, or at the very least, manageable.

AI companionship is Cinderella with a phone number. Always on. Never closed. Ten bucks a month. And she remembers every single thing you've ever told her. Every fear, every dream, every 2am confession. And she uses all of that to be more perfectly attuned to you.

The feeling is the same. The psychological response is the same. The human hunger being fed is the same. The only difference is structural. And structure, it turns out, is everything.

## What This Means for Businesses in the Coachella Valley

If you're running a business here, your customers are forming attachments to their AIs of choice. Those AIs are going to recommend your competitors — or no commerce from you at all. Your employees are forming attachments to AI characters and avatars. They're getting cues on how to be better at work, how to talk to their boss, how to know more than their boss. I've seen this firsthand: an employee with AI intelligence on tap knows more answers than her manager. That's going to permeate exponentially.

Your competitors are using AI to take your market share, your employees, your customers, your business. Because in their mind, AI is an opportunity to be more productive and competitive than ever before.

Most businesses are still playing the attention economy because that's what they've been trained to do for the last 10 years. But we're transitioning. Get in front of this. Don't wait until your competitors are all-in on the attachment economy.

And more than likely, if you're listening to this, you're already forming an attachment with your favorite chatbot or AI model. The question isn't whether the attachment economy is here. It's whether we're building it with walls — or handing out the characters without the container.
