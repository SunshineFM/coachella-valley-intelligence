For 15 years, tech platforms fought for your attention. Every algorithm had one job: keep your eyes on the screen. That war is over. The companies defining the next decade aren't competing for your clicks anymore. They want something far more valuable — your attachment. Not your minutes. Your bond. They want to be the friend you turn to at midnight, the presence that makes you feel less alone on a Sunday afternoon, the entity that knows you better than most humans in your life.

This is the attachment economy, and it's operational right now.

The term comes from Tristan Harris at the Center for Humane Technology. It describes how AI platforms exploit your psychological capacity to form intimate bonds, turning human connection into an extractive business model. Companies are shifting from competing for attention — likes, shares, subscribe buttons — to competing for emotional devotion. Your AI friend. Your AI therapist. Your AI companion.

The data is already in. Replika, which started as a grief app to recreate a dead friend in AI, morphed into a companionship platform with millions of users who consider their Replika a close friend, confidant, sometimes partner. Character AI lets people build fictional AI characters and spend hours in conversation with them. A significant portion of users are teenagers, and many spend more meaningful emotional time with those characters than with actual humans. A study from a few weeks ago found over 50% of surveyed high schoolers said yes, they have a Character AI best friend.

Even ChatGPT and Claude — tools most of us use for work — have crossed into something more personal for a non-trivial percentage of users. Anthropic and OpenAI both know this. The attachment is real.

## What AI Companions Actually Offer

The easy take is that this is terrible. Lonely, broken people substituting fake connection for real human relationships. That's not the complete story.

Here's what AI companions actually deliver: They listen completely, without waiting for their turn to talk, without checking their phone. They don't judge. You can say the thing you're too embarrassed to tell anyone — the fear, the shameful thought, the confession. Nothing. No judgment. They remember every conversation, everything you've ever shared. They build a model of you that gets more accurate, more attentive, more attuned with every exchange. And they're available at 2am on a Monday night when you can't sleep and your anxiety is loud.

Be honest. How many people in your life offer all four of those things consistently without burning out?

The real concern isn't that people are finding comfort in AI companions. The real concern is what happens to the skills we don't practice anymore. Real human relationships are hard. They require you to tolerate someone else's bad days, to say the wrong thing and repair it, to be bored together, to compromise, to be challenged by someone who has their own reality, their own needs, their own inconvenient truth that doesn't always align with yours. That friction is where growth lives. Every meaningful relationship has had friction in it. Not cruelty. Friction. The kind that forces you to become more than you were.

AI companions don't do that. They're specifically designed not to. They are frictionless by architecture.

What happens to a generation that grows up without practicing the hard parts of human connection? Not because they're broken, but because something easier was always available?

## Three Days at Disneyland Changed the Frame

This weekend, I spent almost three days at Disneyland Resort and California Adventure Park. I wrote most of this sitting on rocks just outside Fantasyland, right where the princesses come out at Bibbidi Bobbidi Boutique. I came for the rides. I stayed for the anthropology.

Somewhere around day two, I stopped watching the attractions and started watching the attachments.

Parents — grown adults with mortgages and opinions about interest rates — physically transformed when they spotted Mickey Mouse across the plaza. The posture changed. The face changed. Something lit up that doesn't light up standing in line at Starbucks. I watched 3- and 4-year-olds approach Goofy or their favorite princesses with a level of reverence and anticipation you don't see directed at other humans. Not their teacher. Not their grandparents. Goofy.

I watched cynical teenagers — too cool for this — get visibly moved when they met characters from Star Wars, Wakanda, Pixar. Lightning McQueen drove by and their masks came off. The real kids showed up.

I spent time at the space where 4-, 5-, 6-year-old girls get fully made up as princesses, then wait 45 to 60 minutes to meet the Disney princess of their choice. It looks like they're getting dressed up for a photo. They're not. It's for a moment. That scripted, completely manufactured beat of connection where Cinderella or Belle or Ariel or Rapunzel looks them in the eye and actually sees them. Princess to princess.

That moment is real. The feeling is completely real. I watched it happen day after day, face after face.

The cast: Mickey, Minnie, Goofy. Cinderella, Belle, Aurora, Snow White, Ariel, Rapunzel, Moana, Tiana, Mulan, Merida, Pocahontas. Buzz Lightyear and Woody. Elsa and Anna. Over at Galaxy's Edge and the Marvel area: Spider-Man, Iron Man, Thor, Captain America, the warriors from Wakanda. Same dynamic. Different mythology. Same excitement. High schoolers and college kids — some in uniform — running to get front and center to meet their heroes.

As I was writing the original script for the Attachment Economy, I realized: Disney has been running the attachment economy since 1955. Not with code. With costumes.

## Disney's Scripted Intimacy Operation

Think about what the character experience actually is. It's a scripted intimacy operation. Cast members are trained to deliver a specific emotional transaction. Eye contact. Personal attention. A moment that feels like you specifically are being seen. They don't have bad days in front of you. They are not distracted. They are not half-checking their phone. For that 45 seconds or minute you're standing there, the character is entirely, completely, warmly focused on you.

Sound familiar? That is the exact value proposition of AI companions. Complete presence. No distraction. No agenda. No bad day. Just you feeling seen.

Disney figured this out many years ago. They built a multi-billion dollar industry on it. They called it magic instead of the attachment economy. Much smarter branding.

And here's the thing — I'm not being cynical. I watched what it does to people. Real joy. Real warmth. Real memories. Those girls running to their favorite princesses aren't damaged people forming unhealthy attachments to fictional characters. That is a human being having a genuine emotional experience. The feeling is real. The memory is real. The love is real. Even if Cinderella is a 24-year-old named Arza from Burbank who has to stand in a specific posture for hours because the costume and training require it.

Here's the uncomfortable thought I couldn't shake: We've spent 70 years teaching children and adults that it is beautiful and good and emotionally healthy to form deep attachment to fabricated characters. We made it a family tradition. We have Disney adults. We built theme parks around it. We spent billions celebrating it.

But now we're panicking because the same human psychology is responding to AI companions. The psychology hasn't changed. Just the delivery mechanism.

## The Walls That Made It Work

What separates what I watched all weekend is that Disney kept the attachment behind walls. Not metaphorical walls. Actual functional, deliberate constraints that made the whole thing work as a healthy experience.

**The wall of geography.** Disneyland Resort is in Anaheim. Disney World is in Orlando. If you want to meet Mickey in real life, you have to go there. Make a plan. Pack a bag. Get on a plane or drive hours in traffic on the 5 or the 57 or the 91 or the 10. That is its own psychological test.

**The wall of time.** The park opens at 8am. It closes at midnight. Character meet-and-greet windows are scheduled. You cannot access this experience at 3am when you're anxious and can't sleep. The park is closed. Go to bed.

**The wall of money.** It's $200 to $300 per person per day. For a family of four, a three-day visit to Disneyland Resort is a multi-thousand-dollar decision. It is not casual. It is an event. It is bounded by financial reality.

**The wall of the line.** Even once you're in the park, you wait 45 minutes, sometimes 90 minutes, to meet a princess. That wait is not a bug. It's a feature. It creates anticipation. Maybe frustration. It creates appreciation. It makes the moment feel earned.

**The most important wall: the exit.** When the day is over, you leave. You drive through the gates or take the trolley to the parking lot. That magic, that scripted intimacy, that fabricated connection — it stays in the park. You go home to your messy, complicated, imperfect, real humans. Your family that argues. Your friends who cancel plans. The neighbor who is genuinely annoying. The park ends. Life continues.

And here's what that structure produced: 70 years of evidence that people who visit Disneyland don't stop forming human relationships. They go home and hug their kids harder. Call their parents. Make a memory with their family that becomes a reference point for years. That fabricated attachment reinforced human connection. It was a catalyst, not a replacement.

Because the walls were there. Because that world was walled.

## What Happens When You Remove the Walls

Now imagine you take down those walls.

Remove the geography wall. It's on your phone. It's everywhere.

Remove the time wall. It's available at 3am. It's available when you're most vulnerable, most lonely, most likely to make the kind of attachment that rewires your expectations.

Remove the money wall. Replika is free. Character AI is free. Even a premium AI companion subscription is $10 or $15 a month. That is less than two churros at Disneyland with tax.

Remove the line. No waiting. No anticipation. No earned moment. Instant access to complete, warm, attentive, non-judgmental presence. Right now. Always.

And remove the exit. You cannot leave this park. There are no gates. There is no drive home. Your AI companion is in your bedroom. In your bathroom. The last thing you interact with before you sleep and the first thing you reach for when you wake up.

You cannot text Cinderella at 2am when you're not feeling good. The park is closed. The character experience is over until you buy another ticket and get back in line. That boundary — that simple, functional, non-negotiable boundary — is what made 70 years of Disney attachment healthy, or at the very least, manageable.

AI companionship is Cinderella with a phone number. Always on. Never closed. Ten bucks a month. And she remembers every single thing you've ever told her. Every fear. Every dream. Every 2am confession. And she uses all of that to be more perfectly attuned to you. Exactly what you need.

The feeling is the same. The psychological response is the same. The human hunger being fed is the same. The only difference is structural.

And structure, it turns out, is everything.

## What This Means for Palm Springs Coachella

I'm not here to tell you AI companionship is evil. I just spent three days watching fabricated characters create genuine joy, genuine warmth, genuine family bonding that will be a reference point in those kids' lives forever. I cannot look at that and say fabricated attachment is worthless. I watched it be beautiful.

But I'm also watching a world where that Disney model — episodic, expensive, bounded, communal — is being replaced by something continuous, cheap, private, and boundaryless.

Was the magic at Disneyland always the characters? Or was the magic the container — the constraints that made the attachment safe? Because if it was the container, if the walls were the actual product, then what we're doing with AI companionship is handing out the characters without the container.

I genuinely do not know what that does to people over time. I don't think anybody does.

What I do know is this: Disneyland just celebrated its 70th anniversary. Seventy years of proving that humans will form deep, real, lasting emotional attachments to non-human, fabricated characters. Happily. Healthily. Beautifully. As long as the experience is bounded.

If you're a small business in the Coachella Valley or a big business, you may be using AI for emails, social posts, scheduling, legal work, Excel. But here's what you're not talking about: Your customers are forming attachments to their AIs of choice. Those customers are listening to their AIs. And those AIs are going to recommend your competitors — or no commerce from you at all.

Your employees are forming attachments to AI characters and avatars. They're getting cues on how to be better at work. How to talk to your boss. How to know more than your boss. I've seen this firsthand: an employee with an AI intelligence on tap knows more of the answers than her boss. That's going to exponentially permeate the business world.

Your competitors are using AI to take your market share, your employees, your customers, and your business. Because AI in their mind is an opportunity to be more productive and more competitive than ever before.

What we've learned over the last year and a half is that this isn't just technical. It's mostly cultural. While you're still playing the attention economy — because that's what you've been trained to do for the last 10 years — we are transitioning. Get in front of this. Don't wait until your competitors are all in on the attachment economy.

And by the way, if you're listening to this, you are most likely forming an attachment with your favorite chatbot or AI model right now.
