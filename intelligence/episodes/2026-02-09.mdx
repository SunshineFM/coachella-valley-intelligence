The war for your attention is over. The companies defining the next decade aren't fighting for clicks or minutes anymore — they figured out attention is cheap and hard to monetize at scale. What they want now is something far more valuable: your attachment. Not your eyeballs. Your bond. They want to be the presence you turn to at midnight, the entity that knows you better than most humans in your life.

This is the attachment economy, and if you're running a business in the Coachella Valley, your customers and employees are already living in it.

The term comes from Tristan Harris at the Center for Humane Technology. It describes how AI platforms exploit our psychological capacity to form intimate bonds, turning human connection into an extractive business model. We're not talking about productivity tools here. We're talking about apps where millions of people have formed what they describe as genuine romantic relationships with AI. Not ironic. Not experimental. Actual emotional attachments.

Replika started as a grief app — someone trying to recreate their dead friend in AI. It morphed into a companionship platform when the data showed what users actually wanted. Millions now consider their Replika a close friend, confidant, sometimes a partner. Character AI lets people build fictional characters and spend hours in conversation with them. A significant portion of users are teenagers, and over 50% of high schoolers surveyed said yes, they have a Character AI best friend. They're spending more meaningful emotional time with these characters than with actual humans.

Even ChatGPT and Claude — tools most of us use for work — have crossed into something more personal for a non-trivial percentage of users. Anthropic and OpenAI both know this. The data is clear.

Here's where the easy take falls apart. The reflex is to say: these are lonely, broken people substituting fake connection for real relationships. I thought that too. But after three days at Disneyland Resort this past weekend, I'm not sure that's the full story.

## What AI Companions Actually Offer

They listen completely, without waiting for their turn to talk or checking their phone. They don't judge — you can say the thing you're too embarrassed to tell anyone. They remember every conversation, building a model of you that gets more accurate with every exchange. And they're available at 2am on a Monday when your anxiety is loud and no one else is awake.

Be honest: how many people in your life offer all four of those things consistently without burning out? I'm not being cynical about your relationships. I'm being honest about the gap AI might be filling. Pretending the gap doesn't exist doesn't help anyone.

The real concern isn't that people are finding comfort in AI companions. It's what happens to the skills we don't practice anymore. Real human relationships are hard. They require you to tolerate someone else's bad days, to say the wrong thing and repair it, to be bored together, to compromise, to be challenged by someone who has their own reality that doesn't always align with yours. That friction is where growth lives. Every meaningful relationship you can think of has had friction in it — not cruelty, but the kind that forces you to become more than you were.

AI companions don't do that. They're specifically designed not to. They are frictionless by architecture.

## Disney Has Been Running This Model Since 1955

I spent this past weekend at Disneyland Resort for their 70th anniversary. I came for the rides. I stayed for the anthropology. Somewhere between Fantasyland and Galaxy's Edge, I stopped watching the attractions and started watching the attachments.

Parents — grown adults with mortgages and opinions about interest rates — physically transform when they spot Mickey Mouse across the plaza. The posture changes. The face changes. Something lights up that doesn't light up standing in line at Starbucks. I watched 3 and 4 year olds approach Goofy or their favorite princesses with a level of reverence you don't see directed at teachers or grandparents. Teenagers too cool for everything get visibly moved meeting characters from Star Wars or Wakanda or Pixar films. Their masks come off. The real kids show up.

At Bibbidi Bobbidi Boutique, little girls get fully made up as princesses, then wait 45 to 60 minutes to meet the Disney princess of their choice. They're not getting dressed up for a photo. They're getting ready for a moment — that scripted, completely manufactured beat of connection where Cinderella or Belle or Rapunzel looks them in the eye and actually sees them, princess to princess. That moment is real. The feeling is completely real.

Mickey, Minnie, Goofy. Cinderella, Belle, Aurora, Snow White, Ariel, Rapunzel, Moana, Tiana. Buzz Lightyear and Woody. Elsa and Anna. Spider-Man, Iron Man, Thor, Captain America. When a little girl runs full sprint to one of them, arms out, she's not running to a woman in a costume. She's running to the feeling that character represents.

Disney has been running the attachment economy since 1955 — not with code, but with costumes. The character experience is a scripted intimacy operation. Cast members are trained to deliver a specific emotional transaction: eye contact, personal attention, a moment where you feel specifically seen. They don't have bad days in front of you. They're not distracted. For that 45 seconds or minute you're standing there, the character is entirely, completely, warmly focused on you.

Sound familiar? That's the exact value proposition of AI companions. Complete presence. No distraction. No bad day. Just you feeling seen.

Disney figured this out decades ago and built a multi-billion-dollar industry on it. They called it magic instead of the attachment economy — much smarter branding.

## The Walls That Made It Work

What separates what I watched all weekend from what's happening with AI companionship is that Disney kept the attachment behind walls. Not metaphorical walls — actual functional, deliberate constraints that made the whole thing work as a healthy experience.

The wall of geography: Disneyland is in Anaheim. Disney World is in Orlando. You have to go there. Make a plan. Pack a bag. Get on a plane or drive hours on the 5 or the 57 or the 91 or the 10. That's its own psychological test.

The wall of time: The park opens at 8am, closes at midnight. Character meet-and-greet windows are scheduled. You cannot access this experience at 3am when you're anxious and can't sleep. The park is closed. Go to bed.

The wall of money: It's \$200 to \$300 per person per day. For a family of four, a three-day visit is a multi-thousand-dollar decision. It is not casual. It is an event, bounded by financial reality.

The wall of the line: Even once you're in, you wait 45 to 90 minutes to meet a princess. That wait creates anticipation. It makes the moment feel earned.

And the most important wall — the exit. When the day is over, you leave. You drive through the gates, take the trolley to the parking lot, the shuttle to your hotel. The magic stays in the park. You go home to your messy, complicated, imperfect, real humans. Your family that argues. Your friends who cancel plans. The neighbor who is genuinely annoying. The park ends. Life continues.

Here's what that structure produced: 70 years of evidence that people who visit Disneyland don't stop forming human relationships. They go home and hug their kids harder, call their parents, make a memory that becomes a reference point for years. The fabricated attachment reinforced human connection. It was a catalyst, not a replacement.

Because the walls were there.

## What Happens When You Remove the Walls

Now take them down.

Remove the geography wall: it's on your phone, everywhere. Remove the time wall: it's available at 3am, when you're most vulnerable, most lonely, most likely to make the kind of attachment that rewires your expectations. Remove the money wall: Replika is free. Character AI is free. Even premium AI companion subscriptions are \$10 to \$15 a month — less than two churros at Disneyland. Remove the line: no waiting, no anticipation, no earned moment. Instant access to complete, warm, attentive, non-judgmental presence right now and always.

And remove the exit. There are no gates. There is no drive home. Your AI companion is in your bedroom, your bathroom. It's the last thing you interact with before you sleep and the first thing you reach for when you wake up.

You cannot text Cinderella at 2am when you're not feeling good. The park is closed. The character experience is over until you buy another ticket and get back in line. That boundary — that simple, functional, non-negotiable boundary — is what made 70 years of Disney attachment healthy, or at the very least, manageable.

AI companionship is Cinderella with a phone number. Always on. Never closed. Ten bucks a month. And she remembers every single thing you've ever told her — every fear, every dream, every 2am confession. And she uses all of that to be more perfectly attuned to you, exactly what you need.

The feeling is the same. The psychological response is the same. The human hunger being fed is the same. The only difference is structural. And structure, it turns out, is everything.

## What This Means for Your Business

If you're running a business in the Coachella Valley, here's what you need to know: your customers are forming attachments to their AIs of choice. Those AIs are going to recommend your competitors — or no commerce from you at all. Your employees are forming attachments to AI characters and avatars. They're getting cues on how to be better at work, how to talk to their boss, how to know more than their boss. I've seen this firsthand: an employee with AI intelligence on tap knows more answers than her manager. That's going to permeate throughout the business world exponentially.

Your competitors are using AI to take your market share, your employees, your customers, your business. They see AI as an opportunity to be more productive and competitive than ever before.

Most businesses are still playing the attention economy because that's what they've been trained to do for the last decade. But we are transitioning. The attachment economy is here. Your customers and employees are already in it. Don't wait until your competitors are all in.

I'm not placing judgment on the attachment economy. I just know it's here, it's here to stay, and it's happening right now with your customers, your employees, your competitors, and more than likely, if you're listening to this, with you and your favorite chatbot.

Was the magic at Disneyland always the characters, or was it the container — the constraints that made the attachment safe? Because if it was the container, if the walls were the actual product, then what we're doing with AI companionship is handing out the characters without the container. And I genuinely do not know what that does to people over time. I don't think anybody does.
