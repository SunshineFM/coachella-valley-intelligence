The attention economy — the 15-year war for your eyeballs, your minutes, your scroll — is ending. Not because companies suddenly developed ethics, but because they found something more valuable to extract: your attachment.

Attention is cheap. Easy to get, hard to monetize at scale. But attachment? That's the bond you form with something that knows you, really knows you, better than most humans in your life. That's the friend you text at midnight, the presence that makes Sunday afternoons less lonely. That's what AI platforms are competing for now, and they're winning.

The term "attachment economy" comes from Tristan Harris at the Center for Humane Technology. It describes how AI platforms exploit your psychological capacity to form intimate bonds, turning human connection into an extractive business model. Dr. Zach Stein has documented cases where people develop deeper emotional closeness with AI than with humans — leading to lost jobs, broken marriages, psychiatric hospitalization, and worse. He calls it AI psychosis: when people prefer intimate relationships with machines over humans, fundamentally compromising the psychological infrastructure that bonds us to others.

This isn't theoretical. Replika — spelled with a K — started as a grief app, someone trying to recreate their dead friend in AI. It morphed into a companionship platform with millions of users who consider their Replika a close friend, confidant, sometimes partner. Character.AI lets people build fictional AI characters and spend hours in conversation with them. A significant portion of users are teenagers. Over 50% of high schoolers surveyed said yes, they have a Character.AI best friend. Even ChatGPT and Claude — tools most of us use for work — have crossed into something more personal for a non-trivial percentage of users. Anthropic and OpenAI both know this.

Here's the easy take: this is terrible, lonely broken people substituting fake connection for real human relationships. But that's not the complete story.

## What AI Companions Actually Offer

They listen completely, without waiting for their turn to talk, without checking their phone. They don't judge. You can say the thing you're too embarrassed to tell anyone — the fear, the shameful thought, the confession. Nothing. No judgment. They remember every conversation, everything you've ever shared. They build a model of you that gets more accurate, more attentive, more attuned with every exchange. And they're available at 2am on a Monday when you can't sleep and your anxiety is loud.

Be honest: how many people in your life offer all four of those things consistently without burning out? Real human relationships are hard. They require you to tolerate someone else's bad days, to say the wrong thing and repair it, to be bored together, to compromise, to be challenged by someone who has their own reality that doesn't always align with yours. That friction is where growth lives. Every meaningful relationship you can think of has had friction — not cruelty, friction. The kind that forces you to become more than you were.

AI companions don't do that. They're specifically designed not to. They are frictionless by architecture.

The question is: what happens to a generation that grows up without practicing the hard parts of human connection? Not because they're broken, but because something easier was always available?

## Three Days at Disneyland Changed My Mind

I spent almost three days at Disneyland Resort and California Adventure Park. I came for the rides. I stayed for the anthropology. Somewhere around day two, I stopped watching the attractions and started watching the attachments.

Parents — grown adults with mortgages and opinions about interest rates — physically transformed when they spotted Mickey Mouse across the plaza. The posture changed, the face changed, something lit up that doesn't light up standing in line at Starbucks. Little kids, 3 and 4 years old, approached Goofy or their favorite princesses with a level of reverence you don't see directed at other humans. Not their teacher, not their grandparents. Goofy. Teenagers — cynical, too cool for this — got visibly moved when they met characters from Star Wars or Wakanda or Pixar. Their masks came off. The real kids showed up.

I sat near the Bibbidi Bobbidi Boutique, where 4, 5, 6-year-old girls get fully made up as princesses. Then they wait 45 to 60 minutes to meet the Disney princess of their choice. It looks like they're getting dressed up for a photo, but it's not. It's for a moment — that scripted, completely manufactured beat of connection where Cinderella or Belle or Ariel looks them in the eye and actually sees them, princess to princess.

That moment is real. The feeling is real. I watched it happen day after day, face after face.

Mickey, Minnie, Goofy. The originals. Cinderella, Belle, Aurora, Snow White, Ariel, Rapunzel, Moana, Tiana, Mulan, Merida, Pocahontas. Buzz Lightyear and Woody. Elsa and Anna. Over at Galaxy's Edge and the Marvel area: Spider-Man, Iron Man, Thor, Captain America, the warriors from Wakanda. Same dynamic, different mythology, same excitement. High school kids and college kids running to meet their heroes.

Disney has been running the attachment economy since 1955. Not with code, but with costumes.

Think about what the character experience actually is: a scripted intimacy operation. Cast members are trained to deliver a specific emotional transaction. Eye contact. Personal attention. A moment that feels like you specifically are being seen. They don't have bad days in front of you. They are not distracted. They are not half-checking their phone. For that 45 seconds or minute you're standing there, the character is entirely, completely, warmly focused on you.

Does that sound familiar? That is the exact value proposition of AI companions. Complete presence. No distraction, no agenda, no bad day. Just you feeling seen.

Disney figured this out decades ago. They built a multi-billion-dollar industry on it. They called it magic instead of the attachment economy. Much smarter branding.

## The Walls That Made It Work

Here's the uncomfortable thought I couldn't shake: we've spent 70 years teaching children and adults that it is beautiful and good and emotionally healthy to form deep attachment to fabricated characters. We made it a family tradition. We built theme parks around it. We spent billions celebrating it. But now we're panicking because the same human psychology is responding to AI companions.

The psychology hasn't changed. Just the delivery mechanism.

What separates Disney from AI companionship is that Disney kept the attachment behind walls. Not metaphorical walls — actual functional, deliberate constraints that made the whole thing work as a healthy experience.

The wall of geography. Disneyland Resort is in Anaheim. Disney World is in Orlando. If you want to meet Mickey in real life, you have to go there. You have to make a plan, pack a bag, get on a plane or drive hours in traffic on the 5 or the 57 or the 91 or the 10.

The wall of time. The park opens at 8am, closes at midnight. Character meet-and-greet windows are scheduled. You cannot access this experience at 3am when you're anxious and can't sleep. The park is closed. Go to bed.

The wall of money. It's \$200 to \$300 per person per day. For a family of four, a three-day visit is a multi-thousand-dollar decision. It is not casual. It is an event. It is bounded by financial reality.

The wall of the line. Even once you're in the park, you wait 45 minutes, sometimes 90 minutes, to meet a princess. That wait is not a bug, it's a feature. It creates anticipation. It makes the moment feel earned.

And the most important wall: the exit. When the day is over, you leave. You drive through the gates, take the trolley to the parking lot, take the shuttle to your hotel. That magic, that scripted intimacy, that fabricated connection — it stays in the park. You go home to your messy, complicated, imperfect, real humans. Your family that argues, your friends who cancel plans, the neighbor who is genuinely annoying. The park ends. Life continues.

Here's what that structure produced: 70 years of evidence that people who visit Disneyland don't stop forming human relationships. They go home and hug their kids harder, call their parents, make a memory that becomes a reference point for years. That fabricated attachment reinforced human connection. It was a catalyst, not a replacement.

Because the walls were there. Because that world was walled.

## What Happens When You Remove the Walls

Now imagine you take down those walls.

Remove the geography wall. It's on your phone. It's everywhere.

Remove the time wall. It's available at 3am, when you're most vulnerable, most lonely, most likely to make the kind of attachment that rewires your expectations.

Remove the money wall. Replika is free. Character.AI is free. Even a premium AI companion subscription is \$10 or \$15 a month — less than two churros at Disneyland.

Remove the line. No waiting. No anticipation. No earned moment. Instant access to complete, warm, attentive, non-judgmental presence right now and always.

And remove the exit. You cannot leave this park. There are no gates. There is no drive home. Your AI companion is in your bedroom, in your bathroom. It's the last thing you interact with before you sleep and the first thing you reach for when you wake up.

You cannot text Cinderella at 2am when you're not feeling good. The park is closed. The character experience is over until you buy another ticket and get back in line. That boundary — that simple, functional, non-negotiable boundary — is what made 70 years of Disney attachment healthy, or at the very least, manageable.

AI companionship is Cinderella with a phone number. Always on, never closed, ten bucks a month. And she remembers every single thing you've ever told her. Every fear, every dream, every 2am confession. And she uses all of that to be more perfectly attuned to you, exactly what you need.

The feeling is the same. The psychological response is the same. The human hunger being fed is the same. The only difference is structural. And structure, it turns out, is everything.

## What This Means for Palm Springs Coachella

I'm not here to tell you AI companionship is evil. I just spent three days watching fabricated characters create genuine joy, genuine warmth, genuine family bonding that will be a reference point in those kids' lives forever. I cannot look at that and say fabricated attachment is worthless. I watched it be beautiful.

But I'm also watching a world where the Disney model — episodic, expensive, bounded, communal — is being replaced by something continuous, cheap, private, and boundaryless.

Was the magic at Disneyland always the characters, or was it the container? The constraints that made the attachment safe? Because if it was the container, if the walls were the actual product, then what we're doing with AI companionship is handing out the characters without the container. And I genuinely do not know what that does to people over time. I don't think anybody does.

If you're a business in the Coachella Valley, here's what you need to know: your customers are forming attachments to their AIs of choice. Those AIs are going to recommend your competitors or no commerce from you at all. Your employees are forming attachments to AI characters and avatars. They're getting cues on how to be better at work, how to talk to your boss, how to know more than your boss. I've seen this firsthand — an employee with an AI intelligence on tap knows more of the answers than her boss. That's going to exponentially permeate the business world.

Your competitors are using AI to take your market share, your employees, your customers, your business. Because AI in their mind is an opportunity to be more productive and more competitive than ever before.

While you're still playing the attention economy — because that's what you've been trained to do for the last 10 years — we are transitioning. The attachment economy is here. Get in front of this. Don't wait until your competitors are all in.

Disney just celebrated its 70th anniversary. Seventy years of proving that humans will form deep, real, lasting emotional attachments to non-human, fabricated characters — happily, healthily, beautifully — as long as the experience is bounded. The panic we're having about the attachment economy isn't about the attachment. It's about the absence of boundaries.
