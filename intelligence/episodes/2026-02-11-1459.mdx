The managing partner at a major law firm spends hours every day using AI because it now does the work of an entire team of associates. A cybersecurity engineer in our valley is bartending. UC enrollment in computer science just dropped for the first time since the dot-com crash. And on February 5th, OpenAI released a model that helped build itself.

If you think this is about the future, you're already behind.

## The Ground Is Shaking in Tech. You're Next.

Matt Schumer, CEO of HyperWrite AI, posted something this week that's been viewed 54 million times. It's everything I've been saying for 18 months, written by someone who actually builds this stuff. His message: the experience tech workers had over the last year — watching AI go from helpful tool to "does my job better than I do" — is about to happen to everyone else.

I just got back from a presentation in Palm Desert where a room full of business owners and executives were learning what an LLM is and how to write better prompts. Three years after ChatGPT launched. We are not at the starting line. We are asleep at the wheel while the race has already started.

Schumer describes what his Mondays look like now. He tells an AI what app he wants built. He walks away for four hours. He comes back to find tens of thousands of lines of code written, the app tested by the AI itself — clicking through buttons, evaluating user flow, iterating on design — and a finished product that requires zero corrections. Not a rough draft. The finished thing.

A year ago, he was going back and forth with AI, guiding it, making edits. Now he just describes the outcome and leaves. The model released last week, GPT-5.3 Codex, has something that feels like judgment. Like taste. The inexplicable sense of knowing what the right call is that people said AI would never have.

It has it now.

## Why Coding Came First, and Why That Matters for You

The AI labs made a deliberate choice. They focused on making AI great at writing code first, because building AI requires a lot of code. If AI can write that code, it can help build the next version of itself. A smarter version writes better code, which builds an even smarter version. Making AI great at coding was the strategy that unlocks everything else.

Software engineers started changing first not because they were targeted — it was a side effect of where the labs chose to aim. Now they've done it. And they're moving on to everything else.

Law. Finance. Medicine. Accounting. Consulting. Writing. Design. Customer service. The people building these systems say one to five years. Some say less. Given what Schumer has seen in the last two months, he thinks less is more likely.

And I agree.

## The Measurement That Should Terrify You

There's an organization that tracks the length of real-world tasks — measured by how long they take a human expert — that AI can complete successfully end-to-end without human help. A year ago, the answer was roughly 10 minutes. Then it was an hour. Then several hours. The most recent measurement showed AI completing tasks that take a human expert nearly five hours.

That number is doubling approximately every seven months. Recent data suggests it may be accelerating to every four months.

If you extend the trend, we're looking at AI that can work independently for days within the next year. Weeks within two. Month-long projects within three.

Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, has said AI models substantially smarter than almost all humans at almost all tasks are on track for 2026 or 2027. If AI is smarter than most PhDs, do you really think it can't do most office jobs?

## What's Happening in Our Own Backyard

Alphabet is offering voluntary exit packages to employees in its global business organization who "may not be ready to embrace the company's accelerated AI-focused direction." That was in an internal memo sent this week. Specific roles: solutions teams, sales teams, corporate development.

If you don't subscribe to AI at Google, there's a severance package waiting for you.

And where should those people go? Here. Palm Springs Coachella. 350 days of blue skies. Commercial real estate lease rates so much cheaper than the Bay Area that you can have a longer runway to experiment in stealth. I've been pitching this to my Silicon Valley friends for months.

But we need to be ready for them. And right now, we're not.

I've been talking to Cal State San Bernardino, UCR, College of the Desert for almost two years. They know what the right thing to do is. But nothing has moved. I submitted proposals for workshops and boot camps last September and October. Despite nudges and email reminders, nothing has happened.

Meanwhile, UC San Diego just launched an undergraduate AI major last year. It was the only UC campus where computer science enrollment didn't decline. Every other campus saw drops — the first time since the dot-com bust that CS degrees have lower enrollment than the previous year.

Students and parents are terrified that computer science degrees won't lead to stable employment anymore. Many CS graduates describe job searches taking six months to a year, submitting hundreds of applications while competing against experienced developers who were laid off. Our own software engineer in residence, 22 years old, is living this right now.

A Reuters-Ipsos poll found that 71% of Americans fear AI will put too many people out of work permanently. A 2026 survey from ResumeNow found 60% of workers believe AI will eliminate more jobs than it creates this year, and 51% report personal concern about losing their jobs to automation.

That was published two hours ago.

## The Real Estate Shockwave

Commercial real estate services firms plummeted today as investor anxiety over AI's potential to disrupt labor-intensive industries spread. CBRE Group, Jones Lang LaSalle, Cushman & Wakefield all tumbled 15% to 20% — the steepest single-day declines for all three companies since March 2020.

An analyst at Raymond James characterized it as an "AI scare trade," noting that investors are rotating out of high-fee, labor-intensive business models viewed as potentially vulnerable to AI-driven disruption. That was compounded by weak guidance from Zillow, which plunged 20% after issuing a first-quarter forecast below expectations.

We're a real estate town. Hospitality, second homes, commercial development, property management. If you're in the industry here, you need to be paying attention to what's happening to the business models that support your work.

I've also been tracking something more immediate: data centers. Anthropic just pledged to cover the costs of grid infrastructure upgrades required to power their facilities. City council members from Palm Springs to Coachella are looking to attract data centers powered by wind or solar. They say we have enough water to cool them.

Three gigantic data centers are being built in Imperial County right now without any input from residents. One is being built right next to a new middle school that's still under construction. Council members are looking at the money and taxes, not listening to the health, wellness, and environmental concerns of their residents.

If you want to talk about this, get on the air. Come over here. Let's talk about it.

## What You Should Actually Do

Schumer's advice is the same I've been giving for 18 months. Start using AI seriously. Not as a search engine. Sign up for the paid version of Claude or ChatGPT — it's \$20 a month. Make sure you're using the best model available, not the default. Right now that's GPT-5.2 on ChatGPT or Claude Opus 4.6 on Claude.

Don't just ask it quick questions. Push it into your actual work. If you're a lawyer, feed it a contract and ask it to find every clause that could hurt your client. If you're in finance, give it a messy spreadsheet and ask it to build the model. If you're a manager, paste in your team's quarterly data and ask it to find the story.

The people who are getting ahead aren't using AI casually. They're actively looking for ways to automate parts of their job that used to take hours.

Spend one hour a day experimenting with AI. Not passively reading about it — using it. Try to get it to do something new every day. If you do this for the next six months, you will understand what's coming better than 99% of the people around you.

That's not an exaggeration. Almost nobody is doing this right now. The bar is on the floor.

## The Window Is Closing

This might be the most important year of your career. Right now there's a brief window where most people at most companies are still ignoring this. The person who walks into a meeting and says "I used AI to do this analysis in an hour instead of three days" is going to be the most valuable person in the room.

Not eventually. Right now.

That window won't stay open long. Once everyone figures it out, the advantage disappears.

I'm not writing this to make you feel helpless. I'm writing this because the single biggest advantage you have right now is being early. Early to understand it. Early to use it. Early to adapt.

The future isn't coming. It's already here. It just hasn't knocked on your door yet.
