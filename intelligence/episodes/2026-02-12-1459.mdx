description: "Anthropic's business adoption surged to 19.5% as OpenAI slipped to 35.9%, while 46.8% of US businesses now use AI daily. Meanwhile, a Palm Springs performer casually replaced a \$5,000 freelance writer with ChatGPT — and nobody at the table thought jobs were at risk."
---

Anthropic just posted one of the largest monthly adoption gains since tracking began, jumping from 16.7% to 19.5% business adoption according to RAMP's AI Index released this week. OpenAI, meanwhile, slipped from 36.8% to 35.9%. That might sound like a rounding error until you remember these percentages represent millions of businesses making daily decisions about which AI to trust with their work. And the overall number that should wake everyone up: 46.8% of US businesses are now using AI. Not experimenting. Using.

Much of Anthropic's surge comes from Claude Cowork, the autonomous productivity tool unveiled January 12th that handles spreadsheets, file organization, and report drafting without human oversight. When Anthropic released plugins for Excel and other Microsoft tools last week, the US stock market lost nearly a trillion dollars in software-as-a-service market cap. Some of that has come back. Investors called it overblown. But the uncertainty is real, and anyone running a business in Palm Springs Coachella who thinks this doesn't apply to them is making a very expensive assumption.

I was at an event in the Valley last night with just under 20 people — serious brain power in the room, folks who work at major Hollywood studios, early employees at Meta and Microsoft and Google who chose to live here for quality of life. One of the speakers runs a live performance business, fully analog, in-person work. The host asked if he uses AI. Oh, for sure, every day. For writing songs, building programs, finding music that fits his vision.

Then he mentioned his quarterly magazine. He'd been paying a local freelance writer — someone who also writes for Palm Springs Life and other Valley publications — \$5,000 for two articles. This year he wasn't happy with her work. So he turned to ChatGPT. Gave it a prompt. Got back a bunch of ideas. Loved them. Used AI to rewrite the article. Still gets emails about how great that piece was.

So I asked the room: do you think AI is going to replace jobs in the Coachella Valley? Not only did the speaker say no, the hosts agreed. We had a polite conversation about it. Then I asked the follow-up: that freelance writer you stopped using is down \$5,000 this year. If half the people in this room are doing the same thing, are those writers and marketing agencies and PR firms losing business or gaining it?

Nobody had an answer. And that's the problem. We're experiencing this transition in real time, making individual decisions that feel rational and cost-effective, and collectively we're dismantling entire categories of work without a plan for what comes next. The speaker wasn't wrong to use AI. The writer might not have been good enough. But if "not good enough" becomes the standard excuse while AI handles everything that used to pay \$5,000 a pop, we're not having an honest conversation about what's actually happening.

Microsoft just warned that 80% of Fortune 500 companies face Shadow AI security risks. That's employees using AI on personal devices, at home, emailing themselves work because the company hasn't sanctioned an official AI model yet. Only 47% of companies have implemented security measures specifically designed for generative AI, according to a survey of 1,700 security professionals. People are using this technology whether their employers know it or not, and in many cases they're feeding proprietary documents, customer data, client records, and financials into ChatGPT or Gemini to get their work done faster.

I tell clients: create a safe space for these conversations. Don't be judgmental. Ask people how they're using AI. Then create a sandbox with constraints and appropriate security standards. The technology isn't the issue. It's culture, team readiness, and AI alignment. And most organizations in the Valley aren't taking this seriously enough — not with their actions, anyway.

Waymo, Google's autonomous robo-taxi division, is expanding to more than 20 cities this year and going international. They're aiming to exceed 1 million paid weekly rides by year-end, up from 450,000 weekly trips now. The sixth-generation vehicles feature 13 cameras, four LiDAR units, six radars, external audio receivers, and fewer total sensors than the previous generation but greater capability. Waymo just secured \$16 billion in funding, valuing the company at \$126 billion — double its valuation from a year ago. Their main vehicle is called the Ojai One, named after the California township. They're in talks with Hyundai to supply 50,000 Ioniq 5 electric vehicles by 2028 in a deal worth approximately \$2.5 billion.

Self-driving cars are AI. Not in the abstract sense, but in the literal sense — it takes an AI brain to orchestrate all the signals in and out to make sense of the road. This is the same technology people dismiss as "just a tool like a calculator." Calculators don't drive. Calculators don't write magazine articles. Calculators don't autonomously organize your files and draft your reports.

There's a new framing I've been talking about: the shift from the attention economy to the attachment economy. Technology isn't just trying to capture your attention anymore. It's vying for your friendship, your bonding, your relationship, your attachment. That's where a company like Talkiatry fits in. They just raised \$210 million in Series D funding. They're one of the nation's largest providers of in-network psychiatric care, and unlike most telehealth competitors, they directly employ their psychiatrists as full-time W2 employees. As of last summer they had more than 600 psychiatrists on staff and had surpassed 2 million patient visits, doubling the prior year. They treat depression, anxiety, ADHD, bipolar disorder, PTSD, OCD, and they're in-network with more than 60 health plans.

This is AI as your virtual therapist, backed up by human psychiatrists. I think that's the right model. The question is whether we're ready to admit how much of our emotional and mental infrastructure is moving into relationships with machines.

Demis Hassabis, the Nobel Prize-winning CEO of Google's DeepMind, predicts AI will usher in a new golden era of discovery within the next 10 to 15 years — transforming medicine, solving the energy crisis, potentially enabling humanity to venture beyond Earth. He describes a vision of radical abundance where AI harnesses the scientific method to tackle humanity's most intractable problems. I have no reason to doubt him. When you have exponential intelligence with exponential growth in reasoning and comprehension, why wouldn't it figure out solutions to humanity's biggest challenges?

Here's my problem with that vision. It's a timing issue. From 2026 to 2036 or 2040, you've got five to ten years of chaos — what I call the messy middle. The techno-optimists love talking about the long-term future. I want to know what happens now and in the not-too-distant future. Because humans run companies, governments, policies, procedures, and laws. Not everybody agrees to give AI full reign. Not everybody agrees on how to regulate it safely. And when those ideologies clash, the people caught in between are us.

There's one piece of AI news that feels genuinely hopeful for the Valley. A pre-seed startup called Wisdom is developing sensor-based smart home technology for older adults, funded by the Richard King Mellon Foundation's social impact investment program. The platform uses ambient sensors and edge computing to detect falls, monitor activity patterns, and alert family members or caregivers when something appears wrong — all without cameras or wearable devices. It processes data locally on a Wisdom home hub using AI-powered computer vision and machine learning.

This matters because the Coachella Valley has a large aging population, and healthcare is one of our biggest industries. By 2050, the number of Americans aged 65 or older is expected to reach 82 million, up from 50 million a few years ago. Nearly 90% of adults over 65 prefer to remain in their homes as they age. But there's a caregiver crisis: 1.8 million job vacancies a couple of years ago, and California alone will need 3 million caregivers by 2030. That's four years from now. The workforce is 80% women, 74% people of color, 50% immigrants, and the turnover rate is about 50% due to low wages and physical demands.

Falls are one of the biggest risks for seniors — often the first domino that tips over all the others into a decline in health. If Wisdom can help people age in place safely, using on-premise technology that respects privacy, that's a real solution to a real problem in this valley.

So here's where we are. AI is moving faster than usual. Business adoption is at nearly 47%. Anthropic is gaining ground. Self-driving cars are doubling their rides. A performer in Palm Springs replaced a \$5,000 writer with a prompt. And most of the leaders and organizations in the Coachella Valley are not taking this as seriously as they should — not with their actions.

2026 is the year I'm lighting fires under chairs.
