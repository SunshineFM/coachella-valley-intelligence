Trump wants to impose 25% tariffs on imports from Mexico and Canada starting February 1st. The National Association of Home Builders ran the numbers: that adds roughly $10,000 to the cost of building a new single-family home. Not theoretical. Not "could impact." Ten thousand dollars, because lumber, steel, appliances, and building materials cross those borders constantly.

Here's the compounding problem: Trump also wants to deport what the construction industry estimates is 1.5 million undocumented workers currently building homes across America. The NAHB is being direct about this — you cannot remove that much labor from an industry already struggling with workforce shortages and expect anything except higher costs and slower builds. They're asking for a guest worker program instead, something structured that doesn't collapse the sector.

For Palm Springs Coachella, this isn't abstract policy. We're in the middle of a housing shortage. Every new development, every ADU, every hotel renovation depends on materials that flow across borders and labor that's already expensive and hard to find. Add $10,000 in tariff costs to every unit. Remove a significant portion of the available workforce. Then try to build affordably. The math doesn't work.

The construction industry is now in the strange position of lobbying against deportations not out of ideology but because the alternative is economic collapse. When the people who build things are telling you a policy will make building impossible, that's worth listening to.

## California's Insurance Exodus Continues

State Farm just pulled out of California's commercial property insurance market entirely. Not reducing coverage. Not raising rates. Gone. This follows their earlier exit from homeowner policies, and it's part of a pattern where major insurers have decided California's combination of wildfire risk, litigation environment, and rate restrictions makes the market unworkable.

For commercial property owners in Palm Springs — hotels, retail centers, office buildings — this is a direct hit. You can't operate without insurance. You can't get financing without insurance. And now one of the largest insurers in the country has decided your market is too risky to participate in at any price.

The California FAIR Plan exists as the insurer of last resort, but it's expensive and limited. What we're watching is a slow-motion crisis where the private insurance market withdraws from California entirely, leaving property owners with fewer options and higher costs. Every hotel operator, every retail landlord, every commercial developer in the valley needs to be thinking about this now, not when their policy comes up for renewal and they discover their carrier is gone.

## Meta Abandons Fact-Checking

Meta announced they're ending their third-party fact-checking program in the United States and replacing it with community notes — the system X uses where users add context to posts. Zuckerberg framed this as reducing censorship and admitting that fact-checkers had become "too politically biased."

The practical effect: Facebook and Instagram will no longer label or downrank content that independent fact-checkers identify as false. Instead, they'll rely on crowdsourced corrections that appear below posts, if enough users with diverse viewpoints agree on the context.

This is a significant shift in how information flows on the platforms most Americans still use daily. Whether you think fact-checking was biased or essential, its removal changes what you'll see and what will spread. For local news, for public health information, for anything where accuracy matters, we're now in a different environment.

X's community notes system has worked reasonably well at adding context without heavy-handed moderation, but it's also slower and less comprehensive than dedicated fact-checking. Meta is making a bet that user-generated corrections will be seen as more legitimate than expert review. We'll find out if that's true.

## The Drone Mystery Continues

The FBI now says the mysterious drones spotted over New Jersey and other East Coast locations in December were likely a mix of lawful commercial drones, hobbyist drones, law enforcement drones, and some mistaken sightings of regular aircraft. They found no evidence of a national security threat or foreign involvement.

This is the official explanation after weeks of public concern and speculation. It's also deeply unsatisfying, because it doesn't explain why so many people — including local law enforcement and elected officials — reported coordinated drone activity that seemed beyond hobbyist capabilities.

Either this was mass hysteria and misidentification on a remarkable scale, or the federal government is downplaying something they don't want to discuss publicly. Neither option is particularly comforting. What's clear is that drone technology has advanced to the point where distinguishing between commercial, recreational, law enforcement, and potentially hostile drones is genuinely difficult, and we don't have good systems for tracking or regulating the airspace below traditional aircraft altitudes.

For a region like ours that depends on tourism and has significant infrastructure spread across open desert, the question of who can fly what over our communities isn't theoretical. The technology exists. The regulations are catching up slowly.

## What This Means Here

The through-line in all of this is systems under stress. The construction industry can't absorb simultaneous tariffs and labor deportations. The insurance industry can't make California's risk profile work. Meta can't maintain fact-checking without accusations of bias. The federal government can't explain what people saw in the sky.

These aren't separate stories. They're all examples of institutions that worked reasonably well for decades now struggling to function under new conditions — political polarization, climate change, technological disruption, economic pressure.

For people building businesses in Palm Springs Coachella, the lesson is that you can't assume the systems you depend on will remain stable. Insurance might disappear. Labor might vanish. Materials might double in cost. The platforms you use to reach customers might change their rules overnight.

The businesses that survive are the ones that plan for instability, that build redundancy, that don't depend on any single system continuing to work the way it always has. That's not pessimism. That's realism in 2025.
