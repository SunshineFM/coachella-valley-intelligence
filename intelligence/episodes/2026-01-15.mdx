Controlled Thermal Resources is putting \$2.5 billion into a lithium extraction facility at the Salton Sea. First production is slated for 2026. This is not a pilot project. This is industrial scale — the kind of capital deployment that changes regional employment patterns, tax bases, and what kinds of businesses can survive here.

The timing matters because the Coachella Valley is already short on skilled labor. Not just construction workers, though that's acute. Electricians, HVAC techs, solar installers, the people who actually build and maintain the infrastructure everyone keeps announcing. Sat mentioned this workforce gap explicitly, and it's showing up in project timelines across the valley. You can have all the capital and permits you want, but if you can't staff the site, nothing moves.

Lithium extraction at this scale requires geothermal expertise, chemical engineering, industrial maintenance crews. Those jobs pay well, but they require training that takes years. The valley's community colleges are trying to build programs, but they're racing against a construction boom that's already here. Controlled Thermal is one of several lithium projects in the region. EnergySource Minerals is also active. The Salton Sea geothermal field is becoming a critical node in the battery supply chain, and most people in Palm Springs still think of it as a dead lake.

## The Solar Buildout Nobody Sees

Clearway Energy Group just commissioned a 200-acre solar farm in the Coachella Valley. That's utility-scale generation, not rooftop panels. The project went live this month. Sat didn't specify exact megawatt capacity, but 200 acres of solar in this climate is significant baseload contribution. It's also another data point in the valley's transformation into an energy export region.

This isn't about sustainability theater. It's about land use economics. Solar pencils out here because land is cheaper than in coastal California, sun is abundant, and transmission infrastructure already exists from the old coal and gas days. What's changing is the pace. Projects that used to take a decade from proposal to commission are now moving in three to four years. That's partly permitting reform, partly capital availability, partly the fact that solar is now the cheapest form of new electricity generation in California.

For local businesses, this matters in two ways. First, energy costs. If the valley becomes a net exporter of cheap electricity, industrial users start looking at relocating here. Data centers, manufacturing, anything energy-intensive. Second, construction demand. Every one of these projects needs roads, fencing, security, maintenance contracts. That's local spend if local companies can scale to meet it.

## The Education System Isn't Ready

Coachella Valley Unified School District is trying to build career pathways in construction, healthcare, and technology. Sat covered this as a positive development, and it is — but the gap between what's being taught and what employers need is still enormous. The district is adding CTE programs, but those take years to mature. A kid starting a construction pathway in ninth grade won't be job-ready until 2029 at the earliest. The lithium facility is hiring now.

The structural problem is that the valley's education system was built for a tourism and agriculture economy. Hospitality and seasonal farm work don't require the same technical training as geothermal engineering or utility-scale solar maintenance. The jobs being created now are higher-wage, higher-skill, and the pipeline isn't there yet. That means companies are importing labor from outside the valley, which means housing pressure, which means cost of living increases for everyone already here.

This is fixable, but it requires coordination between school districts, community colleges, and employers that historically hasn't existed. Controlled Thermal and Clearway could fund training programs directly. Some companies do this — Tesla built a training center in Nevada for Gigafactory workers. But most don't, and the valley's smaller contractors can't afford to. So the workforce gap persists.

## What This Means for Local Business

If you're running a business in Palm Springs or Coachella, the next three years are going to be defined by labor costs and availability. Wages are going up because demand is outstripping supply. That's good for workers, hard for small businesses operating on thin margins. Restaurants, retail, services — anything that competes for entry-level labor is going to feel this.

The flip side is opportunity. The construction boom needs subcontractors, suppliers, logistics. Solar farms need cleaning services, security, landscaping. Lithium facilities need everything from catering to equipment rental. If local businesses can position for this work, there's real revenue available. But it requires moving faster than most small businesses are used to. Bidding on contracts, meeting insurance requirements, scaling capacity.

The valley is also going to see more engineers, project managers, and technical workers moving in. That's a different customer base than retirees and weekend tourists. They want different housing, different amenities, different services. Coffee shops that open early. Coworking spaces. Childcare that accommodates shift work. The businesses that adapt to this demographic shift will do well. The ones that don't will wonder why foot traffic is changing.

## The Bigger Picture

What's happening in the Coachella Valley is a case study in energy transition economics. Lithium, solar, geothermal — these aren't separate stories. They're parts of the same infrastructure buildout, driven by the same capital flows, constrained by the same workforce limits. The valley has the natural resources. It has the land. It has the transmission lines. What it doesn't have yet is the human capital to execute at the pace the market is demanding.

That's the gap Sat keeps circling back to. The projects are real. The money is real. The timeline is compressed. And the valley's institutions — schools, workforce development, local government — are still catching up. The question isn't whether this transformation happens. It's whether the people already living here benefit from it, or whether it happens around them while they're priced out.

Controlled Thermal's \$2.5 billion is the largest single investment, but it's not the only one. The valley is becoming a critical node in the clean energy supply chain. That's a 20-year story, not a news cycle. But the next three years will determine who captures the value and who just pays the higher rent.
