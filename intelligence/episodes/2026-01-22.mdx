Waymo just closed a \$5.6 billion funding round. Not a Series A. Not scrappy startup money. This is Andreessen Horowitz, Silver Lake, and T. Rowe Price writing checks to scale a robotaxi network that's already live in San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Phoenix. The company is doing 150,000 paid rides per week right now. That's not a pilot. That's a business.

And if you're building anything in Palm Springs Coachella that depends on how people move — hospitality, retail, events, real estate — this matters more than most tech headlines you'll scroll past today.

## The Waymo Expansion Math

Waymo operates in three cities today. The \$5.6 billion is explicitly for expansion. They're not saying which cities yet, but the pattern is clear: warm weather metros with sprawl, tourism infrastructure, and car dependency. Palm Springs Coachella checks every box.

The company is already testing in Austin and Atlanta. But the Coachella Valley has something those cities don't — a constrained geographic footprint with massive seasonal population swings. That's actually ideal for autonomous deployment. You can map the region thoroughly, handle predictable traffic patterns most of the year, then stress-test the system during Coachella, Stagecoach, and BNP Paribas when the population doubles.

Waymo's vehicles are Jaguar I-PACE electric SUVs retrofitted with lidar, radar, and cameras. They don't need a safety driver anymore. In San Francisco, you can open the app, request a ride, and a car shows up with nobody in it. The door unlocks when you approach. You get in. It drives you. You get out. That's the product.

For Palm Springs, the question isn't whether this is coming. It's whether local leadership understands that autonomous vehicles require infrastructure decisions now — curb space, pickup zones, charging networks, data-sharing agreements with cities. The places that get this right early will shape how the technology integrates. The places that don't will get whatever the default rollout looks like.

## Meta's Llama 4 and the Open Weight Gamble

Meta released Llama 4 Scout and Llama 4 Pro. These are open-weight models, meaning anyone can download them, run them locally, and build on top of them without paying Meta per query. Scout is the faster, cheaper version. Pro is the heavyweight — competitive with GPT-4 and Claude on reasoning tasks.

The interesting move here is that Meta is explicitly positioning Llama as infrastructure. They're not trying to sell you a chatbot subscription. They're trying to make sure that when every company in the world integrates AI into their product, they do it with a model Meta controls the architecture of.

For small businesses and developers in the Coachella Valley, this matters because open-weight models mean you can build AI features without getting locked into OpenAI or Anthropic's pricing. A local hospitality company could run a Llama-based booking assistant on their own servers. A real estate firm could build property analysis tools without sending client data to a third party. The cost structure is completely different when you're not paying per token.

But there's a catch. Running these models locally requires technical capability most businesses don't have. So what you'll see is a new layer of service providers — local or regional firms that host Llama models and offer them as a service. That's an opportunity for anyone in the valley with infrastructure and AI chops.

## The Deepseek Distraction

Deepseek, a Chinese AI lab, released a model they claim was trained for \$6 million. The AI world spent 48 hours losing its mind over this. The narrative was that American AI companies are wasting billions while China is doing it lean and smart.

Except the \$6 million figure is almost certainly bullshit. It likely refers only to the final training run, not the research, prior experiments, compute infrastructure, or data acquisition. It's like saying a restaurant cost \$50,000 to open because that's what you spent on furniture, ignoring the lease, the kitchen equipment, and the two years you spent developing recipes.

The real story is that China is very good at AI research and is competing hard on the global stage. That's worth paying attention to. The \$6 million meme is not.

## xAI's Grok Goes Free

Elon Musk's xAI made Grok free for all X users. Previously it was paywalled behind X Premium. Now anyone with an X account gets access.

This is a user acquisition play. Grok isn't better than ChatGPT or Claude. It's not worse either. It's fine. But by making it free and embedding it directly into X, Musk is betting that distribution beats differentiation. If 500 million people have Grok in their pocket because it's just there when they open X, that's a user base OpenAI can't match.

For people building businesses in Palm Springs Coachella, the takeaway is that AI tools are becoming ambient. You won't "go use an AI." You'll just have AI available wherever you already are. That changes how you think about customer service, content creation, and internal workflows. The question isn't whether to adopt AI. It's whether you're adopting it faster than your competitors.

## Perplexity's Publisher Program and the Future of Search

Perplexity launched a revenue-sharing program with publishers. When Perplexity's AI-generated answers cite a publisher's content, that publisher gets a cut of ad revenue. Time, Fortune, and Entrepreneur signed on.

This is the first real attempt to solve the "AI search engines kill traffic to publishers" problem. Google built its empire by sending traffic. Perplexity and ChatGPT answer questions directly, so users never click through. Publishers were furious. Now Perplexity is saying: we'll pay you directly.

It's not clear this works economically. But it's the beginning of a new model. And for local media in the Coachella Valley, it's a preview of what's coming. AI search engines will summarize your reporting. The question is whether you get paid for that or just become free training data.

## What This Means for Palm Springs Coachella

Waymo's \$5.6 billion raise is the headline, but the through-line in all of this is infrastructure. Autonomous vehicles need physical infrastructure. Open-weight AI models need compute infrastructure. AI search engines need content infrastructure.

The regions that build or attract that infrastructure will have an advantage. The regions that don't will be consumers of someone else's platform.

Palm Springs Coachella has a window right now. The valley is small enough to move fast, wealthy enough to invest, and connected enough to attract the right partners. But infrastructure decisions have long lead times. If Waymo expands here in 2027, the city planning conversations needed to happen in 2025.

The same logic applies to AI. The businesses that start experimenting with Llama models now, that start thinking about how autonomous delivery or robotaxis change their operations, that start building relationships with AI infrastructure providers — those are the businesses that will be positioned when this stops being a novelty and becomes the baseline.

The future is being built right now. The question is whether we're building it here or just waiting for it to arrive.
