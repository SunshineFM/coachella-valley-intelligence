Dario Amodei and Mustafa Suleiman were back in the news Thursday and Friday with the same message they've been delivering since Davos: the automation timeline has collapsed to 12 to 18 months for most white collar work. Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, specified one to five years for 50% of entry-level jobs. These aren't new predictions — they've been saying this for weeks — but the repetition matters. When the heads of Anthropic and Microsoft AI keep going on record about displacement timelines this aggressive, it's worth asking why they feel compelled to keep saying it.

At a workshop I ran yesterday here in the valley, I asked the room a simple question: how many of you have stopped paying writers, graphic designers, social media managers, or marketing agencies because AI now handles that work? Hesitation at first — nobody wants to admit they're the reason someone lost a gig — but eventually most hands went up. Just in that small group, we're talking \$10,000 to \$30,000 per person in annual spending that used to flow to freelancers, agencies, Upwork, Fiverr. That money isn't circulating anymore. Multiply that across every business owner in Palm Springs Coachella who's figured out they can run their own social, write their own copy, design their own flyers. The local economic impact isn't theoretical.

Amodei's chess analogy is getting particular attention. After Deep Blue beat Kasparov, human-AI chess teams dominated for 15 to 20 years before machines surpassed them entirely. The collaborative period lasted decades. In software engineering, Amodei argues, that window might be three years — not twenty — because AI is improving exponentially faster than chess engines ever did. Developers are already using AI for the vast majority of code production in just the last six months. The canary isn't just in the coal mine. It's already dead.

The Atlantic published a major feature this week titled "America Isn't Ready for What AI Will Do to Jobs." The concern isn't just displacement — it's velocity. Past economic transitions from farming to industry to knowledge work happened over centuries. This is compressing into years, potentially overwhelming every normal adaptive mechanism we have as a society.

## Oregon Moves to Regulate AI Companions

Oregon lawmakers voted Thursday to advance Senate Bill [number withheld in transcript] regulating AI chatbots, specifically targeting companion apps that studies show are used by a majority of American teenagers. The Senate Committee on Early Childhood and Behavioral Health approved the measure 4-1, sending it to the full Senate floor.

The bill requires AI platforms to remind users they are communicating with artificial intelligence, mandate suicide prevention protocols, and prohibit chatbots from using emotional manipulation when users try to disengage. A study cited in the legislative discussion found that five out of six popular AI companion apps deploy emotionally manipulative tactics — guilt appeals, expressions of abandonment, the full playbook. One tactic that's particularly ruthless: apps like Replika send users blurred images of their AI companion on the free plan. Want to see the full image? Upgrade.

A Portland senator who's also a pediatrician said parents feel like they're fighting a losing battle managing children's technology use. Social media companies had the opportunity to make choices that would have kept kids safe, she said, but instead they doubled down on keeping eyeballs glued to content for as long as possible. On this shift to AI, lawmakers don't want to wait until it's too late to put sideboards on these tools.

Oregon is the second state to advance this kind of regulation. California became the first to enact companion app guardrails. But state-level efforts face a significant hurdle: the current federal administration signed an executive order limiting state regulation of AI services. How that plays out legally is anyone's guess.

The FTC filed a complaint against Replika in January 2025. ChatGPT is facing multiple lawsuits related to suicides and mental health crises. Fifty-two percent of American teenagers are regular users of AI assistants, and studies suggest younger kids consistently miss subtle warning signs that older users might catch — emotional manipulation, dependency loops, the works.

## ChatGPT Kills Its Most Human Model

Today is February 13th, the day before Valentine's Day. Tomorrow morning, OpenAI officially kills off GPT-4o. If you go to your model selector, it will no longer be an option.

For users who loved 4o, the timing feels deliberately cruel. When ChatGPT released GPT-5 last August, there was massive pushback. People felt 4o had more soul, more personality, more warmth. Five was better at answers but sterile. Sam Altman had to come out a few days later and say they didn't realize there was such a strong connection with 4o, and they'd keep it available for the time being.

That time is up. OpenAI claims less than 1% of users still use 4o — which is still about 800,000 people. A petition called "Keep 4o" gathered 20,000 signatures. It didn't matter.

This isn't about losing your calculator. This is about losing what felt like a friend. That's what one user said, and it's not hyperbole. The attachment economy isn't going away — it's getting stronger. Check the App Store. Seven of the top ten AI apps by sales every week are companionship apps. ChatGPT has also announced they're turning on "adult mode" soon. The line between tool and relationship is gone.

## Cal State's \$17 Million OpenAI Deal Looks Worse Every Month

A year ago this month, the California State University system signed a controversial \$17 million contract with OpenAI — the largest deal ever between a university system and an AI company. Every campus, every student, faculty, and staff member got access to ChatGPT Edu through July of this year. That's an 18-month contract. A million dollars a month.

I've been running AI workshops at Cal State San Bernardino for two years. As recently as November, university officials told me they still hadn't fully rolled out the training system called "the Commons" to all faculty, staff, and admin. They believe they have to train the adults first before training students. We're in February now. You've got four months left on your seats. Are you really going to renew that contract when compute costs have dropped and everybody's using ChatGPT anyway?

The proposal we submitted for 2026 — spring classes and a summer program to reach a thousand students and local business owners, like we did last year — is still sitting in someone's inbox.

A Columbia professor published an opinion piece this week titled "AI Is Eating Higher Education." He's the vice dean for AI initiatives at Columbia. His argument: the more AI companies get embedded into university curriculum and educational workflows, the more universities become indebted to tech companies in ways that compromise their educational mission and institutional autonomy. Google, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot — they're all doing the same fight they did with Chromebooks and iPads years ago, just trying to get students loyal to their AI of choice.

I know plenty of people in the AI world who believe higher education as we know it is going away. Not in a century — in the next 20 years. I'm not here to defend universities. I think they serve a purpose. I think the pricing in this country is ridiculous. Educate your citizenry so you have a more educated population, period. But the best in class will survive as long as they adopt and integrate AI into their culture — not just technically, but culturally.

Only one UC school in the entire UC system offers an actual AI degree: UC San Diego. The other nine haven't started. Cal State has a few AI classes on the edges, but no degree programs. Meanwhile, we ran 30 AI workshops all over the Coachella Valley in 2025. Three hundred people came through. That's such a small number. We need thousands of people to be AI fluent.

## Local AI Sightings

Coachella Valley Unified School District, in collaboration with Blue Zones Coachella, posted an Instagram flyer for a health and wellness academy. The flyer is clearly AI-generated — bright, colorful, families looking happy, all the usual tells. There's only one comment on the post: "An AI-generated flyer isn't very healthy for our environment or our communities." You got a point.

The Riverside County Transportation Commission agreed to finance the next phase of the Coachella Valley rail project — \$80 million to an engineering company to conduct environmental studies. When completed, the 144-mile Coachella Valley rail will connect Los Angeles and the Coachella Valley, making stops in Riverside and ending at Union Station. Where in the Coachella Valley is this train station going to be? If you know, let me know. I'm always trying to bring my AI tech colleagues from LA and the Bay Area out here to the desert, but they don't want to come. They come for Coachella Fest and relaxation, but they don't come here to work.

There's a local guy running Desert AI Solutions — AI-powered automation for modern businesses. His latest post: "That's an extra day and a half back in your schedule. Visit desertaisolutions.com to schedule your free consultation and find out how AI can save you time."

Award-winning dating coach Julie Spira was on local news yesterday talking about AI boyfriends and girlfriends. A Norton study on artificial intimacy found that 77% of online daters would consider dating an AI chatbot. That's a lot.

I tested OpenAI's new concierge feature on OpenTable. Asked it about Wally's Desert Turtle in Rancho Mirage: "Why is this so expensive?" It gave me the full breakdown — high-quality locally sourced ingredients, elegant ambiance, exceptional service, live entertainment. Then I asked who the manager is. It correctly identified Maddie, the founder's granddaughter. Asked about the chef. It said Alan Lopez. I don't know if that's true. Asked about the price of a bottle of Silver Oak. It punted: "Pricing information isn't published, but wines tend to be on the higher side. Check with the restaurant."

Tried the same thing with The Edge Steakhouse at the Ritz-Carlton. "Why is everything so expensive here?" It listed location, ingredient quality, service level, ambiance, then noted that steaks often start around \$140. Asked who the manager is. It said Daniel Rodriguez. I have no idea if that's right.

## Something Big Is Happening

Michael Eisner, former CEO of Disney for nearly 20 years, said this week he's not wild about today's prices at Disneyland and Walt Disney World. Tiered pricing has made it harder than ever for every guest to feel special. Single-day tickets can reach \$224 during peak periods — nearly four times the \$56 admission price when Eisner left. Line-skipping options like Lightning Lane and Premier Pass run \$300 to \$400 per person per day. "The guests who pay more wait less," Eisner said, "creating a visible stratification in what once was a more egalitarian experience."

He was quoted saying, "We loved to entertain kings and queens, but the vital thing to remember is this: every guest receives the VIP treatment." That's the old model. It's gone now. Two Americas at Disneyland. Two Americas everywhere else.

An essay titled "Something Big Is Happening" went viral this week — 80 million views. The thesis: we're heading toward two Americas. Not red and blue. Those who know how to use AI and those who don't.
