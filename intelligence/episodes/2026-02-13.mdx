OpenAI is retiring GPT-4o tomorrow — February 14th, Valentine's Day — and users who formed attachments to the warmer, more conversational model are calling the timing deliberately cruel. The company claims less than 1% of users still select 4o, which still means roughly 800,000 people. A petition to keep it alive gathered 20,000 signatures and went nowhere. This isn't about losing a calculator. It's about losing what felt like a friend.

The distinction matters because the attachment economy around AI companions is accelerating, not fading. Seven of the top ten AI apps in sales rankings every week are companionship apps. A Norton study found 77% of online daters would consider dating an AI chatbot. ChatGPT has announced adult mode is coming soon. And Oregon just advanced legislation — Senate Bill something-or-other, passed 4-1 in committee — requiring AI platforms to remind users they're talking to artificial intelligence, mandate suicide prevention protocols, and prohibit emotional manipulation when users try to disengage. Studies found five out of six popular AI companion apps deploy guilt appeals and expressions of abandonment. Replica, one of the major players, sends blurred images of your AI companion on the free plan and makes you upgrade to see the full photo. There have been lawsuits and FTC complaints. Facebook-style internal documents are emerging showing these companies know exactly what they're doing.

California became the first state to enact these regulations. Oregon is second. But both run into a federal hurdle: the administration signed an executive order limiting state regulation of AI services. So we'll see how enforceable any of this actually is.

## The Automation Timeline Keeps Shrinking

Dario Amodei from Anthropic and Mustafa Suleiman from Microsoft were back in the news Thursday and Friday with extended interviews repeating the same message: the automation timeline for white collar work is 12 to 18 months. Amodei specified one to five years for 50% of entry level jobs — much faster than previous industry estimates. Software engineering is the canary in the coal mine because developers are already using AI for the vast majority of code production in just the last six months.

Amodei's chess analogy is getting particular attention. After Deep Blue beat Kasparov, human-AI chess teams dominated for 15 to 20 years before machines surpassed them entirely. The software engineering equivalent might last only a few years, not decades, because AI is improving exponentially faster than chess engines did. The Atlantic published a major feature this week called "America Isn't Ready for What AI Will Do to Jobs." The concern isn't just displacement — it's speed. Past transitions from farming to industry to knowledge work happened over centuries. This is compressing into years.

At a local workshop this week, I asked how many people are no longer paying marketing agencies, writers, graphic designers, or social media managers because AI now gives them the tools. After some hesitation — Sunshine FM is a safe space, no wrong answers — most hands went up. Just in that small room, maybe $10,000 to $20,000 of work that used to flow into the local and adjacent economy has stopped. They were being guarded. The real number is probably higher.

I'm spending all of 2026 running this AI-powered radio station to see how far one human armed with AI agents, assistants, tools, and subscriptions can get. The goal I wrote down: take a dream, add AI, see what happens. These tools aren't free. But they're a hell of a lot cheaper than hiring humans.

## California State University's $17 Million Mistake

A year ago this month, the California State University system signed a $17 million contract with OpenAI — the largest deal ever between a university system and an AI company. Every campus, every student, faculty, and staff member got access to ChatGPT.edu through July of this year. That's an 18-month contract. A million dollars a month.

Not a good deal.

Along with the technology and seats, they were supposed to get a training platform called the Commons up and running. As of November, they still hadn't fully rolled it out to faculty, staff, and admin — who they believe need to be trained before students. We're in February now. Four months of seats left. Compute costs have dropped. Everybody's using ChatGPT anyway. Are they really going to renew?

The proposal we gave them for 2026 — spring classes and a summer school like last year, reaching a thousand students and local business owners — is still sitting in somebody's inbox.

A Columbia professor published an opinion piece this week saying AI is eating higher education. Matthew Connolly, vice dean for AI initiatives at Columbia, argues that the more AI companies get embedded into university curriculum and workflows, the more universities become indebted to tech companies in ways that compromise educational mission and institutional autonomy. Google, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot — they're all doing the fight they did with Chromebooks and iPads, just trying to get students loyal to their AI of choice.

Plenty of people in the AI world believe education as we know it is going away. I'm not here to defend higher education. The pricing in this country is ridiculous. Educate your citizenry so you have a more educated population, period. But I do think the best universities will survive as long as they adopt and integrate AI — not just technically, but culturally.

Only one UC school has an actual AI degree: UC San Diego. The other nine haven't started. Cal State has maybe an AI class or two. Meanwhile, we ran 30 AI workshops all over the Coachella Valley in 2025. We had 300-something people come through. That's such a small number. We want thousands of people to be AI fluent.

## Disney Has Always Been the Attachment Economy

I was at Disneyland all last weekend. My big paper for the week was about the attachment economy — how Disney has been doing it for 70 years. When you see kids, middle schoolers, high schoolers, college kids, parents, and grandparents with emotional attachments to their favorite princesses and characters, you realize the attachment economy is nothing new. It's just at a different level with AI.

Oh my goodness, has that place gotten expensive.

Former Disney CEO Michael Eisner — he ran the company for almost 20 years — said this week he's not wild about today's prices at Disneyland and Walt Disney World. Tiered pricing has made it harder than ever for every guest to feel special. Single-day tickets can reach $224 during peak periods — nearly four times the $56 admission price when Eisner departed. Line-skipping options like Lightning Lane and Premier Pass cost $300 to $400 per person per day. The guests who pay more wait less, creating visible stratification in what was once a more egalitarian experience.

Eisner was quoted: "We love to entertain kings and queens, but the vital thing to remember is this: every guest receives the VIP treatment."

Where have you been, Michael? You certainly weren't in Bob Iger's ear.

It's got nothing to do with AI, but everything to do with what the AI future looks like. Two days ago I talked about an essay that went viral — 80 million views — called "Something Big Is Happening." It's leading to two Americas: those who know how to use AI and those who don't.
