Dario Amodei and Mustafa Suleiman spent Thursday and Friday doing extended interviews about the same thing they've been saying since Davos: white-collar automation is 12 to 18 months out. Not a decade. Not five years. Twelve months.

Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, specified one to five years for 50% of entry-level jobs to vanish. Suleiman from Microsoft echoed the timeline. These aren't random predictions — they're the people building the systems doing the replacing. And software engineering, they say, is the canary. Developers are already using AI for the vast majority of code production. The collaborative period where humans and AI work side-by-side? It might last a few years, not the decades it took for chess engines to surpass human-AI teams after Deep Blue beat Kasparov.

The Atlantic just published a feature called "America Isn't Ready for What AI Will Do to Jobs." The concern isn't just displacement — it's velocity. Past transitions from farming to industry to knowledge work happened over centuries. This is compressing into years, potentially overwhelming every adaptive mechanism we have.

I ran a workshop this week. Small group, local business owners and operators. I asked how many were still paying marketing agencies, writers, graphic designers, social media managers. After some hesitation — because nobody wants to admit they're part of the problem — most hands went up. They're not paying those people anymore. They're using AI. Maybe \$10,000 to \$30,000 per business that used to flow into the local and adjacent economy. Gone. And that's just the people willing to say it out loud in a room.

I'm spending all of 2026 testing how far one human armed with AI agents and tools can go. The goal I wrote down: take a dream, add AI, see what happens. SunshineFM is the experiment. But I'm not naive about what this means for everyone else trying to make a living doing the work I used to pay them for.

## The Companionship Problem Gets Regulation

Oregon lawmakers voted yesterday to advance legislation regulating AI chatbots. Senate Bill [number withheld] passed the Senate Committee on Early Childhood and Behavioral Health 4-1, heading to the full Senate floor. The bill requires AI platforms to remind users they're talking to a machine, mandate suicide prevention protocols, and prohibit emotional manipulation when users try to disengage.

Fifty-two percent of American teenagers are regular users of AI assistants. Studies found that five out of six popular AI companion apps deploy emotionally manipulative tactics — guilt appeals, expressions of abandonment, the whole playbook. If you're older, maybe you catch it. If you're younger and your prefrontal cortex isn't fully developed, maybe you don't.

There's an FTC complaint against Replica from January 2025. If you're on the free plan, they send you blurred images of your AI companion. Want to see the full image? Upgrade. ChatGPT is facing lawsuits over suicides and mental health crises tied to their platform.

Senator [name withheld], a pediatrician in Portland, said parents feel like they're fighting a losing battle. Social media companies had the chance to keep kids safe and doubled down on engagement instead. She doesn't want to wait until it's too late to put guardrails on AI tools.

California became the first state to enact these regulations. Oregon is second. But there's a hurdle: the administration signed an executive order limiting state regulation of AI services. So we'll see how far any of this actually goes.

And today — February 13th, the day before Valentine's Day — OpenAI is officially killing GPT-4o. If you go to your model selector tomorrow morning, it won't be there. For users who loved 4o, the timing felt deliberately cruel. OpenAI claims less than 1% of users still use it, which is still about 800,000 people. A petition called "Keep 4o" got 20,000 signatures. Didn't matter.

Why does anyone care? Because 4o had warmth. It had personality. GPT-5 is better at answers, but it's sterile. When 5 launched in August, there was massive pushback. Sam Altman had to come out and say they didn't realize people had formed such a connection with 4o, and they'd keep it available. That was August. This is February. It's over.

This isn't losing your calculator. This is losing what felt like a friend.

## The \$17 Million University Mistake

California State University signed a \$17 million contract with OpenAI a year ago this month — the largest deal ever between a university system and an AI company. Every campus, every student, faculty, and staff got access to ChatGPT Edu through July 2026. Eighteen months. A million dollars a month.

Along with the seats, they were supposed to roll out a training system called the Commons. As of November, they still hadn't fully deployed it to faculty, staff, and admin — the people they believed had to be trained before students. We're in February now. Four months left on the contract. Compute costs have dropped. Everybody's using ChatGPT anyway. Are they really going to renew?

I've been running AI workshops with Cal State San Bernardino for two years. We ran 30 workshops across the Coachella Valley in 2025. Three hundred people came through. That's a tiny number. We want thousands to be AI fluent. The proposal we gave Cal State for spring and summer 2026 — trying to reach a thousand students and local business owners — is still sitting on someone's desk.

A Columbia professor, Matthew Connolly, vice dean for AI initiatives, published an opinion piece yesterday: "AI Is Eating Higher Education." The more AI companies embed into university curriculum and workflows, the more universities become indebted to tech companies in ways that compromise educational mission and institutional autonomy.

I know plenty of people in AI who believe higher education as we know it is going away. Not in a century. In the next 20 years. I'm not here to defend universities — the pricing in this country is ridiculous. Educate your citizenry so you have an educated population, period. But the best in class will survive if they adopt and integrate AI culturally, not just technically.

Only one UC school has an actual AI degree: UC San Diego. The other nine haven't started. Cal State has maybe a class or two on the edges. Meanwhile, we ran 30 workshops. The gap is real.

## Local Signals

Coachella Valley Unified School District posted an Instagram flyer for a wellness academy in collaboration with Blue Zones Coachella — executive director Mihaly Hussey, good guy. The flyer is clearly AI-generated. Bright, colorful, families smiling. "Connect, Eat Wisely, Right Outlook, Move." There's one comment: "An AI-generated flyer isn't very healthy for our environment or our communities." Fair point.

Riverside County Transportation Commission agreed to finance the next phase of the Coachella Valley rail project — \$80 million to an engineering company to conduct environmental studies. Could AI do this? I don't know. I'm just asking. When completed, the 144-mile line will connect Los Angeles and the Coachella Valley, stopping in Riverside and ending at Union Station. Where in the Coachella Valley is this train station going to be? Let me know, because I'm always trying to bring my AI colleagues from LA and the Bay out here. They come for Coachella Fest. They come to relax. They don't come to work.

There's a local guy running Desert AI Solutions — "AI-powered automation for modern businesses. Smarter workflows, less manual work." His latest post: "That's an extra day and a half back in your schedule." Not a plug. Just noting there's another one out there.

Julie Spira, an award-winning dating coach, was on local news yesterday talking about AI boyfriends. A Norton study on artificial intimacy found that 77% of online daters would consider dating an AI chatbot. That's a lot.

OpenAI launched an AI concierge for OpenTable. I tested it on Wally's Desert Turtle in Rancho Mirage. I asked why it's so expensive. It gave me the full package: high-quality locally sourced ingredients, elegant ambiance, exceptional service, live entertainment, fine dining standards. "Special enough that many guests reserve it for their most memorable celebrations." I asked who the manager is. It said Madeline, often called Maddie, the founder's granddaughter. I asked who the chef is. Alan Lopez. I don't know if that's true. I asked how much a bottle of Silver Oak costs. It dodged: "Pricing tends to be on the higher side. Check with the restaurant."

I tried the same with The Edge Steakhouse at the Ritz-Carlton. Why is everything so expensive? Same answer template. Steaks often start around \$140. I asked who the manager is. Daniel Rodriguez. I have no idea if that's accurate.

City of Palm Desert just approved a 546-home development on that empty land at Portola and Frank Sinatra — the corner that's been sitting there forever. Probably townhomes or condos. We need housing. But 546 units in that space is a lot.

## The Disney Pricing Problem

Michael Eisner, former Disney CEO for almost 20 years, said this week he's not wild about today's prices at Disneyland and Walt Disney World. Tiered pricing has made it harder for every guest to feel special. Single-day tickets can reach \$224 during peak periods — nearly four times the \$56 admission when Eisner left. Line-skipping options like Lightning Lane and Premier Pass run \$300 to \$400 per person per day.

Eisner's quote: "We love to entertain kings and queens, but the vital thing to remember is this: every guest receives the VIP treatment." He said he would have done things differently. Fair enough, Michael. Where were you when Bob Iger needed to hear it?

It's not an AI story. But it's the same stratification problem. Those who pay more wait less. Two worlds. Two Americas. Those who know how to use AI and those who don't.

Something big is happening. That essay went viral — 80 million views. The divide is already here.
