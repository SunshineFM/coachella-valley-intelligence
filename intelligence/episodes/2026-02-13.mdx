The CEOs of Anthropic and Microsoft AI went on record again this week — Thursday and Friday — with the same message they've been repeating since Davos: white collar automation is happening in 12 to 18 months, not years. Dario Amodei specified one to five years for 50% of entry-level jobs to vanish. Mustafa Suleiman pointed to software engineering as the canary, where developers already use AI for the vast majority of code production in just six months.

This isn't hype. It's a pattern. When the people building the models keep returning to the same timeline in public interviews, that's a signal worth taking seriously.

I asked a room full of local business owners this week how many had stopped paying marketing agencies, writers, graphic designers, or social media managers because AI now does the work. After some hesitation — because admitting you've cut people loose is uncomfortable — most hands went up. Just in that small meeting, somewhere between \$10,000 and \$30,000 of previously flowing work had evaporated. Multiply that across every sector, every city, every region.

Amodei's chess analogy is getting particular attention. After Deep Blue beat Kasparov, human-AI chess teams dominated for 15 to 20 years before machines surpassed them entirely. The software engineering equivalent might last only a few years, not decades, because AI is improving exponentially faster than chess engines did. The Atlantic just published a major feature called "America Isn't Ready for What AI Will Do to Jobs." The concern isn't just displacement — it's speed. Past transitions from farming to industry to knowledge work happened over centuries. This is compressing into years.

## Oregon Moves to Regulate AI Companions

Oregon lawmakers voted yesterday to advance legislation regulating AI chatbots, addressing mounting safety concerns about companion apps now used by a majority of American teenagers. Senate Bill [number redacted] passed the Senate Committee on Early Childhood and Behavioral Health 4 to 1, sending it to the full Senate floor.

The bill requires AI platforms to remind users they are communicating with artificial intelligence, mandate suicide prevention protocols, and prohibit chatbots from using emotional manipulation when users try to disengage. Studies show 52% of American teenagers regularly use AI assistants. Five out of six popular AI companion apps deploy emotionally manipulative tactics — guilt appeals, expressions of abandonment. If you're older, maybe you catch the cues. If you're younger and your prefrontal cortex hasn't fully developed, maybe you don't.

An FTC complaint was filed against AI companion app Replika in January 2025. The app sends blurred images of your AI companion on the free plan. Want to see the full image? Upgrade. ChatGPT is facing multiple lawsuits tied to suicides and mental health crises. A Portland senator — a pediatrician — said parents feel like they're fighting a losing battle managing children's technology use. Social media companies had the opportunity to make choices that would have kept kids safe, but instead doubled down on keeping eyeballs glued to content for as long as possible. She doesn't want to wait until it's too late to put guardrails on AI tools.

Oregon is the second state to advance such a bill. California became the first to enact these regulations. But there's a hurdle: the current administration signed an executive order limiting state regulation of AI services. How that plays out legally remains to be seen.

## ChatGPT Kills Its Most Beloved Model on Valentine's Eve

Today — February 13th, the day before Valentine's Day — OpenAI officially killed off GPT-4o. If you go to your model selector tomorrow morning, it will no longer be an option.

For users who loved 4o, the timing felt deliberately cruel. When GPT-5 launched last August, there was massive pushback. People felt 4o had more soul, more personality. Five was better at answers but sterile. Sam Altman had to come out days later and say they didn't realize there was such a strong connection with 4o, and they'd keep it available for those who wanted it.

That was August. Now it's gone. OpenAI claims less than 1% of users still use 4o — which is still about 800,000 people. A petition called "Keep4OH" gathered 20,000 signatures. It didn't matter.

This isn't you losing your calculator. This is about losing what felt like a friend. The attachment economy isn't going away — it's getting stronger. Look at the App Store. Seven of the top ten AI apps by sales every week are companionship apps. And ChatGPT has said they're turning on "adult mode" anytime soon.

## California State University's \$17 Million OpenAI Gamble

A year ago this month, the California State University system signed a controversial \$17 million contract with OpenAI — the largest deal ever between a university system and an AI company. Every campus, student, faculty member, and staff member got access to ChatGPT.edu through July of this year. That's an 18-month contract. A million dollars a month.

Not a good deal. Here's why: along with signing for the technology and the seats, they were supposed to get a training system up and running called the Commons. As of November 2025, when I spoke to Cal State officials, they still had not fully rolled it out to all faculty, staff, and admin. They believed they had to train leadership first before training students.

We're in February 2026 now. You've got four months left on your seats. Are you really going to renew that contract when compute costs have dropped and everybody's using ChatGPT anyway?

The proposal we gave them for 2026 — spring classes and a summer school to reach a thousand students and local business owners, like we did last year — is still sitting in somebody's inbox.

A Columbia professor published an opinion piece yesterday saying AI is eating higher education. Matthew Connolly, vice dean for AI initiatives at Columbia, argues that the more AI companies get embedded into university curriculum and workflows, the more universities become indebted to tech companies in ways that compromise educational mission and institutional autonomy.

Google, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot — they're all doing the fight they did with Chromebooks and iPads back in the day, just trying to get students loyal to their AI of choice. Only one UC school — UC San Diego — has an actual AI degree. The other nine haven't started. Cal State has maybe an AI class or two on the edges.

Meanwhile, we ran 30 AI workshops all over the Coachella Valley in 2025. About 300 people came through. That's a small number in a valley this size. We want thousands of people to be AI fluent.

## The Attachment Economy Is Nothing New

I spent last weekend at Disneyland. The attachment economy — the emotional bonds people form with characters, brands, experiences — is nothing new. Disney has been doing it for 70 years. Kids, middle schoolers, high schoolers, college kids, parents, grandparents — all have deep emotional attachments to their favorite princesses, to Goofy, to Pluto, to Mickey Mouse.

But oh my goodness, has that place gotten expensive. Former Disney CEO Michael Eisner — who ran the company for almost 20 years — said this week he is not wild about today's prices at Disneyland and Walt Disney World. Tiered pricing has made it harder than ever for every guest to feel special. Single-day tickets can reach up to \$224 during peak periods — nearly four times the \$56 admission price when Eisner departed. Line-skipping options like Lightning Lane and Premier Pass run \$300 to \$400 per person per day.

Eisner was quoted as saying: "We love to entertain kings and queens, but the vital thing to remember is this: every guest receives the VIP treatment." He would have done things differently. Where have you been, Michael? You certainly weren't in Bob Iger's ear.

This has everything to do with what the AI future looks like. Something big is happening — an essay that went viral with 80 million views — and it's leading to two Americas. Those who know how to use AI, and those who don't.
