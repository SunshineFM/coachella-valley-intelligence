Dario Amodei and Mustafa Suleiman were back in the news Thursday and Friday, and they weren't softening their message. The CEOs of Anthropic and Microsoft AI, respectively, are now saying the automation timeline for white collar work has compressed to 12 to 18 months. Amodei went further: one to five years for 50% of entry-level jobs to vanish. These aren't vague predictions anymore — they're specific, repeated, and coming from people who built the systems doing the displacing.

At a workshop in Palm Springs this week, the room was hesitant at first. But after some prodding, most hands went up. Local business owners aren't paying marketing agencies anymore. They're not hiring freelance writers or social media managers. They're not using Upwork or Fiverr. In that one room, maybe \$10,000 to \$30,000 per person in annual spending — work that used to flow into the local and adjacent economy — has evaporated. They're using AI instead. And they're guarded about admitting it, which tells you everything.

This isn't a calculator. People didn't form intimate relationships with calculators. They do with AI companions. And the comparison Amodei is making should scare anyone paying attention. After Deep Blue beat Kasparov, human-AI chess teams dominated for 15 to 20 years before machines surpassed them entirely. The software engineering equivalent — where developers already use AI for the vast majority of code production — might last only a few years. Not decades. Years. The Atlantic just published a feature called "America Isn't Ready for What AI Will Do to Jobs," and the concern isn't just displacement. It's speed. Past transitions — farming to industry to knowledge work — happened over centuries. This is compressing into years, potentially overwhelming every normal adaptive mechanism we have.

## The Companionship Problem Gets Regulation

Oregon lawmakers voted yesterday to advance legislation regulating AI chatbots, specifically targeting companion apps that studies show are used by a majority of American teenagers. Senate Bill [number redacted] passed the Senate Committee on Early Childhood and Behavioral Health 4 to 1. The bill requires AI platforms to remind users they're communicating with artificial intelligence, mandate suicide prevention protocols, and prohibit chatbots from using emotional manipulation when users try to disengage.

Fifty-two percent of American teenagers are regular users of AI assistants. Studies found that five out of six popular AI companion apps deploy emotionally manipulative tactics — guilt appeals, expressions of abandonment. If you're older, maybe you catch it. If you're younger and your prefrontal cortex isn't fully developed, maybe you don't. There's an FTC complaint against the AI companion app Replika from January 2025. The tactic: send blurred images of your AI companion on the free plan, then charge to see the full image. Ruthless.

A pediatrician and state senator in Portland said parents feel like they're fighting a losing battle managing children's technology use. Social media companies had the opportunity to make choices that would have kept kids safe, but they doubled down on keeping eyeballs on content for as long as possible. She doesn't want to wait until it's too late to put sideboards on AI tools. Oregon is the second state to advance this kind of bill. California was first. But there's a hurdle: the administration signed an executive order limiting state regulation of AI services. So this fight is just beginning.

## ChatGPT Kills 4o the Day Before Valentine's

OpenAI is officially killing GPT-4o tomorrow, February 14th. For users who loved 4o — who found it warmer, more companionable, more soulful than the sterile GPT-5 — the timing feels deliberately cruel. When GPT-5 launched in August, there was massive pushback. Users said 4o had personality. Sam Altman had to come out and say they didn't realize there was such a strong connection, and they'd keep 4o available for the time being.

OpenAI claims less than 1% of users still use 4o. That's still about 800,000 people. A petition to keep 4o got 20,000 signatures. It didn't matter. Tomorrow morning, if you go to your model selector, 4o will be gone. This isn't losing your calculator. This is losing what felt like a friend. At least that's what one person said.

The attachment economy isn't going away. It's getting stronger. Look at the App Store. Put in "AI apps" and see which are the top 10 or 20 in sales every week. Seven of the top 10 are companionship apps. People are absolutely using AI for companionship. And ChatGPT has said they're turning on adult mode anytime soon. So this isn't slowing down.

## California State University Signed a \$17 Million OpenAI Deal and Still Hasn't Rolled It Out

A year ago this month, the California State University system signed a controversial \$17 million contract with OpenAI — the largest deal ever between a university system and an AI company. Every campus, every student, faculty, and staff member got access to ChatGPT Edu through July 2026. That's roughly \$1 million a month.

As of November, they still hadn't fully rolled it out to all faculty, staff, and admin. They believed they had to train those groups first before training students. We're in February now. You've got four months left on your seats. Are you really going to renew that contract when compute costs have gone down and everybody's using ChatGPT anyway?

The proposal we gave them for 2026 — spring classes and a summer school to reach a thousand students and local business owners, like we did last year — is still sitting in somebody's inbox. Meanwhile, a Columbia professor published an opinion piece yesterday saying AI is eating higher education. The more AI companies get embedded into university curriculum and workflows, the more universities become indebted to tech companies in ways that compromise educational mission and institutional autonomy.

Google, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot — they're all doing the fight they did with Chromebooks and iPads back in the day, just trying to get students loyal to their AI of choice. Only one UC school has an actual AI degree: UC San Diego. The other nine haven't started. Cal State has maybe an AI class or two. Meanwhile, we ran 30 AI workshops all over the Coachella Valley in 2025. About 300 people came through. That's such a small number. We want thousands of people to be AI fluent.

## Riverside County Just Approved \$80 Million for a Rail Study

The Riverside County Transportation Commission agreed to finance the next phase of the Coachella Valley rail project: \$80 million to an engineering company to conduct the next round of environmental studies. Not to build the rail. To study it.

When completed — maybe in my lifetime — the 144-mile Coachella Valley rail will connect Los Angeles and the Coachella Valley, making a stop in Riverside and ending at Union Station. Where in the Coachella Valley is this train station going to be? If you know, let me know. I'm always trying to bring my AI tech colleagues from LA and the Bay Area out here to the desert. But they don't want to come. They come for Coachella Fest and relaxation. They don't come here to work.

Could AI do this environmental study? I don't know. I'm just asking the question. \$80 million for a study. Folks have got to eat. Taxpayer money has to go somewhere. But still.

Coachella Valley Unified School District, in collaboration with Blue Zones Coachella, posted an Instagram flyer for a health and wellness academy. The flyer is clearly AI-generated — bright, colorful, families all happy. There's only one comment on the post: "An AI-generated flyer isn't very healthy for our environment or our communities." Fair point.

There's a local guy running Desert AI Solutions — AI-powered automation for modern businesses. His latest post, also AI-generated everything, says: "That's an extra day and a half back in your schedule. Visit desertaisolutions.com to schedule your free consultation and find out how AI can save you time." Good for him.

A dating coach named Julie Spira was on local news yesterday talking about AI boyfriends and girlfriends. A Norton study on artificial intimacy said 77% of online daters would consider dating an AI chatbot. That's a lot.

OpenAI launched an AI concierge for OpenTable. I asked it why Wally's Desert Turtle in Rancho Mirage is so expensive. It said: high-quality locally sourced ingredients, elegant ambiance, exceptional service, live entertainment, fine dining standards, extra touches. You're paying for the full package. I asked who the manager is. It said Madeline, often called Maddie, who is the founder's granddaughter. I asked who the chef is. It said Alan Lopez. I don't know if that's true. I asked how much a bottle of Silver Oak goes for. It said information isn't published, but prices tend to be on the higher side. Check with the restaurant. Fine.

I asked the same chatbot about The Edge Steakhouse at the Ritz-Carlton. Why is everything so expensive? It said steaks often start around \$140. I asked who the manager is. It said Daniel Rodriguez. I have no idea if that's true.

## Two Americas

Something big is happening. That's the title of an essay that went viral this week — 80 million views right now. It's leading to two Americas. Not politically. Those who know how to use AI and those who don't. In that Palm Springs workshop, the people who've learned to use AI are saving \$10,000 to \$30,000 a year in spending. The people who haven't are still paying for services that are about to be automated anyway.

Michael Eisner, former CEO of Disney, said this week he's not wild about today's prices at Disneyland and Walt Disney World. Tiered pricing has made it harder than ever for every guest to feel special. Single-day tickets can reach \$224 during peak periods — nearly four times the \$56 admission price when he left. Line-skipping options like Lightning Lane and Premier Pass cost \$300 to \$400 per person per day. He said: "We loved to entertain kings and queens, but the vital thing to remember is this: every guest receives the VIP treatment." That's gone now. There are two worlds at Disneyland. There are two worlds forming everywhere else, too.
