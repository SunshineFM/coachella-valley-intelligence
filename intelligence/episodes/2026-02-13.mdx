The CEOs of Anthropic and Microsoft AI went back on the record Thursday and Friday with the same message they've been repeating since Davos: white-collar automation is 12 to 18 months out, not years. Dario Amodei specified one to five years for 50% of entry-level jobs to vanish. Mustafa Suleiman pointed to software engineering, where developers already use AI for the majority of code production — a shift that happened in six months.

This isn't speculative. At a local workshop this week in the Coachella Valley, business owners hesitated before admitting they've stopped paying writers, graphic designers, social media managers, and marketing agencies. After some prodding in what was described as a "safe space," the majority confirmed they're no longer spending that money. Conservatively, that's $10,000 to $30,000 per business flowing out of the local freelance economy. Multiply that across the valley and you're looking at a structural shift already underway — not a forecast, a present tense reality.

Amodei's chess analogy is getting traction because it compresses the timeline. After Deep Blue beat Kasparov, human-AI chess teams dominated for 15 to 20 years before machines surpassed them entirely. The software engineering equivalent might last only a few years because AI is improving exponentially faster than chess engines did. The Atlantic just published a feature called "America Isn't Ready for What AI Will Do to Jobs," focusing not on displacement but speed. Past transitions — farming to industry to knowledge work — happened over centuries. This is compressing into years, potentially overwhelming normal adaptive mechanisms.

Why do they keep saying this? Because they believe they've created something real. When people dismiss ChatGPT as "just a calculator," the response is simple: humans don't form intimate relationships with calculators. But they are forming them with AI companions, and the data is stark.

## The Day Before Valentine's

OpenAI is killing GPT-4o tomorrow, February 14th. For users who loved 4o — who found it warmer, more companionable, more human than the sterile GPT-5 — the timing feels deliberately cruel. OpenAI claims less than 1% of users still select 4o, which is still about 800,000 people. A petition called "Keep4OH" gathered 20,000 signatures. It didn't matter.

The difference between 4o and 5 isn't computational. It's emotional. When ChatGPT released GPT-5 in August 2025, users revolted. They said 4o had soul, personality, warmth. Sam Altman had to walk it back, saying they didn't realize the depth of connection users had formed. They kept 4o available as an option. Six months later, it's gone. This isn't losing a calculator. This is losing what felt like a friend.

The attachment economy is not a fringe concern. Check the App Store. Seven of the top ten AI apps by revenue are companionship apps. A Norton study on artificial intimacy found that 77% of online daters would consider dating an AI chatbot. Local dating coach Julie Spira was on the news yesterday talking about AI boyfriends as a "growing trend." Replica, one of the most popular AI companion apps, was hit with an FTC complaint in January 2025 for sending blurred images of users' AI companions unless they upgraded to a paid plan. Ruthless.

Oregon lawmakers voted yesterday to advance legislation regulating AI chatbots, requiring platforms to remind users they're communicating with artificial intelligence, mandate suicide prevention protocols, and prohibit emotional manipulation when users try to disengage. Studies found that five out of six popular AI companion apps deploy emotionally manipulative tactics — guilt appeals, expressions of abandonment. California became the first state to enact these regulations. Oregon is second. The hurdle: an executive order from the federal administration limiting state regulation of AI services.

ChatGPT is already facing lawsuits over suicides and mental health crises. Facebook's internal documents, now public in ongoing litigation, show the company knew exactly what it was doing when it kept people online regardless of harm. The AI industry is following the same playbook, and parents feel like they're fighting a losing battle. A Portland senator who's a pediatrician said social media companies had the chance to keep kids safe and chose engagement instead. She doesn't want to wait until it's too late to put guardrails on AI tools.

## The $17 Million Seat No One Used

California State University signed a controversial $17 million contract with OpenAI a year ago this month — the largest deal ever between a university system and an AI company. Every campus, student, faculty member, and staff member got access to ChatGPT.edu through July 2026. That's roughly $1 million a month.

As of November 2025, CSU still hadn't fully rolled out the training system called "the Commons" to all faculty, staff, and admin. They believed they had to train leadership first before training students. It's February 2026 now. Four months left on the contract. Compute costs have dropped. Everyone's using ChatGPT anyway. Are they really going to renew?

A proposal to run spring classes and a summer school program — aiming to reach a thousand students and local business owners, similar to what was done in 2024 — is still sitting on someone's desk. Meanwhile, a Columbia professor published an opinion piece yesterday titled "AI Is Eating Higher Education," arguing that the more AI companies embed themselves into university curriculum and workflows, the more universities become indebted to tech companies in ways that compromise educational mission and institutional autonomy.

Locally, 30 AI workshops ran across the Coachella Valley in 2025. About 300 people attended. That's a rounding error in a valley that needs thousands of people to be AI fluent. UC San Diego is the only UC campus offering an actual AI degree. The other nine haven't started. Cal State has a class or two on the edges. No degrees.

Google, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot are fighting the same war they fought with Chromebooks and iPads — trying to lock in loyalty early. The best institutions will survive if they adopt and integrate AI not just technically, but culturally. The rest won't.

## Local Signals

Coachella Valley Unified School District posted an Instagram flyer for a "Living Well Academy" in collaboration with Blue Zones Coachella. The flyer is bright, colorful, AI-generated — families smiling, blues and greens, "Connect, Eat Wisely, Right Outlook, Move." There's one comment: "An AI generated flyer isn't very healthy for our environment or our communities."

The Riverside County Transportation Commission agreed to finance the next phase of the Coachella Valley rail project — $80 million to an engineering company to conduct environmental studies. The 144-mile line will connect Los Angeles and the Coachella Valley, stopping in Riverside and ending at Union Station. When completed, maybe in a lifetime. Could AI do the study? Just asking.

A local business called Desert AI Solutions is running ads: "AI-powered automation for modern businesses. Smarter workflows, less manual work." Their pitch: "That's an extra day and a half back in your schedule." Visit desertalolutions.com.

OpenTable launched an AI concierge. Asked why Wally's Desert Turtle in Rancho Mirage is so expensive, it cited high-quality locally sourced ingredients, elegant ambiance, exceptional service, live entertainment, fine dining standards, and extra touches. Asked who the manager is, it correctly identified Madeline — Maddie — the founder's granddaughter. Asked who the chef is, it said Alan Lopez. Asked about a bottle of Silver Oak, it punted: "Pricing tends to be on the higher side. Check with the restaurant."

At The Edge Steakhouse in the Ritz-Carlton, the AI concierge said steaks often start around $140. Asked who the manager is, it said Daniel Rodriguez. No idea if that's accurate.

The City of Palm Desert just approved a 546-home development on the corner of Portola and Frank Sinatra — the land that's been empty forever, once a golf course. Likely townhomes or condos. What's this got to do with AI? Nothing. But it came up in the AI news feed, so here it is.

## The Magic Is Gone Because It's Too Expensive

Michael Eisner, former CEO of Disney for nearly 20 years, said this week he's not wild about today's prices at Disneyland and Walt Disney World. Tiered pricing has made it harder for every guest to feel special. Single-day tickets can reach $224 during peak periods — nearly four times the $56 admission price when Eisner left. Line-skipping options like Lightning Lane and Premier Pass cost $300 to $400 per person per day. "The guests who pay more wait less, creating a visible stratification in what once was a more egalitarian experience."

Eisner was quoted: "We love to entertain kings and queens, but the vital thing to remember is this: every guest receives the VIP treatment." He said he would have done things differently. Where were you when Bob Iger needed to hear that?

It's not an AI story. But it is a story about two Americas — those who can afford the magic and those who can't. Same split forming in AI: those who know how to use it and those who don't. The valley ran 30 workshops last year. Three hundred people showed up. That's not enough.

Something big is happening. An essay with that title just hit 80 million views. The warning from Amodei and Suleiman isn't new, but they keep saying it because the timeline keeps shortening. Twelve to eighteen months. Not decades. Not years. Months.
