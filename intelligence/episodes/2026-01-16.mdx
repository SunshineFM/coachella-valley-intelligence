Trump put tariffs back on the table this week, and the numbers are big enough to matter here. Twenty-five percent on all imports from Mexico and Canada. Ten percent on everything from China. Effective February 1st. That's not a negotiating position anymore — that's policy with a start date.

If you're building anything in the Coachella Valley right now, your steel costs just became a variable you can't model. If you're running a hotel and your linens come through a Mexican distributor, your margins just got thinner. If you're in manufacturing and your components cross the border twice before final assembly, you're about to spend the next two weeks with your accountant trying to figure out which costs you eat and which ones you pass through.

The stated justification is fentanyl and immigration enforcement. The mechanism is a blanket tariff structure that doesn't distinguish between a truckload of precursor chemicals and a shipment of rebar. It's a blunt instrument, and blunt instruments don't care about your project timeline.

## What This Means for Desert Construction

Palm Springs Coachella has been in a building cycle. Residential, hospitality, infrastructure — all of it requires materials that move freely across borders under existing trade frameworks. Steel from Mexico. Lumber from Canada. Fixtures and finishes that touch Chinese manufacturing at some point in the supply chain.

A 25% tariff isn't a rounding error. It's a cost structure reset. Contractors who bid jobs in December are now looking at February deliveries that might blow their numbers apart. Developers who penciled projects at certain material costs now have to decide whether to eat the difference, renegotiate, or pause.

And it's not just direct imports. It's the second-order effects. A tariff on Canadian softwood lumber doesn't just hit the lumber — it hits everything built with lumber, everything adjacent to lumber, every supplier who has to rejustify their pricing because their competitors are all recalculating at the same time.

Local builders should be stress-testing their current contracts and having very specific conversations with suppliers about what's locked in and what isn't. If your vendor says "we'll figure it out," that's not an answer. You need to know whether your price is your price or whether it floats with tariff exposure.

## The Hospitality Sector Isn't Insulated

Hotels here run on thin margins and complex supply chains. Linens, furniture, kitchen equipment, cleaning supplies — a lot of it comes from or through Mexico. Some of it originates in China and gets distributed through North American networks.

A 10% or 25% cost increase doesn't always mean a 10% or 25% price increase to the end customer, because hospitality pricing is demand-driven and competitive. Which means operators absorb it, or they find cheaper substitutes, or they stretch replacement cycles and run things a little longer than they planned.

None of those options are great. The desert's hospitality economy depends on maintaining a certain level of finish and experience. If tariffs push operators toward cheaper goods or deferred maintenance, that shows up in guest experience eventually. And in a market where boutique properties and high-touch service are differentiators, that's a risk worth naming.

## Trade War Mechanics and Desert Agriculture

The Coachella Valley grows food. A lot of it goes to Mexico, either directly or as part of integrated supply chains. If the U.S. puts 25% tariffs on Mexican goods, Mexico has historically responded with retaliatory tariffs on U.S. agricultural products.

That's not hypothetical. It happened in 2018. Mexican tariffs on U.S. produce hit growers hard, particularly in border-adjacent regions. If we're running that play again, desert agriculture is in the blast radius.

Growers here should be watching what Mexico signals in the next two weeks. If retaliatory tariffs come, the question becomes whether you can reroute product, whether domestic demand absorbs what you can't export, and whether your margins survive the transition. For farms operating on contract pricing, this could mean renegotiations or breaches, depending on how the contracts were written.

## The Broader Inflation Question

Tariffs are taxes on imports, and taxes on imports get passed through the economy as higher prices. The question isn't whether this creates inflation — it does. The question is how much, how fast, and where it shows up first.

For Palm Springs Coachella, the early signals will be in construction bids, in hospitality procurement costs, and in anything that requires cross-border logistics. If you're signing a contract in February, you're signing it in a different cost environment than you were in December.

The Fed has been trying to manage inflation down without tipping the economy into recession. A new tariff structure works against that. It adds cost pressure at a time when the Fed would prefer cost pressure to be easing. That might mean interest rates stay higher for longer, which means financing costs for development projects don't improve as fast as people were hoping.

## What to Watch

February 1st is the implementation date. Between now and then, watch for three things:

First, whether Mexico or Canada signal retaliatory measures. If they do, this gets more expensive fast.

Second, whether any sectors get carve-outs or exemptions. Tariffs are policy, but policy has escape hatches if the political pressure is right. Construction and agriculture lobbies will be loud.

Third, how your own suppliers respond. If they're repricing, you need to know now. If they're holding prices temporarily, you need to know when that temporary period ends.

This isn't a story about Washington. It's a story about your costs in sixty days. The desert economy is more exposed to cross-border trade than it looks. We're not a border town, but we're close enough that border policy shows up in our spreadsheets.

If you're building, buying, or running a business with supply chain exposure, this is the week to model it out. February 1st is close.
