Coachella tickets went on sale and nobody rushed the gates. Lady Gaga, Green Day, Post Malone, Travis Scott — a headliner roster that would have caused server crashes five years ago is now sitting available days after the announcement. Resale prices are down 30% from last year. General admission is moving at \$549, but the secondary market tells the real story: people aren't convinced.

This matters because Coachella has been the economic anchor story for the valley for two decades. Goldenvoice and the festival's boosters claim a \$1.2 billion annual impact on the region. That number gets repeated in every city council meeting, every tourism pitch, every argument for why we should tolerate the traffic and the strain on infrastructure. But if demand is softening — if the festival is becoming just another large event instead of a cultural pilgrimage — that math changes fast.

The lineup itself is safe. Lady Gaga is the only true stadium-level artist on the bill. Green Day is legacy rock for millennials who now have mortgages. Post Malone and Travis Scott are reliable but not revelatory. There's no Beyoncé moment here, no Daft Punk reunion, nothing that makes this feel like the place you have to be. The undercard has depth — Megan Thee Stallion, Charli XCX, Missy Elliott — but Coachella built its brand on curation and surprise, and this year feels like a Spotify algorithm made the picks.

The festival is also dealing with a structural problem: it expanded. Two weekends means double the supply, which means scarcity disappeared. When Coachella was one weekend and impossible to get into, it was worth the price. Now it's a product you can buy whenever you're ready, and products don't command the same premium as experiences.

For Palm Springs Coachella, the question is what happens if Coachella stops being the tentpole. The valley has spent years building around April. Hotels price their entire year based on festival weekends. Restaurants staff up. Short-term rental owners block out their calendars months in advance. If attendance drops or the festival loses its cultural heat, a lot of local business models need new assumptions.

## Housing Costs Are Accelerating Faster Than Wages

The median home price in Palm Springs hit \$719,000 in December, up 8.4% year-over-year. Rent for a one-bedroom apartment is now averaging \$1,850, which is a 12% increase from last year. Meanwhile, median household income in the city is around \$54,000. The math doesn't work.

The city is considering rent control measures, which will pass or fail based on whether people believe it will help or just reduce supply. Developers say it will freeze new construction. Tenant advocates say it's the only way to keep people from being priced out. Both are probably right.

What's happening is that Palm Springs became desirable to people with money from other places, and those people don't need local wages to afford local housing. Remote workers from LA and San Francisco, retirees cashing out of expensive markets, investors buying short-term rentals — they all set the price floor higher than the people who work here can reach. This isn't unique to Palm Springs, but it's happening faster here because the city is small and the housing stock is limited.

The city council is talking about workforce housing, inclusionary zoning, and incentives for affordable units. All of that takes years. In the meantime, service workers are commuting from Desert Hot Springs or Indio, and the people who make the city run can't afford to live in it.

## Elon Musk Is Now Running Federal Efficiency and It's Chaos

Elon Musk's Department of Government Efficiency — DOGE, because of course — is operational and already making cuts. The department isn't actually a department; it's an advisory body with no official authority, but it has Trump's backing, which means it has power. Musk is proposing \$2 trillion in federal budget cuts, which is roughly a third of discretionary spending. The targets include the IRS, the Department of Education, and various regulatory agencies.

The IRS is losing 20,000 employees, which means fewer audits, slower refunds, and less enforcement. That sounds good if you don't like the IRS, but it also means wealthy tax evaders get away with more while the agency focuses on easier cases. The Department of Education is being restructured, with funding for certain programs being redirected or eliminated. Musk's argument is that the federal government is bloated and inefficient, which is true, but cutting without strategy just breaks things in different ways.

For Palm Springs Coachella, the concern is what happens to federal funding that flows through state and local programs. Infrastructure grants, housing assistance, small business support — a lot of that comes from agencies Musk is targeting. If those programs get cut or delayed, cities like Palm Springs lose leverage to build the things they need.

## The Port Strike Is Back and Supply Chains Are Tightening

The International Longshoremen's Association is threatening another strike after talks with port operators broke down. The union wants a 77% wage increase over six years and guarantees that automation won't replace workers. Port operators say that's unsustainable and that automation is necessary to stay competitive with ports in Asia and Europe.

If the strike happens, it will hit the East and Gulf Coast ports, which handle about 43% of U.S. container traffic. That means delays on everything from electronics to groceries. West Coast ports will absorb some of the overflow, but they're already operating near capacity. Prices will go up. Delivery times will stretch. Retailers will run low on inventory.

For businesses in Palm Springs Coachella, this is a planning problem. If you're ordering products or materials, assume delays and order early. If you're in hospitality or retail, expect customers to be more price-sensitive as inflation ticks up again. Supply chain disruptions don't hit all at once — they compound over weeks, and by the time you notice, you're already behind.

## The Local Angle: What This Means for Building Here

Palm Springs Coachella is in a strange moment. The region has momentum — tourism is strong, real estate is hot, new businesses are opening. But the underlying systems are fragile. Coachella's economic impact might be overstated. Housing costs are pushing out the workforce. Federal funding is uncertain. Supply chains are unreliable.

The people who will succeed here are the ones who plan for volatility. That means not assuming Coachella will always be a goldmine. It means building housing that people who work here can actually afford. It means diversifying revenue streams and not depending on a single event or season. It means ordering supplies early and keeping cash reserves high.

The desert has always been a place for people who see opportunity where others see risk. That's still true. But the risks are real, and they're accelerating.
