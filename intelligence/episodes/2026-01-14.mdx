DeepSeek R1 cost $5.6 million to train. OpenAI's o1 cost somewhere in the low billions. Both models score within a few points of each other on the same benchmarks.

This is not a minor efficiency gain. This is the entire scaling law narrative — the one that said you need infinite compute, infinite capital, infinite energy infrastructure — getting run over by a team in Hangzhou that apparently just decided to be smarter about it.

The model uses 671 billion parameters but only activates 37 billion per token. It's a mixture-of-experts architecture, which isn't new, but DeepSeek made it work at frontier performance levels without burning a billion dollars on H100 clusters. They trained on cheaper hardware. They used better algorithms. They got 90% of the way there for 1% of the cost.

Silicon Valley has spent two years telling us that AGI is a capital game. That the winners will be whoever can raise the most, spend the most, build the biggest data centers. DeepSeek just said: or you could think harder about the architecture.

The implications for Palm Springs Coachella are direct. If you don't need a hundred million dollars and a private power plant to build competitive AI, then the next wave of AI companies doesn't have to be in San Francisco. It can be anywhere smart people want to live and work without paying $4,500 a month for a one-bedroom. It can be here.

## The Supreme Court Decided TikTok's Fate

The Supreme Court upheld the TikTok ban. Not the law that might lead to a ban — the actual ban. TikTok goes dark in the U.S. on January 19th unless ByteDance sells to a non-Chinese buyer or the government grants an extension.

This is the first time the U.S. has banned a major consumer platform on national security grounds. The court didn't buy the First Amendment argument. The government's case — that TikTok's data flows to Beijing and its algorithm can be manipulated by the CCP — was enough.

170 million Americans use TikTok. A lot of them are small business owners who built entire customer bases on the platform. A lot of them are in hospitality, retail, events — the kinds of businesses that drive Palm Springs Coachella. If TikTok goes dark on Sunday, they lose their primary discovery engine overnight.

The optimistic read: ByteDance sells U.S. TikTok to someone like Oracle or a consortium, the app stays live under new ownership, and everyone moves on. The pessimistic read: ByteDance refuses to sell, the app goes dark, and 170 million people suddenly need to figure out where to spend their attention. Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts are the obvious winners, but neither has TikTok's distribution model for small accounts.

For local businesses that never built an email list or an owned audience, this is a real problem.

## Elon Musk Is Now Worth More Than Costco

Elon Musk's net worth hit $433 billion. Costco's market cap is $430 billion.

One human is now worth more than a company that operates 890 warehouses, employs 300,000 people, and sells $250 billion worth of stuff every year. This is not normal. This is what happens when Tesla's stock becomes a proxy bet on Musk's entire empire — Tesla, SpaceX, Starlink, xAI, Neuralink, the Trump administration's efficiency commission, whatever he's building next.

Tesla's market cap is over $1.4 trillion despite the fact that it sells fewer cars than Toyota and has worse margins than it did two years ago. The stock isn't pricing in car sales. It's pricing in robotaxis, Optimus robots, energy storage, and the possibility that Musk's government role gives him structural advantages no other CEO has ever had.

The risk is obvious. If any part of that story breaks — if Full Self-Driving doesn't ship, if the robotaxi reveal disappoints again, if the Trump relationship sours — the stock craters and $433 billion becomes $250 billion in a week.

But for now, the market believes. And that belief is worth more than Costco.

## The Rest of the Landscape

A federal judge blocked the merger between Tapestry and Capri — the deal that would have put Coach, Kate Spade, Michael Kors, Versace, and Jimmy Choo under one roof. The FTC argued it would reduce competition in the "accessible luxury" handbag market. Tapestry and Capri both said they'd walk away from the deal rather than fight it. That's $8.5 billion in deal value gone because the government decided $300 handbags are their own antitrust category.

Microsoft is cutting 10% of its underperforming sales and marketing staff. Not a mass layoff. Not a restructuring. Just a performance-based cut of people who weren't hitting numbers. It's the kind of thing that happens quietly in January at every big company, but it's notable because Microsoft has been on a hiring spree for two years to support its AI build-out. If they're trimming now, it means they're not seeing the revenue growth they expected from Copilot and Azure AI yet.

Northvolt, the Swedish battery startup that was supposed to be Europe's answer to CATL and LG, filed for bankruptcy. They raised $15 billion. They built a gigafactory in Sweden. They had deals with BMW, Volkswagen, and Volvo. And they still couldn't make the unit economics work. The lesson: building batteries at scale is harder than raising money from people who want there to be a European battery champion.

## What This Means Here

DeepSeek matters because it breaks the idea that the next decade of technology is determined by whoever has the most capital. If you can train frontier models for single-digit millions, then the next wave of AI companies can start in places like Palm Springs Coachella — where cost of living is lower, where talent is underpriced, where you can actually think instead of spending 40 hours a week on Sand Hill Road.

TikTok matters because a lot of local businesses built their entire customer acquisition strategy on one platform, and that platform might disappear in five days. If you're running a hotel, a restaurant, an event space, a retail shop — and you haven't built an email list or an owned channel — this is the week to start.

And Musk's $433 billion matters because it shows how much the market is willing to believe in one person's ability to execute across six different moonshots at once. That works until it doesn't. But while it works, it warps everything around it — capital allocation, talent flows, government relationships, the definition of what's possible.

The question isn't whether these things are sustainable. The question is what you do while they're happening.
