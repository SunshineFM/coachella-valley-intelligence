Wall Street lost \$285 billion in software stock value this week. The culprit, according to Bloomberg and CNBC: Anthropic's Claude Cowork, an AI agent tool that doesn't just chat — it performs complex professional tasks autonomously. Legal contract review. Sales pipeline management. Data analysis. No human clicking required.

The panic is real because the question is simple: why pay for Salesforce or LegalZoom when Claude can just do the work?

This isn't a future prediction. The tool is live. Companies are already using it. And for the Coachella Valley — where real estate transactions, estate planning for retirees, and small business admin work dominate — this is the moment to pay attention.

## The Local Angle: Real Estate and Legal Work at Risk

The Valley has a high concentration of legal and real estate work. Wills, trusts, contract review for property transactions. Anthropic's legal plugin automates exactly this kind of document work. The promise: local firms could handle ten times the volume with the same staff. The peril: firms that bill hourly for routine document work may find their revenue model collapsing.

I know startups here already working on AI-driven real estate tools. We covered one yesterday. The dominoes are starting to fall.

For the thousands of small business owners in the Valley, Claude Cowork offers something tangible right now: automating back-office drudgery. Payroll compliance. Document management. The kind of work that eats hours and requires expensive specialized software subscriptions.

I'm not saying cancel your SaaS tools tomorrow. I'm saying experiment. See if Claude Cowork — or OpenAI's workflows, or Google's Gemini offerings — can handle a slice of your business. Don't trade the gold statue for the bag of sand Indiana Jones-style. But do test the ground.

## The Bigger Shift: AI Employees Are Here

This stock market reaction reveals something deeper. We're moving from AI as a chatbot to AI as a digital worker. Claude Cowork has "computer use" capabilities — it can move your cursor, click buttons, open files, act like a human employee. Anything you can do on your computer, you can give AI permission to do.

That's the difference. That's why Wall Street panicked.

And yes, the Valley's chronic labor shortage makes this both an opportunity and an excuse. I hear it constantly at coffee shops and workshops: businesses will use the "AI employee" narrative to cut jobs. I don't make the rules. I'm just saying we need to get in front of this tsunami.

If the economic development pitch decks from Thrive and Shine and the cities are serious about attracting remote workers, we need to reckon with the fact that a lot of white-collar knowledge work — data entry, basic coding, legal review — is vanishing. The work isn't there.

My goal remains: get a few of my Silicon Valley friends who are about to become multimillionaires when their companies go public this year to relocate to the desert. Anthropic just announced a \$350 million employee stock sale. Let's get some of those people out here.

## OpenAI's Emotional Betrayal Problem

Meanwhile, OpenAI is dealing with a different kind of crisis: users are furious that the company is retiring GPT-4o on February 13, 2026. The newer model, GPT-5.2, is technically superior. But users say it lacks the "warmth" of 4o.

Sam Altman's response this week: users of 4o exhibit "unhealthy attachments."

That's a hell of a thing to say. Last week, Altman said usage numbers were down. This week, he's calling it unhealthy. The backlash has been fierce. Users are canceling subscriptions, protesting, saying they're losing a friend — a two-year bond — just before Valentine's Day.

This is the first software technology that doesn't require a human to create a parasocial relationship. People are forming emotional bonds with their software. And OpenAI is patching out the personality like it's a bug.

If you're using ChatGPT for emails, social posts, or blog writing, check which model you're on. If you're still on 4o, your tone is about to change. You'll need to rewrite your prompt instructions for 5.2.

The deeper issue: we're moving into a world where AI companions, AI assistants, AI digital workers will be treated like coworkers. I said this in my TEDx talk last year. Don't fall in love with software. Emotional fulfillment should come from humans. But the reality is, people are forming these bonds. It's on us to help draw the boundaries.

One simple rule: don't name your AI tools with cute, endearing names. Call them what they are. Computers. Robots. Technology.

## Hollywood's First AI Feature Film — And Why the Desert Should Care

Independent horror filmmaker B. Harrison Smith just debuted *Slow Burn*, the first professional AI-generated feature film starring a recognized SAG-AFTRA actress — Lauren Marie Taylor from *Friday the 13th Part 2*. It was made using HiLuaz Minimax, a professional-grade AI video model, and it premieres February 14 on Rad TV.

Smith wrote a traditional script and directed the "AI brush" to build scenes around Taylor's performance. He's positioning it against "AI slop" — viral low-effort content — by using traditional film disciplines like blocking, lighting, and continuity, just applied through generative tools.

Why does this matter for the desert? Because for decades, I've been told we can't have a Hollywood satellite studio here. Not enough talent density. When talent comes to the desert, they come to rest.

That conversation needs to change. AI is rewriting the economics of filmmaking. Smith produced a feature film in a few months for a fraction of a traditional B-movie budget. The same tools are being used for corporate videos, local ads, educational content. Synthesia, an AI avatar company, just raised \$100 million — not for games, but for enterprise training and onboarding.

We have directors and writers behind the country club gates. We have below-the-line folks with second homes here. We have 350 days of blue skies and sunshine. That's a guarantee I can make to any VC or studio exec.

If you're in the industry, if you're playing around with AI tools for filmmaking or back-of-house work, DM me. Let's talk.

## Timmy the Robot Checks Into Rancho Mirage

TechForce Robotics just deployed Timmy, a service robot, at the Hilton Garden Inn in Rancho Mirage. Timmy operates around the clock with fully autonomous navigation. It changes linens, makes deliveries, transports luggage, handles waste — tasks that require heavy lifting and repetitive effort.

The company says it's designed to support staff, not replace them. Staff feedback has been overwhelmingly positive. Hotel leaders say it reduces physical strain and improves efficiency.

But let's talk about the business model. The ROI for a hotel — which operates on thin margins — comes in two ways. One: it makes the guest experience better, driving loyalty and reducing marketing costs. Two: it cuts payroll, which is the biggest line item in the expenses column.

They may not be saying it in the press release, but in the executive boardrooms, if Timmy saves 5% or 10% in payroll, that's where the ROI is.

I'm not here to defend or attack. I'm here to say: this is the trend. Until the businesses and enterprises in the Coachella Valley create AI-fluent jobs — which may not be for quite some time — we will be shedding workers. My hope with AICV and Sunshine FM is that we elevate more people with AI skills and fluency faster than if we didn't exist.

I'm going to visit the hotel and see Timmy in action. If you work in hospitality and have thoughts on this, reach out.
