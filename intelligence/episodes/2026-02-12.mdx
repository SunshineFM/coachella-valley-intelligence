A performer in Palm Desert stood up at an event this week and told a room of nearly twenty people that he'd stopped paying a local freelance writer. She used to get \$5,000 per assignment — writing articles for his quarterly magazine. This year, he wasn't happy with her work. So he opened ChatGPT, fed it a prompt, loved the ideas it returned, and had the AI rewrite the whole thing. Readers are still emailing him about how great that issue was.

When asked if AI would replace jobs in the Coachella Valley, he said no. So did the hosts. Then the follow-up question landed: that writer is down \$5,000. If half the people in this room are doing the same thing, are those freelance writers, marketing agencies, and PR firms losing business or gaining it?

Nobody had an answer. And that's the problem — not the technology, but the refusal to see what's already happening. The valley is full of people using AI at home, on their phones, emailing themselves the results because their companies haven't sanctioned it yet. Microsoft warns that 80% of Fortune 500 companies face Shadow AI security risks. Only 47% have implemented security measures for generative AI. Your employees are already using it. You just don't know what data they're feeding into it.

## Anthropic Is Winning

Anthropic's business adoption rate jumped from 16.7% to 19.5% in a single month, one of the largest gains since tracking began. OpenAI slipped from 36.8% to 35.9%. That might sound small until you realize overall business AI adoption in the US just hit 46.8%. Nearly half of all American businesses are using AI daily. Most aren't choosing one model over another — they're using several. But Anthropic's Claude Cowork, released January 12th, is the reason for the surge.

Claude Cowork doesn't chat. It executes. It creates spreadsheets, organizes files, drafts reports, handles routine office tasks with minimal human oversight. Last week, when Anthropic released plugins for Excel and other Microsoft tools, the US stock market lost nearly a trillion dollars in software-as-a-service market cap. Some of that came back. Some investors said it was overblown. But the uncertainty is real. The concern is real.

Matt Schumer's viral post "Something Big is Happening" — the one that hit 54 million views and got read on yesterday's show — wasn't comparing AI to COVID as a health crisis. He was saying: remember how COVID changed everything and we never saw it coming? AI will feel the same way. He wrote it for his parents after watching the Super Bowl with family who kept asking what this AI stuff is really about. He wanted them to get in front of it, to learn it, to experiment with it, because he believes it will be deeply disruptive.

He's right. And the Coachella Valley is not taking this seriously enough — not with actions, anyway.

## Waymo Doubles Down, Wisdom Ages in Place

Waymo, Google's autonomous robo-taxi unit, is expanding to more than twenty cities this year and going international. The sixth-generation vehicles will be built in Arizona and aim to exceed one million paid weekly rides by year-end, up from 450,000. Waymo just raised \$16 billion at a \$126 billion valuation — double what it was a year ago. The main vehicle is called the Ojai One. Waymo is in talks with Hyundai to supply 50,000 Ioniq 5 electric vehicles by 2028 in a deal worth \$2.5 billion.

Self-driving cars are AI. Not the kind you chat with, but the kind that orchestrates thousands of sensor inputs in real time to navigate the world. This is the shift from the attention economy to the attachment economy — technology that doesn't just want your eyeballs, but your trust, your relationship, your dependency.

Talkiatry, one of the nation's largest in-network psychiatric care providers, just raised \$210 million in Series D funding. Unlike most telehealth competitors, Talkiatry employs its psychiatrists as full-time W-2 employees — more than 600 of them. The company has surpassed 2 million patient visits, doubling year-over-year. It treats depression, anxiety, ADHD, bipolar disorder, PTSD, and OCD, and is in-network with over sixty health plans. This is AI-assisted therapy backed by human psychiatrists — a model that works because it doesn't pretend humans aren't necessary.

Wisdom, a pre-seed AI startup, is developing sensor-based smart home technology for older adults. It just secured funding from the Richard King Mellon Foundation to help seniors age in place without cameras or wearables. The system uses ambient sensors and edge computing — meaning data stays on-premise — to detect falls, monitor activity patterns, and alert caregivers when something seems wrong. It processes everything locally on a Wisdom home hub using computer vision and machine learning. In collaboration with Samsung Health, it's working to clinically validate fall risk calculations.

This matters in the Coachella Valley, where healthcare is one of the biggest industries because of the aging population. By 2050, the number of Americans aged 65 or older will reach 82 million, up from 50 million a few years ago. Nearly 90% of adults over 65 want to stay in their homes as they age. California alone will need 3 million caregivers by 2030 — four years from now. Right now, there are 1.8 million job vacancies. The caregiver workforce is 80% women, 74% people of color, and 50% immigrants. Low wages, high turnover (around 50%), and physical demands are causing the crisis.

If Wisdom can solve this with AI, it should be piloted here. The valley has the population, the need, and the infrastructure. What it doesn't have is urgency.

## The Messy Middle

Demis Hassabis, Nobel Prize-winning CEO of Google DeepMind, predicts AI will usher in a new golden era of discovery within ten to fifteen years — curing diseases, solving the energy crisis, enabling humanity to venture beyond Earth. He's probably right. But between 2026 and 2040, there's chaos. That's the messy middle. The part where humans run companies, governments, and policies, and not everybody agrees on how to regulate AI or give it free rein. The part where a freelance writer loses \$5,000 and nobody thinks it's a problem because the output was good and the readers were happy.

The technology isn't the issue. It's culture, team readiness, and AI alignment. Most mid-sized companies in the valley — the ones with 200 to 1,000 employees — can't create a roadmap, playbook, or manual for how to integrate AI. But individuals are using it anyway. At home. On their phones. Emailing themselves the results. That's Shadow AI, and it's a security risk, a liability, and a sign that leadership isn't leading.

The question isn't whether AI will affect the Coachella Valley. It's whether the valley will prepare for it or pretend it's not happening until it's too late.
