A Palm Desert performer stood up at an event this week and said the quiet part out loud: he used to pay a local freelance writer $5,000 for two articles in his quarterly magazine. This year he wasn't happy with her work. So he turned to ChatGPT. Gave it a prompt. Loved the ideas. Rewrote the articles with AI. Still gets emails saying it was the best issue yet. The writer? Down $5,000. Gone.

He didn't say this with malice. He said it with enthusiasm. The host asked if AI would replace jobs in the Coachella Valley. He said no. The hosts agreed. But when pressed—if half the people in that room are doing what you just did, are those freelance writers getting more business or less?—the conversation stalled. No judgment, just math. This is a guy who works in an analog business, live performance, in-person programs. If he's replacing knowledge workers with a text prompt, what do we think is happening at the 200-employee hospitality companies, the PR agencies, the marketing firms?

The technology isn't theoretical anymore. It's $5,000 line items disappearing from local invoices.

## Anthropic Just Took a Massive Bite Out of OpenAI's Lead

While Palm Springs debates whether this is real, the business world has already moved. Anthropic—maker of Claude AI—hit 19.5% business adoption in the US according to RAMP's AI Index released this week. That's up from 16.7% the month prior, one of the largest single-month gains since tracking began. OpenAI, meanwhile, slipped from 36.8% to 35.9%. 

Those percentages represent millions of businesses. And this isn't zero-sum—most companies use multiple models. But the velocity matters. Anthropic came from behind in 2025 by focusing on enterprise and coding. Now in early 2026, they're winning on momentum. Much of that growth stems from Claude Cowork, the AI productivity tool unveiled January 12th that autonomously handles routine office tasks—creating spreadsheets, organizing files, drafting reports—without a chat interface. You give it a job. It does the job.

When Anthropic released the plugin for Claude Cowork to integrate with Excel and other Microsoft tools last week, US software-as-a-service stocks lost nearly a trillion dollars in market cap in a single day. Some of that bounced back. Investors called it overblown. But the uncertainty is real. If an AI can do Excel work autonomously, what exactly are we paying SaaS subscriptions for?

Overall business AI adoption in the US has now reached 46.8%. That's nearly half of all businesses. Not experimenting. Adopting.

## Shadow AI Is Already Inside 80% of Fortune 500 Companies

Microsoft released a report this week warning that 80% of Fortune 500 companies face Shadow AI security risks. That's employees building AI agents with low-code or no-code tools, or just using ChatGPT on their personal devices to do company work, then emailing it to themselves. Only 47% of companies surveyed—1,700 security professionals—have implemented security measures specifically designed for generative AI.

This isn't a future problem. A hospitality management company with 1,000 properties worldwide told me last week they can't create an AI roadmap or get executive sign-off on which models to use company-wide. So employees are using AI anyway. At home. On their phones. Uploading proprietary documents, customer data, financials to get their work done. It's called Shadow AI, and it's already here.

The gap between adoption and security is the vulnerability. The recommendation: create a sandbox. Let employees experiment within constraints. Don't make it punitive. Make it curious. But get your IT and security teams to set standards before someone uploads the wrong spreadsheet to the wrong model and you're in a compliance nightmare.

## Waymo Will Hit 1 Million Paid Rides Per Week by Year-End

Waymo, Google's autonomous robo-taxi unit, announced its sixth-generation vehicles will expand to more than 20 cities this year, including international markets like London and Tokyo. The company aims to exceed 1 million paid weekly rides by the end of 2026, up from 450,000 weekly trips now. That's more than doubling.

The new vehicles—called the Ojai One, a nice nod to the California township—feature 13 cameras, four LiDAR units, six radars, external audio receivers, and fewer total sensors than the previous generation but with greater capability. Waymo just closed a $16 billion funding round, valuing the company at $126 billion. That's double its valuation from just over a year ago.

Waymo is also in talks with Hyundai to supply 50,000 Ioniq 5 electric vehicles by 2028 in a deal worth approximately $2.5 billion. Why does this matter for a valley that thinks AI is just a chatbot? Because AI isn't just text. It's robotics. It's cars. It's the brain that orchestrates sensors, signals, decisions in real time. Self-driving cars are AI. And they're scaling faster than most people realize.

## The Attention Economy Is Dead. Welcome to the Attachment Economy.

There's a new framing making the rounds: we're transitioning from the attention economy to the attachment economy. Technology isn't just trying to grab your eyeballs anymore. It's trying to be your friend. Your companion. Your therapist.

Which brings us to Talky—yes, that's the name—a telepsychiatry provider that just raised $210 million in a Series D. Unlike most telehealth competitors that use contract providers, Talky directly employs its psychiatrists as full-time W2 employees. The company treats depression, anxiety, ADHD, bipolar disorder, PTSD, OCD. It's in-network with more than 60 health plans. As of last summer, Talky had grown to more than 600 psychiatrists and surpassed 2 million patient visits, doubling year-over-year.

This is human-in-the-loop AI therapy. You're talking to a real psychiatrist, but the system is powered by AI triage, scheduling, patient matching. It's a model that ensures continuity of care while scaling access. And it's working. Big VCs are in. The business model—likely billing through the black box of health insurance—is apparently printing money.

The question is: how long before the AI does more of the therapy itself? We're already seeing AI companions, AI coaches, AI confidants. The line between tool and relationship is blurring fast.

## Demis Hassabis Says AI Will Cure Everything in 10 to 15 Years

Demis Hassabis, Nobel Prize-winning CEO of Google DeepMind, predicted this week that AI will usher in a new golden era of discovery within 10 to 15 years. He's talking about curing major diseases, solving the energy crisis, enabling humanity to venture beyond Earth. Radical abundance. AI harnessing the scientific method to tackle humanity's most intractable problems.

I have no reason to doubt him. Hassabis has been winning chess tournaments since he was 4 years old. He's built AlphaGo, AlphaFold, some of the most important AI breakthroughs of the last decade. If anyone can see the trajectory, it's him.

But here's the problem: he's talking about 2036 or 2040. What about 2026 to 2030? That's the messy middle. That's the five to ten years where humans—who run companies, governments, policies, laws—are going to be dealing with mass displacement, economic chaos, and ideological clashes over how much autonomy to give AI. The techno-optimists talk about the promised land. I want to know what we're supposed to do in the desert on the way there.

Because the people who get hurt in the messy middle are the freelance writers losing $5,000 gigs. The caregivers who can't compete with AI-powered monitoring systems. The knowledge workers whose entire job description just became a prompt.

## An AI Startup That Might Actually Help the Valley's Aging Population

Here's a use case that matters locally: Wisdom, a pre-seed AI startup, is developing sensor-based smart home technology to help older adults age in place. The Coachella Valley has a large aging population. Healthcare is one of the biggest industries here. If Wisdom can use AI to let seniors stay in their homes safely, that's a win.

The system uses ambient sensors and edge computing—meaning data is processed locally on a home hub, not sent to the cloud—to detect falls, monitor activity patterns, and alert family or caregivers when something appears wrong. No cameras. No wearables. No buttons to press. It works passively in the background.

Wisdom was co-founded in 2024 by former tech executives Kathy Minter and Dawn Newsom. It's partnering with Samsung Health to clinically validate fall risk measurements. The company is targeting home care agencies, senior living communities, and hospital discharge programs rather than selling directly to consumers.

Why does this matter? By 2050, the number of Americans aged 65 or older is expected to reach 82 million, up from 50 million a few years ago. Nearly 90% of adults over 65 prefer to remain in their homes as they age. And there's a caregiver crisis: California alone will need 3 million caregivers by 2030. Right now there are 1.8 million job vacancies nationally. The workforce is 80% women, 74% people of color, 50% immigrants. Wages are low. Turnover is 50%. The system is breaking.

If AI can help fill that gap—monitoring, alerting, preventing falls (the single biggest domino that tips seniors into decline)—it's one of the few AI applications that feels unambiguously good. Wisdom processes data locally. Privacy concerns are real, but the trade-off might be worth it when the alternative is a fall that no one knows about for hours.

## What Happens Next

The performer at the Palm Desert event wasn't wrong to use ChatGPT. The freelance writer he replaced might have genuinely underdelivered. But when half the room does the same thing, we're not talking about individual decisions anymore. We're talking about structural change. And the Valley isn't ready for it.

Leaders here aren't taking this seriously. Not with their actions. I ran 30 AI workshops and boot camps in 2025 through AICV, the fiscally sponsored nonprofit. Didn't move the needle the way I wanted. So 2026 is about Sunshine FM—an AI-co-created, co-produced, co-broadcast radio station. An experiment in public. One human, an army of AI agents, trying to build something that doesn't exist yet.

Take a dream. Add AI. See what happens.

The question for you: what do you do for a living? Do you feel secure in it? And if half the people in your industry start doing what that performer did, where does that leave you?

I don't have the answer. But I know we need to start asking the question.
