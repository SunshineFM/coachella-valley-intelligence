A performer in Palm Desert stood up at an event this week and told a room of twenty people that he'd stopped paying a local freelance writer \$5,000 per assignment because ChatGPT did it better. He still gets emails praising the work. The writer — who also freelances for Palm Springs Life and other local magazines — is down five grand. No one in the room thought AI would replace jobs in the Coachella Valley.

That's the \$5,000 question hanging over this entire region. Not whether AI is useful — everyone agrees it is. Not whether it's impressive — the performer loved his AI-written articles. The question is what happens to the people who used to do that work, and whether we're watching a slow-motion collapse of knowledge work income or just a temporary disruption before something better emerges.

Because here's the thing: if half the people in that room are making the same choice, the freelance writers, marketing agencies, and PR firms in this valley are bleeding revenue right now. They might be using AI themselves to stay competitive, but that just raises the next question — who's actually creating the value, and what do white-collar knowledge workers do when the tool becomes better than the worker?

## Anthropic Is Eating OpenAI's Lunch

While local businesses figure out whether to fire their contractors, the enterprise world has already made its choice. Anthropic — the company behind Claude — hit 19.5% business adoption in February 2026, up from 16.7% the month before. That's one of the largest monthly gains since tracking began. OpenAI, meanwhile, slipped from 36.8% to 35.9%. 

Overall business AI adoption in the US is now at 46.8%. That's nearly half of all American businesses using AI daily. Most aren't picking one model over another — they're using multiple. But Anthropic's surge is being driven almost entirely by Claude Cowork, the AI productivity tool that handles routine office tasks autonomously. It doesn't wait for prompts. It creates spreadsheets, organizes files, drafts reports, and executes tasks with minimal human oversight.

When Anthropic released the plugin for Claude Cowork to integrate with Excel and other Microsoft tools last week, the US stock market lost nearly a trillion dollars in software-as-a-service market cap in a single day. Some of that came back. Some investors said it was overblown. But the uncertainty is real. The concern is real. And if you run a business in Palm Springs Coachella and you're reading this kind of news but assuming it won't affect you — you might be right. You might also be dead wrong.

## Shadow AI Is Already Inside Your Company

Microsoft just warned that 80% of Fortune 500 companies face Shadow AI security risks. That's not a future problem. That's happening right now. Employees are using AI at home on their personal devices, doing company work, and emailing it to themselves because no one has sanctioned an official AI model for company-wide use.

Only 47% of companies have implemented security measures specifically designed for generative AI, according to a survey of 1,700 security professionals commissioned by Microsoft. The gap between adoption and security is creating vulnerabilities that could turn digital assistants into corporate liabilities.

This is the messy middle. The period between "AI is a neat tool" and "AI has reorganized how we work" where chaos reigns because humans run companies, governments, policies, and laws — and not everyone agrees on how to regulate or deploy AI safely. The techno-optimists talk about AI curing diseases and solving the energy crisis in ten to fifteen years, and they're probably right. But between now and 2036, there's a five-to-ten-year window of pure chaos where people lose jobs, companies collapse, and no one has figured out the new rules yet.

If you're a business leader in the Coachella Valley, the question isn't whether to use AI. Your employees are already using it. The question is whether you're going to create a safe space for experimentation inside the company with proper security and constraints, or whether you're going to let Shadow AI proliferate on personal laptops and cell phones until something breaks.

## Waymo Is Doubling Down on Robo-Taxis

Google's autonomous vehicle division Waymo is expanding its sixth-generation robo-taxis to more than twenty cities this year, including international markets like London and Tokyo. The company aims to exceed one million paid weekly rides by the end of 2026, up from 450,000 weekly trips currently.

Waymo just raised \$16 billion in its latest funding round, valuing the company at \$126 billion — double its valuation from a year ago. The new vehicles feature thirteen cameras, four LiDAR units, six radars, external audio receivers, and fewer total sensors than the previous generation but with greater capability. The main vehicle is called the Ojai One, named after the California township.

Waymo is also in talks with Hyundai to supply 50,000 Ioniq 5 electric vehicles by 2028 in a deal worth approximately \$2.5 billion. That's two years out, which feels slow given the pace of everything else, but it's a signal that autonomous vehicles are moving from experiment to infrastructure.

This matters because AI isn't just something you chat with. It's being embedded in robotics, cars, and physical systems. A lot of people in the Coachella Valley still think AI is just a tool like a calculator or a spreadsheet. Self-driving cars are AI. The orchestration of sensors, signals, and decisions happening in real time is an AI brain. And that brain is about to be everywhere.

## The Attention Economy Is Dead. The Attachment Economy Is Here.

There's a new framing making the rounds: we're transitioning from the attention economy to the attachment economy. Technology isn't just vying for your attention anymore. It's vying for your friendship, your bonding, your relationship, your attachment.

Talkiatry — a telepsychiatry company and one of the nation's largest providers of in-network psychiatric care — just raised \$210 million in a Series D round. Unlike most telehealth competitors that rely on contract-based providers, Talkiatry directly employs its psychiatrists as full-time W2 employees. The company says this ensures better continuity of care and stronger patient-physician relationships.

As of summer 2025, Talkiatry had grown to more than 600 psychiatrists and had surpassed 2 million patient visits, doubling the previous year's total. The company treats depression, anxiety, ADHD, bipolar disorder, PTSD, and OCD, and is in-network with more than sixty health plans.

This is AI as virtual therapist, but backed up by human psychiatrists. It's a hybrid model that acknowledges people want human connection but also need scalable access. And it's a sign that AI isn't replacing humans in every domain — it's augmenting them in ways that make care more accessible and relationships more sustainable.

## Aging in Place with AI, No Cameras Required

Wisdom, a pre-seed AI startup, is developing sensor-based smart home technology for older adults to age in place without cameras or wearable devices. The company was co-founded by former tech executives Kathy Minter and Dawn Newsom in 2024 and has developed a platform that uses ambient sensors and edge computing to detect falls, monitor activity patterns, and alert family members or caregivers when something appears wrong.

Unlike traditional medical alert devices that require users to press a button, Wisdom's system works passively in the background. It processes data locally on a home hub and uses AI-powered computer vision and machine learning to identify potential falls or changes in daily routines. In collaboration with Samsung Health, the system is being clinically validated to measure fall risk.

This matters because the United States faces a projected caregiver shortage. By 2050, the number of Americans aged 65 or older is expected to reach 82 million, up from 50 million a few years ago. Nearly 90% of adults over 65 prefer to remain in their homes as they age. In California alone, 3 million caregivers will be needed by 2030 — just four years from now.

The caregiver workforce is predominantly women (80%), people of color (74%), and immigrants (50%). There are currently 1.8 million job vacancies in the caregiving sector, with turnover rates around 50% due to low wages and physical demands. Ten thousand people turn 65 every day in the US.

The Coachella Valley has a large aging population and healthcare is one of the biggest industries here because of it. If Wisdom can create an AI-powered system that helps older adults age in place safely without invading their privacy, that's a real solution to a real problem. And if local developers can help bring that technology to senior centers in the valley, even better.

## The Messy Middle

Demis Hassabis, the Nobel Prize-winning CEO of Google's DeepMind, predicts that AI will usher in a new golden era of discovery within the next ten to fifteen years — transforming medicine, solving the energy crisis, potentially enabling humanity to venture beyond Earth. He's probably right.

But between now and then, there's the messy middle. The five to ten years where freelance writers lose \$5,000 assignments, where companies run Shadow AI on personal devices, where no one has figured out the new rules. The period where humans — who run companies, governments, policies, and laws — clash over how to regulate, deploy, and live with AI.

The technology isn't the issue. It's culture. It's team readiness. It's AI alignment. And it's the question of what we do with the humans caught in the middle.
