A performer in Palm Desert stood up at an event this week and said something that should make every freelance writer, marketing agency, and PR firm in the valley sit up straight. He'd been paying a local writer — someone who also freelances for Palm Springs Life — \$5,000 per assignment for his quarterly magazine. This year, he wasn't happy with her work. So he turned to ChatGPT. Gave it a prompt. Got back ideas he loved. Used AI again to rewrite the article. Published it. Still gets emails saying it was brilliant.

That writer is down \$5,000. And if half the people in that room are doing the same thing, how many other freelancers, agencies, and knowledge workers in Palm Springs Coachella are quietly losing business right now?

This is not a hypothetical. This is happening in real time, in our valley, to people who thought their expertise was safe. And it's happening at the exact moment that AI adoption in US businesses just hit 46.8% — a number I didn't think we'd see until much later.

## Anthropic Takes the Lead

According to RAMP's AI Index released this week, Anthropic's business adoption rate surged from 16.7% to 19.5% in a single month — one of the largest monthly gains since tracking began. OpenAI, meanwhile, slipped from 36.8% to 35.9%. That may sound marginal until you realize we're talking about millions of businesses shifting their daily workflows.

Much of Anthropic's momentum comes from Claude Cowork, the AI productivity tool unveiled January 12th that autonomously handles routine office tasks — creating spreadsheets, organizing files, drafting reports — with minimal human oversight. Unlike traditional chat interfaces, Cowork doesn't wait for you to ask. It executes.

Last week, when Anthropic released plugins for Claude to work inside Excel and other Microsoft tools, the US stock market lost nearly a trillion dollars in software-as-a-service market cap. Some of that has come back. Some investors think it was overblown. But the uncertainty is real. The concern is real.

And here's the thing most leaders in Palm Springs Coachella are missing: this isn't a question of whether AI will affect your business. It's a question of whether you're going to control how it does, or whether your employees are already using it in ways you don't know about.

## Shadow AI Is Already Inside Your Company

Microsoft warns that 80% of Fortune 500 companies face Shadow AI security risks. That's the term for employees using AI tools on their personal devices, at home, on their phones, emailing results to themselves because the company hasn't sanctioned an official AI model yet.

A survey of 1,700 security professionals commissioned by Microsoft found that only 47% of companies have implemented security measures specifically designed for generative AI. The gap between adoption and security is creating vulnerabilities that could turn digital assistants into corporate liabilities.

I see this constantly in the valley. Small restaurants and coffee shops use AI here and there. Mid-size companies — hospitality groups managing a thousand properties worldwide, for example — can't create a roadmap or playbook for AI integration. But individuals inside those companies are using it anyway. They're using it at home to do their work. They're doing it on their mobile phones. And they're sharing proprietary documents, customer data, client records, financials to get their work done.

You want to create a safe space for these conversations. You also want to allow for curiosity and experimentation within the company — not on employees' cell phones or laptops, and certainly not on unmonitored desktops at work. Create a sandbox. Create constraints. Your IT department and CIOs should establish appropriate security and safety standards.

The technology is not the issue. It's culture. It's team readiness. It's AI alignment.

## The Messy Middle

Demis Hassabis, the Nobel Prize-winning CEO of Google's DeepMind, predicts that AI will usher in a new golden era of discovery within the next 10 to 15 years — transforming medicine, solving the energy crisis, potentially enabling humanity to venture beyond Earth. He describes a vision of radical abundance where AI harnesses the scientific method to tackle humanity's most intractable problems.

I have no reason to doubt him. When you see AI's exponential growth in understanding, comprehension, and reasoning, why wouldn't it figure out solutions to humanity's biggest problems?

Here's the challenge. Two parts.

First, timing. From 2026 to 2036 or 2040, you've got five to ten years of chaos. The messy middle. When techno-optimists talk about AI doing amazing things for humanity, I want to know what it's going to do now and in the not-too-distant future. Because the second part is this: humans are involved. Humans run companies. Humans run governments. Humans create policies, procedures, laws. Not everybody agrees to give AI full reign. Not everybody agrees to regulate AI safely and securely.

And when those two ideologies clash, the people caught in the middle are humans. While I love what Demis is building, I want his team to also figure out what we humans are going to be doing with AI right now — not just to prepare for that future, but to provide for ourselves and our families.

Because that's the chaos being unleashed on society. And Palm Springs Coachella is not immune.

## Waymo and the Attachment Economy

Waymo — Google's autonomous robo-taxi division — is expanding its sixth-generation vehicles across more than 20 cities and into international markets this year. The company aims to exceed 1 million paid weekly rides by the end of the year, up from 450,000 weekly trips. Waymo secured \$16 billion in its latest funding round, valuing it at about \$126 billion — basically doubling its valuation in just over a year.

The main vehicle is called the Ojai One. Waymo is also in talks with Hyundai to supply 50,000 Ioniq 5 electric vehicles by 2028 in a deal worth approximately \$2.5 billion.

This matters because AI is not just something we text or chat with. AI is being used in robotics and cars. Self-driving cars are AI — it takes that AI brain to orchestrate all the signals in and out to make sense of the world.

And this falls into a new framing I've been talking about: the shift from the attention economy to the attachment economy. The technology is not just vying for your attention anymore. It's vying for your friendship, your bonding, your relationship, your attachment.

Which brings me to Talkiatry.

## AI Therapy and the Caregiver Crisis

Talkiatry, one of the nation's largest providers of in-network psychiatric care, announced a \$210 million Series D fundraise. Unlike many telehealth competitors that rely on contract-based providers, Talkiatry directly employs its psychiatrists as full-time W2 employees — a model the company says ensures better continuity of care and stronger patient-physician relationships.

As of last summer, the company had grown its workforce to more than 600 psychiatrists and had surpassed 2 million patient visits, basically doubling what it had a year prior. The company treats conditions including depression, anxiety, ADHD, bipolar disorder, PTSD, and OCD, and is in-network with more than 60 health plans.

This is AI-assisted therapy backed by human psychiatrists. And it's arriving at exactly the right moment.

By 2050, the number of Americans aged 65 or older is expected to reach 82 million, up from 50 million a few years ago. Nearly 90% of adults over 65 prefer to remain in their homes as they age. But the United States faces a projected caregiver shortage. In California alone, about 3 million caregivers will be needed by 2030 — just four years from now. There are already 1.8 million job vacancies. Low wages, high turnover of about 50%, and physical demands are causing the crisis, leaving families struggling to find care for aging parents.

Ten thousand people turn 65 each day in the US. The caregiver workforce is predominantly women (80%), people of color (74%), and immigrants (50%).

Which is why a pre-seed startup called Wisdom matters for Palm Springs Coachella.

Wisdom is developing sensor-based smart home technology for older adults, funded by a social impact investment program through the Richard King Mellon Foundation. The company is building an AI-powered home monitoring system designed to help parents live independently without cameras or wearable devices.

Co-founded by former tech executives Kathy Minter and Dawn Newsom in 2024, Wisdom uses ambient sensors and edge computing to detect falls, monitor activity patterns, and alert family members or caregivers when something appears wrong. Unlike traditional medical alert devices that require users to press a button, Wisdom's system works passively in the background.

The system processes data locally on a Wisdom home hub and uses AI-powered computer vision and machine learning to identify potential falls or changes in daily routines. In collaboration with Samsung Health, it's developing clinically validated calculations to measure fall risk.

Falling is one of the biggest risks for aging seniors. It's also one of the biggest dominoes that tips over all the other dominoes into a decline in health.

Wisdom is targeting home care agencies, senior living communities, and hospital discharge programs rather than selling directly to consumers. And given that the Coachella Valley has a large percentage of aging seniors and active adults, and that healthcare is one of the biggest industries in town mostly because of this aging population, this technology could have real impact here.

If you're building something like this, if you're developing on-premise hubs for aging senior centers, reach out. Let's talk about bringing this to the valley.

## What Happens Next

I was at that event in Palm Desert with just under 20 people. A lot of brain power and talent in the room — folks who work at large studios in Hollywood, folks who work at large tech companies, folks who were early at Meta and Microsoft and Google and chose to live in the valley because of quality of life.

When the host asked the performer if AI was going to replace jobs in the Coachella Valley, he said no. The hosts agreed. We had a conversation about whether it would or wouldn't.

Then I asked a follow-up. That freelance writer you decided not to use anymore — she's down \$5,000 this year. If half the people in this room are doing that, are those other freelance writers, those other marketing agencies, those other PR companies losing business or getting more business?

I don't know where this goes. I imagine marketing agencies and PR agencies and freelance writers are using AI anyway. But the question becomes: who's actually creating the value? And what do we as professionals, white-collar knowledge workers, do if this is just anecdotally the sign of things to come?

There's no judgment here. I'm just asking the question because I want to learn. And if people are going to be losing jobs, I'm trying to do something about it. But if you absolutely believe you're only using AI because other people are not very good at their own work, that's a different conversation.

My question to you is this: where do you see this going? What do you do for a living? And do you feel secure in it?

Because privacy is already gone. People have already given up their privacy. And the messy middle is here.
