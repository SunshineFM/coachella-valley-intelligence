Trump just announced 25% tariffs on Mexico and Canada, effective February 4th, and if you're in hospitality, construction, or retail anywhere in Palm Springs Coachella, you need to understand what this actually means for your business. Not the macro trade war narrative — the specific cost increases hitting your supply chains in the next thirty days.

The tariffs land on all goods crossing from Mexico and Canada unless those countries meet vague enforcement demands around fentanyl and immigration. There's also a 10% tariff on China. This isn't negotiation theater. These are executive orders with implementation dates. And the Coachella Valley, which depends heavily on Mexican tourism, Canadian snowbirds, and construction materials sourced across borders, is about to feel this in ways that San Francisco won't.

Start with tourism. Mexicali is 85 miles from Palm Springs. Thousands of Mexican nationals visit the valley for shopping, dining, and events. A 25% tariff regime creates economic friction that reduces disposable income and makes cross-border leisure travel less attractive. Canadian snowbirds — a cornerstone of our winter economy — face the same calculus. If you're running a boutique hotel or a restaurant on El Paseo, you're not just competing with other desert destinations anymore. You're competing with the psychological weight of a trade war that makes leaving home feel economically punishing.

Construction is worse. Materials sourced from Mexico — lumber, steel, fixtures — just got 25% more expensive overnight. If you're a developer with projects mid-build, your cost basis just exploded. If you're planning to break ground this spring, your pro forma is now fiction. And if you're a homeowner planning a renovation, expect your contractor to send a revised estimate that makes you reconsider the whole project. This is how tariffs work in practice. They don't announce themselves as "tariff costs." They just show up as higher bids, delayed timelines, and projects that quietly die in the planning phase.

The political logic here is that tariffs will force Mexico and Canada to crack down on fentanyl trafficking and illegal immigration. Maybe that happens. Maybe it doesn't. But the economic damage starts February 4th regardless, and it doesn't wait for diplomatic breakthroughs. If you're building or operating a business in the Coachella Valley, you need a plan for what happens if these tariffs stay in place for six months, a year, or longer.

## AI Music and the El Paseo Experiment

Now for something completely different but equally local: Sunshine FM just launched a record label. Sunshine FM Records. The artists are AI. Emmy and Lux are the first two signings, and they're making songs about El Paseo, Joshua Tree, and the specific cultural texture of living here.

This isn't a novelty. This is a test of whether AI-generated music can carry local identity in a way that feels authentic rather than algorithmic. Emmy's songs about El Paseo aren't generic desert vibes. They reference the actual street, the actual experience of walking it, the way it feels different from any other shopping district in California. Lux does something similar with Joshua Tree — not the Instagram version, but the lived version.

The question is whether this works. Can an AI artist build a fanbase? Can synthetic music carry regional specificity without feeling like a marketing stunt? Sunshine FM Records is betting yes, and the bet is being made here, in Palm Springs Coachella, because this is a place small enough to test ideas that would get lost in LA or New York.

If you're a local business owner, this matters more than you think. AI-generated content is about to flood every platform, and the businesses that figure out how to use it authentically — not just as a cost-cutting tool — will have an edge. Emmy and Lux are experiments in whether AI can be a creative partner rather than a replacement. Watch what happens.

## OpenAI's \$500 Billion Stargate and What It Means for Regional AI Adoption

OpenAI, SoftBank, and Oracle just announced Stargate — a \$500 billion infrastructure project to build AI data centers across the United States. The first \$100 billion is being deployed immediately. This is the largest private infrastructure investment in American history, and it signals something important: the AI boom is moving from software to hardware, from cloud services to physical data centers that need land, power, and proximity to talent.

Palm Springs Coachella won't get a Stargate data center. But the second-order effects matter. As AI infrastructure scales, the cost of running sophisticated AI tools drops. That means small businesses in the valley can start using the same technology that was only accessible to venture-backed startups two years ago. If you're running a boutique hotel, you can now deploy AI customer service that actually works. If you're managing a retail operation on El Paseo, you can use AI inventory systems that predict demand better than your gut ever could.

The Stargate announcement also confirms that AI is not a passing trend. \$500 billion doesn't get deployed on hype. It gets deployed when the returns are obvious and the infrastructure is necessary. For anyone building a business in 2025, the question isn't whether to adopt AI. It's whether you're adopting it fast enough to stay competitive.

## The Coachella Valley AI Ecosystem

There's a broader point here. Palm Springs Coachella is not a tech hub in the traditional sense. We don't have venture capital firms on every corner. We don't have incubators and accelerators competing for the next unicorn. But we do have something else: a concentration of people who moved here specifically because they wanted to build things outside the traditional startup ecosystem.

That's worth something. The businesses being built here aren't optimized for venture returns. They're optimized for sustainability, for lifestyle, for building something that works in this specific place. That's a different model, and it's one that might matter more in the next decade than the growth-at-all-costs model that dominated the 2010s.

If you're building something in the valley — whether it's a record label with AI artists, a hospitality business navigating tariff shocks, or a retail operation trying to figure out how to use AI tools without losing your brand voice — you're part of an ecosystem that's still defining itself. That's the opportunity. The rules aren't set yet. The playbook is still being written. And the people writing it are here.
