OpenAI spent $157 billion training o3. Not million. Billion. With a B.

That number landed this week from SemiAnalysis, and it's the kind of figure that reframes everything happening in AI right now. For context: that's more than the GDP of Hungary. It's roughly what the entire global semiconductor industry spends on R&D in a year. And it's what OpenAI burned to build a reasoning model that costs about $1,000 per task to run.

Meanwhile, Anthropic shipped Claude 3.7 Sonnet — a model that handles most real-world work for a few cents per call, runs faster, and doesn't require a hedge fund's capital budget to deploy.

The gap between those two realities is where the actual story lives. Not in the benchmarks. In the economics.

## The Reasoning Tax

o3 is legitimately impressive. It hit 25% on the Frontier Math benchmark — a test designed to be unsolvable by current AI, where previous models scored under 2%. It cleared 87.7% on the USA Math Olympiad qualifier. These are hard problems. The kind where you need to think, backtrack, explore dead ends, and synthesize across domains.

But here's what that costs: o3 uses something OpenAI calls "private chain of thought." The model doesn't just answer. It reasons internally, sometimes for minutes, burning tokens the whole time. High compute mode can chew through the equivalent of a small research team's monthly budget on a single question.

That's fine if you're trying to prove a theorem or solve a novel materials science problem. It's insane if you're building a customer service bot or summarizing contracts or writing marketing copy — which is what 95% of businesses actually need AI to do.

The question isn't whether o3 is powerful. It obviously is. The question is whether that power is useful at that price, or whether we've hit the point where frontier capability and practical deployment have diverged so far that they're not even in the same market anymore.

## Claude 3.7 and the Real Build

Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet shipped the same week. It's not trying to win math olympiads. It's trying to be the model you actually use.

It's 2x faster than the previous Sonnet. It costs half as much. It handles vision, tool use, and extended context windows without melting your API budget. And on the benchmarks that matter for real work — coding, analysis, instruction following — it's right there with GPT-4 and better than most alternatives.

This is the model you'd build a business on. Not because it's the most powerful thing in the lab, but because it's powerful enough and you can actually afford to run it at scale.

For anyone building in Palm Springs Coachella — whether that's a hospitality tech startup, a real estate analytics tool, or an AI-powered service layer for local government — this is the tier that matters. You don't need a model that can solve novel math proofs. You need a model that can handle 10,000 customer interactions a day without bankrupting you.

## Gemini 2.0 Flash and the Speed Layer

Google's Gemini 2.0 Flash is playing a different game entirely. It's optimized for speed and multimodal work — image generation, real-time voice, spatial understanding. It's not trying to be the smartest model. It's trying to be the fastest one that's still smart enough.

This matters for local use cases more than people realize. A property management company that wants to auto-generate listing descriptions with photos. A tourism app that needs to answer questions about trail conditions in real time. A city planning tool that overlays zoning data on satellite imagery. These are Gemini 2.0 Flash problems, not o3 problems.

The model also ships with native tool integration and a new agent framework called Jules, which is designed to handle multi-step workflows without constant human supervision. That's the direction the whole industry is heading — not bigger models, but models that can do more with less oversight.

## The DeepSeek Wildcard

Then there's DeepSeek. A Chinese lab that just released R1 — a reasoning model that reportedly matches o1 performance at a fraction of the cost. They're claiming breakthrough efficiency through a technique called mixture of experts, where the model only activates the parts it needs for a given task instead of running the whole thing every time.

If that's real — and early reports suggest it might be — it's a problem for OpenAI's entire pricing model. Because if someone else can deliver 80% of the reasoning capability at 10% of the cost, the $157 billion training run starts to look less like an investment and more like a sunk cost.

DeepSeek also matters because it's a reminder that the frontier isn't just in San Francisco anymore. The best AI work is happening in Beijing, Paris, London, and increasingly in smaller labs that can't afford to burn nine figures on a single model. That's forcing everyone to get more efficient, which is good for the rest of us.

## What This Means for the Desert

If you're building something in Palm Springs Coachella, the lesson is pretty clear: don't wait for the frontier. Build on the models that are here, cheap, and good enough.

Claude 3.7 Sonnet is good enough for almost everything. Gemini 2.0 Flash is good enough for the rest. And if you need reasoning, you can probably get 90% of the way there with clever prompting and tool use instead of paying $1,000 a pop for o3.

The other lesson: the cost curve is moving in your favor. A year ago, running a sophisticated AI product required serious capital. Today, you can build something genuinely useful for a few hundred bucks a month in API costs. That's a different game.

The businesses that win in this environment won't be the ones using the most expensive models. They'll be the ones that figure out how to deliver value with the cheapest models that still work. That's an engineering problem, not a capital problem. And it's one you can solve from the desert as easily as you can from San Francisco.
