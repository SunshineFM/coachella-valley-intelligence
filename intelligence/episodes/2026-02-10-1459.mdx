Daniela Amodei just said what we've been teaching in workshops all year: the humanities are back. The Anthropic co-founder told ABC News that her company prioritizes hiring people who are "exceptional communicators, possess high emotional intelligence, and are kind, compassionate and curious with a desire to assist others" over those with purely technical backgrounds. Not coding bootcamp grads. Not Stanford CS majors. People who can feel, listen, and care.

This matters in Palm Springs Coachella because for two decades, anyone studying literature or psychology or philosophy has been told they picked the wrong major. That the future belonged to engineers. And maybe for those two decades, that was true. But with AI agents now writing code, debugging systems, and generating entire applications in minutes, the scarcest resource isn't technical skill — it's the ability to understand what humans actually need and how to deliver it without making them feel like they're talking to a machine.

If you run a hospitality business, a retail operation, a creative agency, or any service where human connection drives revenue, this is your moment. The people you thought were "soft skill" hires are about to become your most valuable assets. High emotional intelligence isn't a nice-to-have. It's the competitive advantage when everyone else has access to the same AI tools.

## The Ad War Nobody Asked For

OpenAI flipped the switch. Ads are now live in ChatGPT for free and lower-tier users. If you're paying \$20 a month for Plus or \$200 for Pro, you're safe. Everyone else gets sponsored modules.

Here's why this happened: OpenAI burned through \$2.5 billion in the first half of last year. Only 5% of their 800 million weekly active users pay for subscriptions. That's unsustainable when you're running the world's most compute-intensive consumer product. Sam Altman's pitch is simple — if ads can account for 20% of revenue, the company survives. Without them, the free tier dies or gets so crippled it's useless.

Anthropic ran a Super Bowl commercial trolling "ads in AI" without naming OpenAI directly. Altman fired back, saying more people in Texas alone use ChatGPT for free than the total number of people using Claude in the US. His argument: we're democratizing access. Claude is for rich people. We're for everyone.

But here's the tension. People use ChatGPT for deeply personal things — therapy-adjacent conversations, career advice, relationship problems. It feels like a companion, not a search engine. The moment that trust breaks, the whole model collapses. OpenAI promises no ads in health, mental health, or political conversations. No ads for users under 18. Advertisers get aggregated metrics only, no access to individual chats. But the slippery slope is real. Google started with clearly labeled sponsored results. Now the font is microscopic and the entire first page is ads.

For local businesses in the Valley, this is a preview of what's coming to every AI platform you touch. If you're building customer service bots, shopping assistants, or voice agents, you'll face the same choice: charge users or monetize through sponsors. And if you choose ads, you better have ironclad trust, because one bad experience and your customers are gone.

## Spotify Wants to Remix Your Favorite Songs (If the Labels Ever Agree)

Spotify co-CEO Gustav Soderstrom told investors they've already built the technology to let fans create AI remixes and covers of their favorite artists' music. The catch? They're waiting on the music industry to figure out licensing.

This is rich. Spotify spent the last 15 years destroying the economics of recorded music for everyone except the top 1% of artists. Now they want to add another revenue stream — AI-generated remixes — and they're positioning themselves as the good guys waiting for the labels to catch up.

If you're an independent artist or band in Palm Springs Coachella, this doesn't help you. You already make your living through live performance, merch, and licensing. Streaming pays \$60 to \$85 for 10,000 plays before everyone takes their cut. AI remixes won't change that. If anything, the wall gets higher. More noise. More content. Less signal.

## YouTube Music Adds AI Playlist Generator, Nobody Cares

YouTube Music rolled out an AI-powered playlist generator for premium subscribers. You describe a mood or genre through text or voice — "progressive house mix for a chill party" or "90s classic hits" — and it builds a playlist for you.

This is a retention play. They're trying to stop churn by giving subscribers more value. But making playlists was never the hard part. Discovery was. Curation was. Knowing what you didn't know you wanted — that's what great DJs do. An AI that just mashes together tracks based on keywords isn't solving that problem.

If you're using YouTube Music Premium and you've tried this feature, let me know. I'm not the target market, but I'm curious if it actually delivers something better than Spotify's algorithm or Apple Music's human-curated playlists.

## AI Shopping Assistants Are Failing, But the Money's Pouring In Anyway

Nearly 70% of mobile shoppers are willing to try AI shopping assistants. Nearly 70% of those who try them bail because the product recommendations are terrible.

Chatbots have been around forever, and we've hated them forever. The promise of AI was supposed to fix this. Instead, we're getting the same endless loops, the same inability to understand context, the same feeling that you're talking to a wall.

But here's what's wild: AI platforms are now sending over a billion referral visits to major websites. ChatGPT alone is driving 800 million weekly active users. Target is seeing 40% monthly growth in traffic from ChatGPT. Amazon's chatbot Rufus — which most people don't even know exists — has 300 million users, and people who use it are 60% more likely to buy something.

Last year, 12% of US shoppers used AI assistants. That number just hit 35%. Half of those consumers say they'd let AI handle the entire shopping process, including payment. Agent-to-agent commerce is here. We called this in workshops last summer. The infrastructure is live. The consumer willingness is there. The only thing missing is trust.

If you're a small business or enterprise in the Coachella Valley and you want to build an AI receptionist or customer service agent that doesn't suck, reach out. There are great developers in town. There are also a lot of people who will sell you a chatbot you can build yourself in 20 minutes that will destroy your customer experience. Know the difference.

## The Burnout Nobody Saw Coming

A presenter at an AI training session in Palm Desert this morning said something I've heard a hundred times: "You can get 10 social media posts done in five minutes now. What used to take half a day. You'll have so much more time to do what you really want to do."

Then I came back and read a Harvard Business Review study published yesterday. Workers who adopt AI tools are experiencing burnout at higher rates because workloads expand rather than contract.

UC Berkeley researchers spent eight months observing a 200-person tech company that gave employees access to commercial AI tools without mandating their use. The pattern was consistent: nobody was pressured to hit new targets, but people started doing more because the tools made more feel achievable. Rather than leaving work early, employees voluntarily expanded their to-do lists. Work bled into lunch breaks and late evenings.

One engineer told the researchers: "You thought that maybe because you could be more productive with AI, then you can save some time, you can work less, but in reality you don't work less, you just work the same amount or even more."

The researchers call this "workload creep" or "expectation creep." Once teams learned they could generate drafts or code or analysis in minutes, their KPIs quietly ratcheted up. Product managers started writing code. Researchers took on engineering tasks. The conversational interface of AI eliminates blank page syndrome, which sounds great until you realize it also eliminates stopping points.

Previous research from the Upwork Research Institute found that 77% of employees using AI tools said the tools added to their workload. A Quantum Workplace survey showed that frequent AI users report 45% higher burnout rates compared to those who rarely or never use the technology.

For Palm Springs Coachella, this is the warning. If you're a social media manager and your AI agent writes 10 posts in 30 minutes instead of four hours, you're not getting the afternoon off. You're preparing more posts for tomorrow. Or repurposing them into blogs. Or turning them into video scripts. Your workload gets heavier because you're doing more.

This is great for productivity. It's terrible for mental health. And it's especially terrible if you're trading hours for a paycheck and your sense of value is tied to how much you produce.

The Berkeley researchers warned that without intentional guidelines — what they call an "AI practice," what I call an AI Council — the inherent tendency of AI-assisted work is not to reduce workloads but to intensify them. Workload creep leads to cognitive fatigue, burnout, and weakened decision making. The productivity surge at the beginning gives way to lower quality work, turnover, and collapse.

You have to be intentional. You have to be responsible. You have to be strategic about what outcomes you want, personally and professionally. Otherwise, the tools that were supposed to free you will bury you.
