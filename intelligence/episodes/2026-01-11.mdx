OpenAI just shipped an agent that books your dinner reservations and buys your concert tickets. It's called Operator, it launched January 9th, and it represents the first time a major AI lab has put a reasoning model into the wild with the ability to actually do things on the web — not just answer questions, but click buttons, fill forms, complete transactions.

This matters for Palm Springs Coachella because we're a service economy. Hospitality, events, tourism, real estate — all industries where the interface between customer intent and transaction is currently friction. Someone wants a tee time at Indian Wells, a table at Workshop, tickets to Coachella. Right now that's phone calls, booking platforms, waitlists. Operator is the beginning of that layer disappearing. The agent just does it.

The technical architecture is worth understanding. Operator runs on GPT-4O, the same model powering ChatGPT, but with a new capability called Computer-Using Agent — CUA. It can navigate websites the way a human would. OpenAI trained it by having humans demonstrate tasks, then letting the model practice in a sandboxed browser environment. It learned to handle dynamic pages, deal with CAPTCHAs, recover from errors. The result is something that feels less like a chatbot and more like a very patient assistant who never gets tired of filling out forms.

There are constraints. Operator can't create accounts, can't make payments directly — it hands off to the user for anything involving credentials or money. It's also limited to certain categories: travel, shopping, dining, services. OpenAI is clearly being cautious. But the direction is obvious. Every local business with an online booking system is about to get a new category of customer: agents acting on behalf of humans.

The question for operators here is whether your digital infrastructure is legible to an AI. If Operator can't parse your reservation flow, can't understand your availability calendar, can't complete a transaction — you're invisible to the next wave of demand. That's not a 2030 problem. Operator is live now for ChatGPT Pro subscribers, \$200 a month, and OpenAI says broader access is coming soon.

## The \$6 Million Training Run That Broke The Cost Curve

DeepSeek, a Chinese AI lab most people haven't heard of, just released a model that performs comparably to GPT-4O and Claude Sonnet. The training cost was \$5.6 million. For context, frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic are rumored to cost between \$100 million and \$1 billion to train. DeepSeek did it for pocket change.

The model is called DeepSeek-V3. It uses a mixture-of-experts architecture — 671 billion parameters total, but only 37 billion active per token. That's the efficiency trick. Most of the model stays dormant for any given task, which means lower compute costs during both training and inference. DeepSeek also claims they trained it in two months using 2,788 NVIDIA H800 GPUs. The H800 is the export-restricted version of the H100, designed to comply with U.S. chip controls on China. Somehow, they made it work anyway.

The implications are straightforward. If you can train a frontier-class model for single-digit millions, the barrier to entry for AI development just collapsed. That means more labs, more models, more competition — and probably more local applications. A startup in Palm Springs doesn't need OpenAI's capital structure to build something useful on top of a capable foundation model. The cost curve just bent in favor of the small and the weird.

There's also a geopolitical angle. U.S. export controls were supposed to slow Chinese AI progress by limiting access to advanced chips. DeepSeek's results suggest that strategy isn't working. They found a way around the constraints, and they did it efficiently enough to embarrass the incumbents. That's going to accelerate the policy debate in Washington about whether chip restrictions are effective or just theatrical.

For anyone building here, the takeaway is simple: model access is no longer a moat. The interesting work is in application, in taste, in knowing what to build for a specific place and a specific audience. That's where local advantage lives.

## Why Palm Springs Needs Its Own Media Infrastructure

This show exists because general-purpose media doesn't cover what's happening here. Not in detail. Not with specificity. Not in a way that's useful if you're actually building something in this market.

The Coachella Valley has 500,000 people, \$30 billion in real estate value, two of the largest music festivals in the world, a growing tech and creative class, and a climate that makes it habitable when the rest of the country is frozen or flooded. It's also two hours from Los Angeles, which means it's close enough to matter but far enough to have its own logic.

And yet the information environment here is thin. Local news covers city council meetings and traffic accidents. National news covers national news. The gap in between — the business moves, the funding rounds, the new projects, the people relocating here and why — that's not systematically covered anywhere.

SunshineFM is an experiment in filling that gap using AI as infrastructure. The content is human-written, human-edited, human-voiced. But the production system is AI-assisted. Transcription, research, distribution — all automated. That means the cost structure is low enough to be sustainable without venture funding or a large subscriber base. It also means the format can be flexible. Some episodes will be short. Some will be long. Some will be interviews. Some will be solo commentary. The constraint is quality, not length.

The business model is still forming. Sponsorships, probably. Subscriptions, maybe. Consulting for local businesses that want better intelligence on what's happening in their sector. The goal isn't to build a media empire. It's to build something useful that couldn't exist otherwise — a continuous intelligence layer for a place that deserves one.

If you're building something here, or thinking about it, this show is for you. If you're trying to understand how AI is going to change local economies, this is a live case study. And if you just want to know what's actually happening in Palm Springs Coachella beyond the surface layer — welcome. You're in the right place.
