Palm Springs has to build 9,600 housing units by 2031. That's not an aspiration — it's a state mandate under California's Regional Housing Needs Assessment. The city is nowhere close. Construction costs are brutal, labor is scarce, and the math doesn't work for developers trying to build anything affordable. This is the central tension in the desert right now: a place that's attracting remote workers, tech founders, and outside capital while the people who actually run the hotels, restaurants, and services can't afford to live here.

The housing crisis isn't abstract. It's showing up in workforce shortages across every sector. Hospitality can't staff fully. Retail has constant turnover. The people building the new Palm Springs often don't realize how fragile the service economy underneath them has become. And the gap is widening. As property values climb and short-term rentals eat into long-term housing stock, the disconnect between who lives here and who works here gets worse.

What makes this harder is that the solutions aren't obvious. Building more housing requires land, labor, materials, and financing — all of which are expensive in the desert. Developers can make the numbers work on luxury condos and vacation rentals. They can't make them work on workforce housing without subsidies, which the city doesn't have enough of. So the mandate sits there, and the gap grows.

## Trump's Second Term Starts With a Flurry of Executive Orders

Donald Trump was inaugurated for his second term today. Within hours, he signed a stack of executive orders that will reshape federal policy across multiple domains. He declared a national energy emergency, opening up federal lands for oil and gas drilling. He paused all federal hiring except for military and national security roles. He delayed the TikTok ban by 75 days, giving ByteDance more time to negotiate a sale or restructure. He pulled the U.S. out of the Paris Climate Agreement again. He ended remote work for federal employees, requiring them to return to offices five days a week.

The energy emergency is significant. It's a signal that the administration intends to prioritize domestic energy production over climate commitments, and it gives the executive branch broad authority to fast-track permitting and bypass environmental reviews. For the Coachella Valley, this could mean more pressure on solar and wind projects to compete with subsidized fossil fuels, even as the region continues to build out renewable infrastructure.

The federal hiring freeze and remote work ban won't directly hit Palm Springs hard — there isn't a massive federal workforce here — but it's a cultural signal. The Trump administration is explicitly rejecting the remote work model that helped fuel the desert's population growth over the past few years. If other employers follow that lead, the flow of remote workers to places like Palm Springs could slow.

The TikTok delay is pure negotiation theater. Trump is giving ByteDance more time, but he's also making it clear that the app's future in the U.S. depends on a deal that satisfies national security concerns. For creators and small businesses in the Coachella Valley who've built audiences on TikTok, this is another 75 days of uncertainty.

## DeepSeek Disrupts the AI Arms Race

A Chinese AI lab called DeepSeek just released a model that's competitive with OpenAI's best work — and they claim they built it for a fraction of the cost. DeepSeek says their model was trained for under $6 million, compared to the hundreds of millions that OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic have spent on their frontier models. If that's true, it's a serious problem for the American AI companies that have been raising billions on the premise that building cutting-edge AI requires massive capital and compute.

The release triggered a sharp sell-off in Nvidia stock, which dropped over 10% in a single day. Nvidia has been the biggest beneficiary of the AI boom because every major lab needs their GPUs to train models. If DeepSeek's approach works — if you can build competitive models without buying tens of thousands of Nvidia chips — then the entire infrastructure layer of AI gets repriced.

There's skepticism about DeepSeek's claims. Some researchers think the $6 million figure is misleading, that it doesn't account for the full cost of compute and data. Others think DeepSeek is using techniques that trade off capability for efficiency in ways that won't scale. But even if the claims are inflated, the fact that a Chinese lab is now releasing models that rival American ones is a geopolitical shift. The U.S. has been trying to maintain an AI lead through export controls on chips and compute. DeepSeek suggests that lead is narrower than policymakers assumed.

For Palm Springs, this matters because the desert has been positioning itself as a landing spot for tech workers and founders leaving the Bay Area. If the AI boom slows, if the capital dries up, if Nvidia's dominance fades, that affects the kind of people moving here and the kind of money flowing into local real estate and businesses.

## Meta Ends Fact-Checking, Embraces Community Notes

Meta announced it's ending its third-party fact-checking program and replacing it with a community notes system modeled on X (formerly Twitter). Mark Zuckerberg framed this as a move toward free expression, saying the old system had become too politically biased. In practice, it means Facebook and Instagram will no longer label or downrank posts flagged by outside fact-checkers. Instead, users will be able to add context to posts, and the most helpful notes will surface based on community voting.

This is a significant shift. Meta's fact-checking program was one of the most visible content moderation systems on the internet. It was also one of the most controversial, with conservatives arguing it was biased against right-wing content and progressives arguing it didn't go far enough to stop misinformation. By moving to community notes, Meta is offloading the moderation burden to users and reducing its own liability.

The timing is notable. Trump just took office, and Meta is making a change that will likely result in less content moderation around political speech. Whether that's a response to political pressure or a genuine shift in Zuckerberg's thinking about free expression, the effect is the same: more unfiltered political content on the platform.

For local businesses and creators in the Coachella Valley, this changes the information environment. Community notes can work well when the community is engaged and diverse. They can also be gamed by coordinated groups. If you're running a business page or a local news account, you're now operating in a system where misinformation can spread faster and corrections depend on user participation rather than institutional fact-checkers.

## What This Means for the Desert

The through-line here is uncertainty. Palm Springs is in the middle of a housing crisis with no clear path to meeting state mandates. The federal government is pulling back on climate commitments and remote work, both of which have shaped the valley's recent growth. The AI boom that brought tech money and tech workers to the desert might be hitting a wall. And the information systems that local businesses rely on are shifting toward less moderation and more user-driven content.

None of this is fatal. But it does mean that the assumptions that drove growth over the past few years — remote work is permanent, tech money is infinite, climate policy favors renewables, social platforms are stable — are all being tested at once. The people building businesses and lives here need to be thinking about what comes next if any of those assumptions break.
