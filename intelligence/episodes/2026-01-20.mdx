Donald Trump took the oath of office today for his second term as President of the United States. The ceremony happened at noon Eastern, moved indoors to the Capitol Rotunda because of cold weather — the first indoor inauguration since Ronald Reagan's second term in 1985. Trump's speech ran about thirty minutes and hit familiar themes: border security, energy dominance, ending what he called "woke" policies in government and military. He signed executive orders immediately after, including one declaring a national energy emergency to speed up drilling permits and another pulling the United States out of the Paris Climate Agreement for the second time.

The timing matters for Palm Springs Coachella because the policy direction collides directly with what's happening in California right now. While Trump was speaking about American energy independence, Los Angeles was still burning. The Palisades and Eaton fires have now killed at least 24 people and destroyed more than 12,000 structures. The economic damage estimate sits at \$250 billion, making this the costliest wildfire disaster in American history. And the insurance industry has essentially left the state.

State Farm, Allstate, and other major carriers stopped writing new homeowner policies in California over the past two years. The ones still operating have non-renewed hundreds of thousands of policies. What's left is the FAIR Plan — California's insurer of last resort — which now covers \$458 billion in property exposure, up from \$347 billion just last year. That's a 32% increase in twelve months. The FAIR Plan wasn't designed to be the primary market. It was supposed to be a safety net for high-risk properties that couldn't get coverage anywhere else. Now it's becoming the default option for middle-class homeowners across the state.

Here's the direct Palm Springs connection: property insurance costs are about to spike hard across California, including here. Even if your house isn't in a high fire zone, you're in the same risk pool. Premiums will rise to cover the systemic losses. And if you're trying to buy a home, good luck getting a mortgage without proof of insurance. Lenders won't close without it. So you're either paying double or triple what you paid two years ago, or you're in the FAIR Plan with minimal coverage and massive out-of-pocket risk if something happens.

This hits the Coachella Valley workforce housing crisis from another angle. We already have a severe shortage of affordable housing for the people who actually work here — hospitality staff, retail workers, healthcare employees, teachers. Now add an insurance cost layer that makes homeownership even more unattainable. The gap widens.

## The \$20 Billion Housing Shortage No One's Talking About

California needs to build 2.5 million new homes just to meet current demand. That's the number from the state's own housing assessment. We're not building anywhere close to that pace. And the homes being built are almost entirely in the luxury and vacation rental segments, not workforce housing. Palm Springs and the surrounding cities have the same problem at local scale: tons of short-term rentals and second homes, very little inventory for year-round residents who work service jobs.

The math is brutal. If you're making \$20 an hour — which is roughly what many hospitality and retail jobs pay here — you're bringing home maybe \$2,800 a month after taxes. Standard budgeting says you shouldn't spend more than 30% of your income on housing. That's \$840 a month. Show me the \$840 apartment in Palm Springs. It doesn't exist. Even a studio runs \$1,400 to \$1,800 if you can find one. So workers are either cost-burdened, living with multiple roommates, or commuting from Desert Hot Springs, Indio, or further out.

This isn't sustainable if we want a functioning local economy. Restaurants, hotels, hospitals, schools — they all need staff. If those people can't afford to live here, businesses can't operate. It's already happening. You see "closed Tuesdays and Wednesdays" signs all over town because owners can't find enough workers to stay open seven days a week.

The state has tried to address this with streamlined approval processes for affordable housing projects, but local opposition and construction costs keep killing deals. And now insurance costs are another obstacle. Developers can't pencil out projects if insurance is going to eat another 15% of the budget.

## Trump's Economic Agenda and What It Means Here

Trump's executive orders today focused heavily on energy and deregulation. He wants to open up federal lands for oil and gas drilling, speed up pipeline approvals, and roll back environmental reviews that slow down projects. He also signed orders targeting what he called "weaponization" of government agencies and ending diversity programs in federal hiring.

For Palm Springs Coachella, the energy piece is complicated. We have significant renewable energy infrastructure here — solar farms, wind turbines, the whole Coachella Valley is part of California's clean energy buildout. If federal policy shifts toward fossil fuels and away from renewables subsidies, that could slow investment in new solar projects. On the other hand, if permitting gets faster and cheaper, maybe some projects that were stalled move forward.

The bigger question is what happens to federal climate funding. The Inflation Reduction Act under Biden put billions into clean energy tax credits and infrastructure. If Trump rolls that back or redirects it, California projects lose a major funding source. And since California has its own aggressive climate goals that don't align with Trump's agenda, we're likely headed for another four years of federal-state conflict on environmental policy.

Locally, that means uncertainty for developers, investors, and businesses trying to plan multi-year projects. You can't build a solar farm or a housing development if you don't know what the regulatory environment will look like in two years.

## The Workforce Housing Crisis Is a Business Crisis

The episode kept coming back to this: Palm Springs Coachella has a workforce problem because we have a housing problem. And it's not just about building more units. It's about building the *right kind* of units in the *right places* at the *right price points*.

We need dense, affordable housing near employment centers. We need local governments to actually approve these projects instead of slow-walking them to death. We need state and federal policy that makes it financially viable to build workforce housing instead of luxury condos.

And we need employers to recognize that if they want a stable workforce, they have a stake in solving this. Some larger companies are starting to do workforce housing partnerships — building or subsidizing apartments for their own employees. That's going to have to become more common here if we want restaurants, hotels, and healthcare facilities to stay staffed.

The alternative is a continued hollowing out of the year-round economy, where Palm Springs becomes even more of a part-time resort town with a transient, under-housed service class commuting in from an hour away. That's not a sustainable model. It's not even a desirable one.

## What Happens Next

Trump's second term starts today with a very different California than the one he left in 2021. The state is dealing with unprecedented wildfire damage, an insurance market in collapse, and a housing crisis that predates the fires but is now compounded by them. Meanwhile, the federal government is moving in the opposite direction on climate policy, energy policy, and regulatory philosophy.

For people building businesses or lives in Palm Springs Coachella, the next few years are going to require navigating that tension. State policy will keep pushing toward renewables, dense housing, and climate adaptation. Federal policy will push toward deregulation, fossil fuels, and rolling back environmental protections. And local governments will be caught in the middle, trying to manage growth, housing costs, and workforce shortages with limited tools and competing pressures.

The question isn't whether change is coming. It's already here. The question is whether we build systems that make life workable for the people who actually live and work in this valley, or whether we keep optimizing for tourists and second-homeowners while the local economy slowly breaks.
