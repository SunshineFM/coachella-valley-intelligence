## 2026-02-11: Signal Drop

**Alphabet Offers Exit Packages to Employees Who Won't Embrace AI**
Google's parent company sent an internal memo yesterday offering voluntary severance to employees in sales, solutions, and corporate development who "may not be ready to embrace the company's accelerated AI-focused direction." This isn't a layoff—it's an invitation to leave if you can't keep up with the pace. For tech workers burned out on the AI arms race, Palm Springs Coachella offers 350 days of sunshine and a chance to build something on your own terms. If you're one of them, reach out.

**UC Computer Science Enrollment Drops for First Time Since Dot-Com Bust**
Undergrad CS enrollment across the UC system declined this year—the first drop since the early 2000s—as students question whether traditional tech degrees lead to stable jobs anymore. Only UC San Diego bucked the trend, the sole campus offering an undergraduate AI major. Meanwhile, Cal State San Bernardino and UCR have had AI program proposals sitting on desks since last fall with no action. Our students deserve better than being two years behind the curve.

**AI Can Now Automate 11.7% of US Labor Market, MIT Tool Estimates**
MIT's Iceberg Index simulation estimates current AI systems can already handle tasks representing 20 million workers and \$1.2 trillion in wages—though researchers stress this reflects technical capability, not imminent job loss. A separate Stanford analysis found a 13% employment decline for workers ages 22-25 in AI-exposed fields since 2022. The gap between what AI *can* do and what companies *will* do is closing fast, and the Coachella Valley workforce isn't preparing.

**Commercial Real Estate Stocks Plunge 15-20% on AI Disruption Fears**
CBRE, Jones Lang LaSalle, and Cushman & Wakefield all tumbled double digits yesterday as investors rotated out of "high-fee, labor-intensive business models" vulnerable to AI disruption. Zillow's weak guidance compounded the sell-off, dropping 20% after missing EBITDA forecasts. For a region banking on real estate as an economic engine, this is a canary in the coal mine. The question: are local brokers and developers using AI to get ahead, or waiting for it to eat their lunch?

**AI Startup Dono Raises Funding to Modernize Fragmented Property Records**
The US real estate market is worth \$50 trillion, but it's tethered to 3,700 county registries running on Frankenstein tech and literal pen-and-paper systems. Dono's AI platform transforms those records into accessible ownership data, targeting an industry where 50% of the title insurance workforce is expected to retire in five years. This is infrastructure-level disruption—unglamorous, invisible, and absolutely critical for anyone in real estate to understand.

**AI Models Now Help Build Themselves, OpenAI Confirms**
OpenAI's latest release, GPT-5.3 Codex, is "the first model that was instrumental in creating itself," according to technical documentation published February 5th. The AI debugged its own training, managed deployment, and diagnosed test results autonomously. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei says AI now writes much of the code at his company and predicts we're one to two years from AI autonomously building the next generation. The intelligence explosion researchers warned about isn't theoretical anymore—it's already started.

**71% of Americans Fear AI Will Permanently Displace Workers, Poll Finds**
A Reuters-Ipsos poll found that 71% of Americans believe AI will put too many people out of work permanently, while a separate Resume Now survey showed 60% of workers think AI will eliminate more jobs than it creates this year. This isn't abstract anxiety—it's workers watching what happened to software engineers and realizing they're next. The Coachella Valley can either lead the conversation on workforce adaptation or get swept aside by it.

**Matt Schumer's Viral Post: "Something Big is Happening"**
Hyperwrite AI CEO Matt Schumer published a 54-million-view post on X yesterday laying out what tech insiders have been watching unfold: AI isn't just improving incrementally, it's crossing capability thresholds that eliminate entire categories of cognitive work. His message to family and friends outside tech: "The experience tech workers have had over the past year—watching AI go from helpful tool to does-my-job-better-than-I-do—is the experience everyone else is about to have." He's not predicting the future. He's describing what already happened to him and warning the rest of us we're next.

Local residents report minimal consultation on health, environmental, or infrastructure impacts. This is our backyard—if you care about how AI infrastructure gets built in the desert, now's the time to show up at county meetings and ask questions.
