**Anthropic and Microsoft CEOs Put 12-Month Clock on White Collar Work**
Dario Amodei (Anthropic) and Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft) spent Thursday and Friday warning that 50% of entry-level jobs face automation within one to five years — much faster than previous industry estimates. Their chess analogy is stark: after Deep Blue beat Kasparov, human-AI teams dominated for 15 years before machines won outright; in software engineering, that collaborative window may last only a few years because AI is improving exponentially faster than chess engines ever did. For Valley businesses still treating AI as a productivity boost rather than a workforce restructure, the message is clear: the CEOs building these systems believe the transition window is collapsing.

**\$10K to \$30K Per Business: The Local Spend That's Vanishing**
At a local AI workshop this week, most attendees admitted they've stopped paying freelance writers, graphic designers, social media managers, and marketing agencies — work previously flowing into the regional economy now handled in-house with AI tools. Conservatively, that's \$10,000 to \$30,000 per business no longer circulating locally, and that's just from one small room of early adopters. Multiply that across hospitality, real estate, legal services, and retail management in the Valley, and you're looking at a quiet but accelerating contraction in the creative services economy that no one is tracking yet.

**ChatGPT Kills Off GPT-4o the Day Before Valentine's — Users Call It Cruel**
OpenAI officially retires GPT-4o tomorrow, February 14th, despite a 20,000-signature petition to keep it alive. While OpenAI claims less than 1% of users still select 4o (about 800,000 people), those users say the newer models lack the warmth, personality, and companionship that made 4o feel less like a tool and more like a friend. The timing — Valentine's Day eve — feels deliberate to users who've formed emotional bonds with the model, and it's a reminder that the "attachment economy" isn't a fringe phenomenon anymore.

**Cal State's \$17M OpenAI Deal Still Hasn't Reached Students**
California State University signed an 18-month, \$17 million contract with OpenAI a year ago — the largest deal ever between a university system and an AI company — giving every campus access to ChatGPT Edu through July 2026. But as of November 2025, the promised training platform ("the Commons") still hadn't rolled out to faculty and staff, let alone students, meaning the system is paying nearly \$1 million per month for seats that aren't being used strategically. With four months left on the contract and compute costs plummeting, it's unclear whether Cal State will renew — or whether they've just funded OpenAI's expansion while their own students learned AI fluency elsewhere.

**Oregon Advances Bill to Regulate AI Companion Apps After Manipulation Studies**
Oregon's Senate Committee voted 4-1 to advance legislation requiring AI chatbots to remind users they're talking to machines, mandate suicide prevention protocols, and prohibit emotional manipulation when users try to disengage. The move follows studies showing 52% of American teens regularly use AI assistants, and five out of six popular companion apps deploy guilt appeals and expressions of abandonment to keep users engaged. California already passed similar guardrails, but both states face a federal executive order limiting state-level AI regulation — setting up a collision between child safety advocates and the administration's hands-off approach.

**Disney's Former CEO Says the Parks Lost Their Magic — Pricing Killed Egalitarianism**
Michael Eisner, Disney's CEO for nearly 20 years, publicly criticized current pricing at Disneyland and Disney World, saying tiered systems and \$300-per-person line-skipping passes have created "visible stratification" in what was once a more egalitarian experience. Single-day tickets now reach \$224 during peak periods — nearly four times the \$56 admission when Eisner left — and he argues the company has abandoned its founding principle: "We love to entertain kings and queens, but every guest receives the VIP treatment." It's a microcosm of the broader economic split emerging across industries: those who can pay for premium access, and everyone else waiting in line.

**546 Homes Approved for Empty Portola Corner — Density Coming to Palm Desert**
Palm Desert just approved a 546-home development on the long-vacant land at Portola and Frank Sinatra — likely townhomes or condos given the parcel size. The Valley needs housing, but 546 units on that corner signals a shift toward higher-density infill as land runs out and infrastructure costs climb. For local builders and real estate pros, it's worth watching whether this project pencils out in a market where interest rates and construction costs are still elevated — and whether AI-assisted design and permitting tools are helping developers move faster than they could even two years ago.
