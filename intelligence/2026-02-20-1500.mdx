**Federal Reserve Governor Uses "Essentially Unemployable" to Describe AI-Displaced Workers**
On February 17th, Federal Reserve Governor Michael S. Barr told the New York Association for Business Economics that one of three AI scenarios includes workers becoming "essentially unemployable" as agentic AI systems replace professional and service jobs. This isn't activist rhetoric — this is the Fed, the institution responsible for maximum employment, modeling outcomes most policymakers won't say out loud. For the Coachella Valley, the highest-risk industries are transportation and logistics along the I-10, hospitality back office operations, and retail, while healthcare, hands-on hospitality services, and skilled trades remain most durable.

**Hollywood Professionals Gathered in Rancho Mirage This Week — and the Mood Was Grim**
The Hollywood Professionals Association's tech retreat at the Westin in Rancho Mirage drew 800 attendees this week, from studio VPs to AI startup founders, and the sentiment was darker than expected. Robert Tercek, former Sony Pictures SVP of digital media, arrived expecting excitement about AI but found near-universal opposition — guilds dead set against it, studios befuddled by copyright, and no one using AI for mission-critical filmmaking despite it being "the one technology innovation that can save Hollywood from the current malaise." With 40% of LA jobs already vanished and original scripted series production down 20% from its 600-show peak, the industry is in secular decline, and guild negotiations starting this month are headed for disaster.

**Google Lyria 3 Lets Anyone Generate 30-Second Music Tracks From a Photo**
Google dropped Lyria 3 this week, an AI music tool that generates 30-second tracks from a photo or text prompt with full control over style, vocals, and tempo. The tech works, the output is high quality, and it's effortless — which means millions of algorithmically generated clips are about to flood feeds, DMs, and inboxes with no curation layer. For anyone building media or brand presence in the Valley, the strategic question is shifting from "can we use AI tools" to "what makes us irreplaceable when everybody else has the same tools."

**Hollywood's Guild Negotiations Have Three Outcomes — None of Them Good**
Tercek predicts three scenarios for the guild negotiations happening in the next few weeks: guilds prevail and studios collapse under unaffordable costs, studios resist and everyone goes on strike again, or both sides compromise and accept AI as a normal part of workflow with smaller writer rooms supplemented by ChatGPT, fewer human actors, and 50% lower production costs. He thinks the third scenario is the only path to survival, but nobody in Los Angeles is ready to accept it. The pattern is familiar — Hollywood resisted streaming 20 years ago until it was too late, and now tech companies own entertainment.

**Computer Science Enrollment Drops for First Time in 20 Years**
For the first time in two decades, fewer students are enrolling in computer science degrees than the year before. This wasn't elaborated on in detail, but it signals that young workers are already reading the labor market signals — software development is a high-exposure field where early-career workers are experiencing adverse effects from AI adoption right now, not in some distant future. The Fed's "essentially unemployable" language isn't hypothetical for this cohort.

**SunshineFM's Curation Thesis: Geotag Signal vs. Generative Noise**
SunshineFM sits between national syndicated radio playlists and the coming flood of AI-generated 30-second music clips — it's a 24/7 AI radio station geotagged to Palm Springs and Coachella Valley, with curated AI-generated artists on SunshineFM Records. The station generates dozens of tracks daily but releases only 2 to 3, maintaining a human curation layer to cut through AI slop. The question for anyone building media right now is whether you're adding to the noise or cutting through it — and curation is the answer.

**Is Now the Time for Hollywood East in Coachella Valley?**
With Hollywood in decline, guild negotiations collapsing, and AI production centers likely moving to cities that embrace AI-native filmmaking, the Coachella Valley has a 12-to-24-month window to build infrastructure for displaced Hollywood talent. The Valley has the DNA — two hours from LA, film-friendly landscapes, lower cost of living, existing creative community, and entertainment industry history from the '50s and '60s. What's missing is technical talent, production facilities, funding ecosystem, and training programs — all solvable if leadership decides to act like a startup instead of an incumbent protecting old industries.

## The Signal

The institutions that manage employment and the industries that create entertainment are both modeling catastrophic labor displacement scenarios — and neither has a plan beyond "be nimble and bold." For the Coachella Valley, the question isn't whether AI disruption is coming, but whether we're building infrastructure for the refugees before other regions do. The 12-to-24-month window to establish AI-native production studios, distributed AI ownership models, and training programs is open right now, but only if leadership decides to act like a startup instead of an incumbent.
