**Fed Governor Barr Puts "Essentially Unemployable" on the Record**

On February 17, Federal Reserve Governor Michael S. Barr addressed the New York Association for Business Economics and laid out three scenarios for AI's labor market impact — one of which includes the phrase "essentially unemployable" to describe workers displaced by agentic AI systems. The Fed doesn't float dystopian language without reason: this is scenario planning from the institution responsible for maximum employment, and it signals the Fed is actively modeling outcomes most policymakers won't say out loud.

**The Jobless Boom: Thriving Economy, Collapsed Labor Demand**

Barr's first scenario is the one worth sitting with: agentic AI systems — capable of achieving goals with minimal human supervision — replace massive swaths of professional and service jobs, autonomous vehicles eliminate transportation work, and robotics empty manufacturing floors. The result is a "vastly productive economy" where winners are capital holders and "AI superstars," and everyone else is essentially unemployable without profound changes to education, training, and the social safety net. The gains from growth need to be redistributed, Barr says — but the mechanism for doing that doesn't exist yet.

**The AI Bust: Best Outcome for Workers, Worst for Investors**

Barr's second scenario is AI hitting a ceiling — training data runs out, electricity costs make scaling unviable, and the \$1 trillion in projected AI investment over the next five years doesn't produce the productivity gains businesses expect. AI becomes "ubiquitous, even indispensable, but not necessarily revolutionary" — like email or social media. The financial sector absorbs the damage from overinvestment, comparable to fiber optic cable overbuilding in the 2000s. Perversely, this is the scenario where most workers keep their jobs.

**The Balanced Path: Disruption, But Navigable**

Barr's preferred scenario follows the pattern of previous technological revolutions: gradual adoption, skill mismatch unemployment that clears over time as training adjusts, and strong productivity growth that raises real wages broadly. He points to existing research suggesting AI adoption is already leading to reallocation within firms rather than mass unemployment. He calls for society to be "nimble and bold, investing now in worker training and new job creation" — but offers no specific actor, agency, or timeline for who does that work.

**Young Tech Workers Already Feeling It**

Barr specifically flags early-career workers in high-exposure fields like software development as already experiencing adverse effects from AI adoption. This isn't a future risk — it's present tense. Tech sector employment contractions and media industry layoffs are the visible edge of what Barr is modeling at the macroeconomic level. The reallocation story is harder to see than the elimination story.

**The Distributed AI Ownership Question Barr Doesn't Ask**

All three of Barr's scenarios assume AI is centralized — deployed by large tech companies and used by everyone else. None of his scenarios considers what happens in regions where businesses own their AI infrastructure locally rather than renting it from Microsoft, Google, or OpenAI. That distinction matters for where money flows, who controls what gets automated, and whether the productivity gains stay in the region or get extracted to Seattle and San Francisco.

**Coachella Valley Exposure Map**

Barr's jobless boom scenario lands differently across regional economies. For the Coachella Valley, the highest-risk industries are transportation and logistics along the I-10 corridor, hospitality back-office operations, and retail. The most durable jobs are in healthcare, hands-on hospitality services where wealthy visitors pay a premium for human interaction, and skilled trades — electricians, plumbers, and HVAC workers in a region where air conditioning is infrastructure. The bust scenario — AI becoming widely adopted but not transformative — may be the best realistic outcome for a regional economy built on tourism, healthcare, and events.

## Local Radar

- **College of the Desert and AICV workforce programs**: The AI Coachella Valley initiative is running workshops through Cal State San Bernardino and UC Riverside Extension — the most relevant local response to Barr's "balanced path" scenario currently operating in the Valley.
- **Regional workforce coordination**: Whether Palm Springs, Rancho Mirage, Palm Desert, and Indio move toward a joint workforce transition strategy is the key question Barr's speech makes newly urgent — worth watching what, if anything, comes out of local leadership conversations this quarter.
- **I-10 corridor logistics exposure**: With autonomous vehicle technology advancing, the transportation and trucking jobs along the I-10 corridor represent the Coachella Valley's highest near-term labor displacement risk under Barr's jobless boom scenario.

## The Signal

The Federal Reserve is now openly scenario-planning for mass structural unemployment — not as a fringe risk but as one of three plausible futures a sitting governor put on the record. For Coachella Valley operators and decision-makers, the uncomfortable read is that Barr's "balanced path" requires exactly the kind of nimble regional coordination and rapid reskilling infrastructure that doesn't yet exist here. The regions that fare best across all three scenarios are the ones that move before the unemployment numbers spike — not after. The question isn't which scenario is coming. It's whether we're building for any of them.
