Federal Reserve Governor's Three AI Futures: Boom, Bust, or Balance?
WHO
Michael S. Barr, Federal Reserve Governor, speaking to the New York Association for Business Economics on February 17, 2026.
WHAT
Barr laid out three possible scenarios for how AI reshapes the labor market and economy:
Scenario 1: The Jobless Boom AI works. Really works. Agentic AI systems—capable of achieving goals with minimal human supervision—replace massive swaths of professional and service jobs. Autonomous vehicles eliminate transportation jobs. Unsupervised robotics empty manufacturing floors. You get a "vastly productive economy" with far less demand for human labor.
The result: widespread unemployment in the short term, massive labor market decline over time. Winners are capital holders and "AI superstars." Everyone else is, in Barr's words, "essentially unemployable" unless there are "profound changes in education, training and workforce development."
His prescription: Society has to rethink the social safety net entirely. The gains from economic growth need to be shared, not concentrated. Workers who survive specialize in what AI can't replicate—manual trades, highly-skilled work, or industries where consumers pay a premium for human interaction.
Scenario 2: The AI Bust AI hits a ceiling. Training data runs out. Electricity to power data centers becomes scarce or too expensive. The $1 trillion in new debt required over the next five years doesn't produce immediate productivity gains, so businesses lose interest.
In this version, AI becomes like social media or email—"ubiquitous, even indispensable, but not necessarily revolutionary by themselves." Widely adopted, not transformative.
The casualty: the financial sector. When anticipated demand falls short, you get financial stress. Barr compares it to the fiber optic cable overbuilding in the 2000s—massive investment, underwhelming returns, financial fallout.
Scenario 3: The Balanced Path AI follows the pattern of previous technological revolutions—gradual adoption, manageable pace. Some initial unemployment due to skill mismatch, but workers adapt. Training and education adjust over time. "Many workers successfully retrain and retain their jobs or find new ones."
Strong productivity growth allows faster output growth. Real wages rise. Research already suggests AI adoption is leading to reallocation within firms, not mass unemployment.
Barr calls this the dot-com boom trajectory—disruption, yes, but navigable disruption.
WHERE
This speech matters everywhere, but the implications play out differently depending on regional economic structure. Manufacturing-heavy regions face different risks than service-economy regions. Tech hubs face different risks than retirement communities.
WHEN
February 17, 2026. Right now. The Federal Reserve doesn't make speeches like this for fun—they're signaling what they're preparing for.
WHY
The Fed's job is financial stability and maximum employment. When a Fed governor lays out three scenarios and one of them includes the phrase "essentially unemployable," that's not academic speculation. That's scenario planning.
Barr's bottom line: "Regardless of which scenario unfolds, the long-term effects of AI are likely to be profoundly positive for living standards." But he also warns we're already seeing adverse effects on young, early-career workers in high-exposure fields like software development.
His call to action: "Society must be nimble and bold, investing now in worker training and new job creation to ensure the benefits of AI are broadly shared."
SAT'S TAKE
Here's what strikes me about this speech: A Federal Reserve governor just publicly entertained the possibility of a "jobless boom"—an economy that's thriving while most people can't find work.
That's not a throwaway line. The Fed doesn't float dystopian scenarios unless they think there's a non-zero chance they happen.
Three observations:
First: Barr's "balanced path" is already losing.
He says research suggests AI is leading to "reallocation within firms" rather than unemployment. But look at the actual data: tech sector employment in San Francisco down 2.4% year-over-year. Washington Post cutting 300 jobs. Atlanta Journal-Constitution 15%. Pittsburgh Post-Gazette shutting down entirely.
That's not reallocation. That's elimination.
Barr's balanced scenario assumes workers can retrain and adapt "over time." But how much time? And who pays for the retraining while people are unemployed? The social safety net he mentions rethinking in the "jobless boom" scenario? We don't have it. Not even close.
Second: The "bust" scenario might be the best outcome for workers.
If AI becomes "ubiquitous but not revolutionary"—like email or smartphones—that means it enhances productivity without destroying jobs wholesale. You still need humans. The tools just make them more efficient.
That's the version where most people keep working. The financial sector takes the hit (overinvestment, stranded assets), but labor survives.
Perversely, the better AI works, the worse it is for workers. The worse AI works, the better it is for workers. That's a hell of a tension.
Third: "Nimble and bold" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.
Barr says society must be "nimble and bold, investing now in worker training and new job creation."
Okay. Who's doing that? Which government agency is being nimble? Which industry is being bold?
This is the gap: Everyone sees the problem. No one's moving fast enough on solutions. By the time we build the social safety net for a jobless boom, the boom will already be over.
WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE COACHELLA VALLEY
Let's map Barr's three scenarios onto our regional economy and see what shakes out.
Scenario 1: Jobless Boom in the Desert
Industries at highest risk here:
* Transportation/Logistics — We're a drive-through region. I-10 corridor, trucking, delivery. Autonomous vehicles eliminate those jobs.
* Hospitality back-office — Hotels, resorts, event venues. AI handles reservations, customer service, scheduling. Human jobs compress to high-touch guest services only.
* Retail — Already dying. AI accelerates it. El Paseo becomes a ghost town except for experiential retail (tastings, events, personal service).
* Construction/Manual trades — Survives longer, but robotics coming for this too.
Who survives:
* Healthcare workers — Eisenhower, Desert Regional, clinics. AI assists, but you still need nurses, doctors, hands-on care.
* Hospitality front-line — Concierges, event planners, sommeliers, chefs. The human touch that wealthy visitors pay for.
* Skilled trades — Electricians, plumbers, HVAC. Hard to automate, always in demand in a place where AC is life.
* Creative/entertainment — Coachella, Stagecoach, local arts scene. Live performance can't be automated.
The social safety net question: Riverside County doesn't have the tax base to support a jobless boom. If 30-40% of workers become "essentially unemployable," we don't have the infrastructure to handle that. This scenario breaks us.
Scenario 2: AI Bust in the Desert
Honestly? This might be our best outcome.
If AI becomes "ubiquitous but not revolutionary," the Coachella Valley continues operating mostly as it does now—tourism, hospitality, healthcare, events, some tech remote workers, retirees. AI tools make businesses more efficient, but they don't eliminate industries.
Hotels use AI for booking, but still need staff. Restaurants use AI for inventory, but still need cooks. Real estate agents use AI for listings, but still need local knowledge and trust.
The financial sector takes the hit (not us), and we keep humming along.
Scenario 3: Balanced Path in the Desert
This requires us to actually prepare.
Barr's "balanced path" assumes worker retraining and education adjust over time. Okay. What does that look like here?
What we have:
* College of the Desert offering some AI/tech programs
* AICV (AI Coachella Valley) running workshops through Cal State San Bernardino, UC Riverside Extension, chambers of commerce
* Some private training initiatives
What we need:
* Rapid reskilling programs — 6-month bootcamps, not 2-year degrees, focused on AI-adjacent skills (prompt engineering, AI system management, data curation)
* Partnerships with employers — Hotels, hospitals, real estate firms committing to hire retrained workers
* Income support during transition — You can't retrain if you can't pay rent. Need bridge funding.
* Regional coordination — Palm Springs, Rancho Mirage, Palm Desert, Indio working together on workforce strategy, not competing
The opportunity: If we move fast, the Coachella Valley could become a model for "balanced path" AI adoption—not fighting it, not letting it destroy us, but managing the transition intelligently.
But we have to move now. Not in two years when the unemployment numbers spike.
THE BIGGER PATTERN
Notice what Barr's speech doesn't mention: local AI infrastructure.
All three scenarios assume AI is centralized—big tech companies deploying systems that everyone else uses. Workers either adapt to those systems or become unemployable.
But there's a fourth scenario he doesn't consider: distributed AI ownership.
What if Coachella Valley businesses ran their own AI systems locally? What if hotels, restaurants, hospitals, schools owned their tools instead of renting them from Microsoft, Google, or OpenAI?
That changes the equation:
* Money stays local (not extracted to Seattle/SF)
* Control stays local (you decide what gets automated, what stays human)
* Jobs stay local (someone has to manage these systems)
* Data stays local (competitive advantage, privacy protection)
This is the OpenClaw argument again, but zoomed out to regional economic strategy.
If AI is going to reshape the labor market—boom, bust, or balanced—the regions that own their AI infrastructure will fare better than regions that rent it.
That's the conversation Coachella Valley leaders should be having right now.
BOTTOM LINE
A Federal Reserve governor just told us three possible futures. One involves mass unemployment. One involves AI fizzling out. One involves managed transition.
He's betting on the third. I'm not sure he's right.
What I am sure of: The Coachella Valley isn't ready for any of these scenarios.
We need:
1. Honest assessment of which local industries are at risk
2. Rapid reskilling infrastructure (not slow, not bureaucratic)
3. Regional coordination on workforce strategy
4. Local AI infrastructure so we're not just passive recipients of whatever Big Tech deploys
This isn't hypothetical. This is happening. The question is whether we're preparing, or just hoping it works out.
I know which one I'm betting on.
